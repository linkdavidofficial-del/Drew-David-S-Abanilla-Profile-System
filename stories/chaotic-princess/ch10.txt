Chapter 10: THE MAGE WITH A SNAKE FOR A SCARF

The training yard was empty when Elliana arrived the next morning—properly empty, not the usual barracks bustle of junior knights and instructors and Caius annoying whoever happened to be nearby. Just packed dirt, a rack of practice weapons, and the pale gray light of early dawn still clinging to the walls.

"You're early," Grayer said, without turning around. He stood at the center of the yard, arms loose at his sides, sword already unsheathed and resting point-down in the dirt.

"You're earlier," Elliana said, rolling her shoulders as she approached. "How long have you been standing here?"

"Long enough to know you'd show up early too." He turned, finally, sharp eyes crinkling with something that might have been approval. "Figured you'd want privacy for your first real lesson. Less chance of an audience if you embarrass yourself."

"I don't embarrass. I only elevate the room's overall entertainment value."

"We'll see." He hefted his own blade—a plain, unadorned longsword that looked, at a glance, like nothing special at all, and yet moved through the air with a low, humming weight that made the hair on Elliana's arms stand up the moment he settled into his stance. "Come at me. Whatever you've got. Don't hold back on my account—I've had worse thrown at me by things with considerably more teeth."

Elliana didn't need to be told twice.

She came in fast and low, wooden practice sword whistling through the air in a strike aimed at his ribs—not textbook, not clean, nothing Lysander would have approved of, but fast, and unpredictable, and backed by ten years of chasing crocodiles and dodging boar tusks.

Grayer's blade met hers with almost lazy ease, deflecting the strike a half-inch wide, and he was already stepping into the space she'd left open, close enough that she had to abandon the follow-through and twist away instead.

"Feral," he said, sounding pleased rather than critical. "Wild. No wasted motion, but no structure either. You fight like something that's spent its whole life running from bigger things and figuring out how to bite back."

"That's oddly specific and also extremely accurate," Elliana said, circling, already looking for another opening.

She found one—or thought she did—darting in toward his exposed left side, blade angled for a strike that should have connected clean.

It didn't.

Grayer's foot caught her ankle mid-step, simple and unglamorous, and the world tilted sideways as she hit the dirt hard enough to knock the air clean out of her lungs.

"OOF—"

"Advice," Grayer said, standing over her with the sword resting easy against his shoulder, not even breathing hard. "Wild is good. Wild keeps you alive against things that expect textbook form. But wild without any structure at all just means you telegraph your openings to anyone paying close enough attention. I've been paying close attention." He extended a hand down to help her up. "You lead with your hips before every strike. Little thing. Most people wouldn't catch it. I've fought enough wild things to know what to look for."

Elliana coughed, grabbed his hand, and hauled herself back up, grinning despite the dull ache blooming across her tailbone. "Honestly? Fair. I figured I'd lose. I'm strong, sure, and fast, but I've got maybe four months of actual formal training under my belt and you've got what, forty years of professional monster-murdering?"

"Closer to fifty, but who's counting." Grayer sheathed his sword, studying her with open curiosity now instead of assessment. "You take losing well. Most students your age—royal or otherwise—throw a tantrum the first time someone puts them on the ground. You just got back up and started analyzing the fight."

"Tantrums are inefficient. Analysis gets you better faster." She rubbed the back of her neck, working out a knot. "Also I've been thrown off a crocodile more times than I can count. This barely registers."

Grayer laughed—that same low, rumbling sound from the day before—and nodded slowly to himself, as if confirming something he'd already suspected.

"Starting today," he said, "two hours, every morning. Sword forms, footwork, conditioning, and eventually, once your foundation's solid, we'll talk about how to actually start building toward sword aura. That's years off yet, mind you—rank five doesn't come cheap, and I won't have you rushing it and hurting yourself. But you've got the raw material. Strength, speed, and a body that doesn't quit even when it should."

"Two hours a day," Elliana repeated. "That's it? I figured you'd want more, given how far behind I apparently am."

"Two hours a day is plenty," Grayer said, "because you're smarter than you let on, Boar Slayer, and I don't just mean the arithmetic your tutors keep whispering about behind your back."

Elliana's eyebrow ticked upward.

"You listen," Grayer continued, settling back into an easy, storytelling cadence. "Yesterday, while your parents were fussing over introductions, I was explaining sword ranks. You made it a point to look bored. Kept glancing at the ceiling, picking at your sleeve, the whole performance. But when I got to the part about how sword aura forms from the body and blade synchronizing under repeated mana-conditioned strain, you stopped picking at your sleeve. Just for a second. Long enough to tell me you were actually listening the whole time, and the boredom was for show."

Elliana said nothing, which was, in its own way, confirmation enough.

"I've trained a lot of students in my time," Grayer said. "Nobles who wanted the title without the work. Farm boys with more raw talent than sense. A few genuine prodigies who burned out because nobody taught them patience. You're something else entirely. You act like you don't care, right up until the exact moment something actually matters, and then you're paying more attention than anyone else in the room." He shrugged, hefting his sword back over one shoulder. "Two hours a day is enough, because you'll spend the other twenty-two thinking about what we covered whether you mean to or not. That's rarer than raw talent, in my experience. Talent gets you far. Genuine curiosity is what turns talent into mastery."

"That was almost a nice thing to say," Elliana said, grinning. "Careful, old man, keep that up and I might start respecting you."

"Gods forbid." Grayer was already turning to leave, waving one hand lazily over his shoulder. "Same time tomorrow, Boar Slayer. Stretch tonight. You're going to be sore in places you didn't know could be sore."

He was right. She was.

* * *

It was barely past midday when a servant found her—freshly changed out of her training clothes and into something marginally more presentable, hair still damp from a hasty wash—with the now-familiar summons to the throne room.

"Again?" Elliana asked, following the servant down the corridor. "Two royal summons in as many days. Are you people running out of things to do, or is this an actual pattern now?"

"I couldn't say, Your Highness," the servant said, in the diplomatically neutral tone of someone who had learned, over years of palace service, never to speculate about royal business out loud.

The throne room, when she arrived, held a woman Elliana had never seen before—and, she noted immediately, a woman who was very difficult *not* to notice.

She was tall, striking, with long, dark reddish-brown hair that fell in loose waves past her waist, and she wore flowing purple robes embroidered with silver constellations that seemed to shift, faintly, when the light caught them just right. A tall, pointed mage's hat sat at a jaunty angle atop her head. At her hip hung a scroll case so massive it was nearly as long as a horse, strapped diagonally across her back, and in one hand she held a gnarled wooden staff topped with a smooth, faintly glowing crystal.

And wrapped around her neck and shoulders, coiled loosely like the world's most unsettling scarf, was a snake nearly six feet long, its scales a deep, glossy black, faint metallic plating visible along its spine where scale gave way to something closer to armor.

Elliana blinked.

"That's a big snake," she said, by way of greeting.

The woman turned, and her face split into a wide, delighted grin. "And you must be the famous Boar Slayer Princess! Oh, you're just as feisty as the rumors said." She swept forward in a rustle of purple robes, arms already opening for what was clearly about to be an extremely enthusiastic hug. "Come here, come here, let me look at—"

Elliana, with the ease of a decade spent dodging things considerably more dangerous than an overeager stranger, wiggled neatly out from under the incoming embrace, ending up a few feet to the side with her arms crossed and an unimpressed pout on her face.

"I don't hug strangers," she said, and then, because she couldn't quite help herself, added, "Also, I'm the best. Just so we're starting from an accurate baseline."

The woman blinked, startled for exactly a second, and then burst into delighted laughter, the Armored Anaconda around her shoulders shifting lazily in response to the movement without ever quite waking up.

"Oh, I LIKE her," the woman said, to the room at large, while Queen Marianne, standing nearby, simply lowered her face into one hand with the weary resignation of a woman who had stopped being surprised by her daughter's antics sometime around the crocodile incident.

"Elliana," Marianne said, from behind her palm, "this is Sera. She'll be your instructor in magic."

"I already have magic tutors," Elliana said, tilting her head.

"Basic theory tutors, yes," the King said, stepping in from where he'd been quietly observing. "But we needed someone who understood beast taming specifically—someone with real, practical experience, not just theory. Sera used to serve as a court magician here, years ago, before she left to open her own shop in the capital. She trained under druids before that. Properly trained, mind you—she can perform official taming rites, not just the general theory of it."

"An old friend," Marianne added, dropping her hand at last, though her expression remained somewhere between fond and faintly exhausted. "We reached out the moment we realized what we actually needed. She was kind enough to make time."

"More than kind enough," Sera said, waving a hand with theatrical modesty. "The moment I heard the words 'Boar Slayer Princess' and 'needs a beast-taming mage,' I dropped everything. Do you have any idea how rare it is to get to teach someone who's already tamed something without knowing the first thing about the actual spellwork? That's like watching a child accidentally reinvent alchemy in their bathtub. Fascinating."

"I tamed Sparky by vibes," Elliana said, with total sincerity. "Vibes and being really, really annoying at him until he gave up."

"That," Sera said, delighted, "is exactly what I mean." She crouched down slightly, bringing herself more to Elliana's eye level, the Armored Anaconda's head lifting lazily off her shoulder to regard the princess with slow, ancient-looking eyes. "Let's start simple. Tell me—what do you already know about mana? About how it moves through the body?"

Elliana considered this for a moment, then launched into an answer with the easy, unbothered confidence of someone who had, admittedly, absorbed far more from Rosaria's dinner-table magic theory lectures than she usually let on.

"Mana circulates through channels in the body, kind of like blood, except instead of a heart it's driven by something called a core—everyone's born with one, but the size and density varies wildly. Spells work by shaping mana through intent and a specific incantation structure, and the more precisely you can shape it, the less raw mana you waste doing it. Rank one and two spells are basically mana with training wheels—minor stuff, cantrips, nothing that requires real structural discipline. Rank three is where you start needing actual formal technique instead of just brute-forcing it."

Sera's eyebrows had climbed steadily higher throughout this entire recitation, and by the time Elliana finished, she was staring with an expression caught somewhere between delight and genuine astonishment.

"That," Sera said slowly, "is a distressingly good summary for someone whose official magic education consists of, and I quote your mother's letter, 'basic tutoring, mostly ignored in favor of hunting boars.'"

"I have a very absorbent brain," Elliana said. "It just prefers to absorb things quietly, while looking bored, so nobody gets any funny ideas about assigning me MORE lessons."

"Wonderful. Excellent. I foresee absolutely no issues teaching you." Sera clapped her hands together, delighted, the anaconda around her shoulders shifting with a low, dry rustle of scales. "We'll start with taming theory properly, then—since apparently you've already done the hard part by accident. A taming spell succeeds when a unique mark manifests on the beast in question. Where it appears, and what it looks like, depends on the bond itself—species, temperament, the nature of the connection between tamer and beast. It's not something you can fake or force. Either the mark appears, or the taming fails and you've just made a very large, very annoyed animal even angrier at you."

"Sparky doesn't have a mark," Elliana said, frowning slightly. "Or, I mean, I never checked. Does he need one?"

"Oh, he has one, dear." Sera's grin widened. "I checked the moment I walked in and saw you two together in the courtyard on my way in. Right on his forehead, mostly hidden under the scale ridges—very faint, easy to miss unless you know what you're looking for. Congratulations, you've already performed an official taming bond. On a wild Electro Crocodile. As an infant. Without knowing a single thing about the spellwork involved."

She laughed—a genuine, delighted sound, clapping her hands together again. "I have never, in my entire career, met someone who backed into competent spellwork purely through sheer force of personality. This is going to be an absolute joy to teach."

"So no lessons needed, then? Great, I'll be going—"

"Oh, absolutely not, sit back down, we have SO much to cover." Sera's staff tapped once against the marble floor, the crystal atop it flickering with faint, amused light. "Accidentally succeeding once doesn't mean you understand why it worked, and understanding why is what lets you do it reliably, on purpose, with creatures considerably more dangerous than a river crocodile. Now—mana rings. Tell me what you know."

Elliana opened her mouth, considered simply reciting Rosaria's exact explanation from memory, and decided that was, on reflection, the most efficient path forward.

"A mana ring is a physical structure that forms around the heart, sort of like a second ribcage made of condensed mana instead of bone," she said. "The denser the ring, the more raw power a mage can channel without losing control of the shape. You can stack multiple rings—more rings means more total capacity, though each additional ring gets exponentially harder to form. My sister Rosaria's a third circle mage already, which is apparently absurd for someone her age, and that's why she can cast rank four and five spells without her control falling apart halfway through."

Sera stared at her for a long moment, then turned to Queen Marianne with an expression of pure, undisguised delight.

"I would like to formally request," she said, "that you never, under any circumstances, let this child believe she isn't paying attention in lessons. She is clearly paying attention to everything, all the time, and simply choosing when to admit it for maximum comedic effect."

"Welcome to my life," Marianne said dryly.

Sera turned back to Elliana, and her sharp eyes caught, for the first time, on something small and gold glinting faintly against the back of the princess's hand—a tiny mark, shaped unmistakably like a grinning jester's mask, cap and bells and all.

"Now that," Sera said, voice dropping into something quieter, more genuinely curious, "is not a taming mark. And it's not any blessing sigil I've ever seen catalogued, and believe me, dear, I've seen quite a few in my time." She reached out, not quite touching, studying the small mark with open fascination. "What is that?"

Elliana's grin turned sharp and immediately, thoroughly unhelpful. "Wouldn't you like to know."

"I would, actually. Very much."

"Too bad."

Sera studied her for a long moment—not offended, exactly, just deeply, visibly curious in the way of a scholar denied a fascinating footnote—before letting out a rueful little laugh and straightening back up.

"Fair enough," she said. "A woman's entitled to her secrets. Gods know I have a few of my own." She glanced down at the anaconda still draped comfortably across her shoulders. "I won't pry. Much. But if you ever feel like sharing, I promise you'll have my full and undivided professional curiosity."

"Noted," Elliana said, already relaxing now that the topic had, mercifully, been dropped. "So. Lessons. Where, when, all that boring logistics stuff."

"The far tower," Sera said, already turning, robes swirling with the motion. "Used to be my old workspace, actually, back when I served the court properly. Nobody's touched it in years—it'll want a good cleaning, but the wards are still intact, which means it's the one place in this entire palace where you can set something on fire without the whole building panicking about it."

Elliana's grin widened into something considerably more dangerous.

"I'm going to like you," she said.

"I already know," Sera said, sweeping out of the throne room in a rustle of purple robes and shifting scales, "that the feeling's mutual."
