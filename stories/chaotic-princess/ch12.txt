Chapter 12: SCIENCE, NOT STUPIDITY

The Forbidden Forest looked, up close, exactly as unwelcoming as its name suggested—thick, twisted trees crowding close overhead, choking out most of the moonlight, the underbrush tangled enough that even Sparky had to slow his usual thundering pace to something more careful, more deliberate.

Zeus and Hera peeled off from her shoulders the moment they crossed the tree line, rising up above the canopy in slow, wide circles, scanning the terrain below with the sharp, patient focus of hunting birds who took their work considerably more seriously than their handler generally took anything.

"Find me a cave," Elliana called up after them, more out of habit than any real expectation they'd understood the specific request. "Or a camp. Or anything that looks like thirty bandits decided to make a home out of it."

She followed their circling flight path deeper into the woods, Sparky picking his way carefully over roots and loose stone, until, nearly twenty minutes later, Hera let out a sharp, descending cry and dove toward a rocky outcropping half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging vines.

Elliana slid down off Sparky's back, crept the last stretch on foot, and peered around the edge of a large boulder—head poking out at an angle that would have looked, to any outside observer, almost comically cautious, like a cartoon character checking around a corner before tiptoeing past.

There it was. A cave mouth, dark and unassuming from a distance, except for the path leading up to it—worn smooth and bare of grass in a way that spoke clearly of regular, heavy foot traffic. Definitely occupied. Definitely the right place.

"Stay," she whispered to Sparky and the Thunderbirds, holding up a flat palm in the universal gesture for *do not follow me into the obviously dangerous cave, please.* Sparky rumbled low in what might have been protest, might have been agreement, and settled back into the shadow of the boulder regardless, patient as ever.

Elliana drew her sword—still just a well-made steel blade, nothing enchanted, nothing special, but three months under Grayer's relentless instruction had turned it into something considerably more dangerous in her hands than it would have been a year ago—and crept forward into the cave.

The tunnel opened up gradually, torches bracketed along the rough stone walls at irregular intervals, casting long, flickering shadows that made the passage feel considerably deeper than it probably was. She moved quietly, senses alert, following the sound of distant voices, laughter, the clatter of cookware, until the tunnel finally opened into a wide central chamber.

And there they were.

The entire gang, or near enough to it—men lounging around a scattering of cookfires, weapons stacked carelessly nearby, sacks and crates of what was almost certainly stolen goods piled up against the far wall. She counted quickly, eyes scanning the chamber. More than she'd expected, even accounting for the bounty poster's estimate. Thirty, maybe more.

Elliana grinned, sharp and delighted, in the darkness of the tunnel mouth.

Jackpot.

She backed out slowly, carefully, retracing her steps until she was back out in the cool night air, where Sparky waited exactly where she'd left him, one large eye tracking her return with patient curiosity.

"Big group," she murmured, crouching down beside him, mind already racing through possibilities. Thirty-plus armed bandits was, even with three months of proper sword training under her belt, considerably more than she could realistically fight head-on and expect to walk away from without serious injury. Grayer's voice echoed faintly in the back of her mind: *wild is good, but wild without sense gets you killed against numbers you can't out-maneuver.*

Charging in swinging, the way some overconfident, poorly-written isekai protagonist might, was clearly off the table. That was the stupid option. The generic option. The option that got fictional heroes killed in the first ten pages so the story could hurry up and introduce someone smarter.

She was not going to be the stupid option.

She was going to be the science option.

"Sparky," she said, patting his snout thoughtfully, "I need you to move a boulder for me. A big one. Big enough to completely seal that cave entrance."

Sparky rumbled, already lumbering toward the rocky outcropping near the cave mouth, where a genuinely massive boulder sat half-buried in the undergrowth—easily large enough to block the entrance entirely, if positioned correctly.

While he worked, straining against the stone with slow, powerful heaves, electricity crackling faintly with the effort, Elliana raised both hands toward the cave entrance and began, quietly, methodically, casting.

She'd only learned proper fire magic a few weeks earlier—rank two spells, nothing especially flashy, small controlled bursts of flame meant more for lighting torches or cauterizing wounds in a pinch than for anything resembling actual combat. But a rank two fireball, cast eight times in rapid succession, aimed carefully into a sealed, enclosed space, wasn't really about the flames themselves.

It was about what the flames consumed.

Fire needs oxygen,* she thought, grinning as the eighth small fireball sailed into the cave mouth and detonated somewhere deep within, muffled and distant. *Burn enough of it fast enough, in a space that can't refill it quickly, and you don't even need the fire to touch anyone directly. The fire does the work of vacuuming the air out for you.

It was, she reflected, one of the more genuinely useful things her past life had left rattling around in her memory—more useful, certainly, than four-hour meetings or motivational seminars or the correct way to format a quarterly expense report. Basic chemistry. Combustion. The kind of thing a bored office worker picked up somewhere between actual competence and actual burnout, filed away uselessly for years, only to become, apparently, a legitimate tactical advantage in a fantasy kingdom that had never once needed to invent the phrase "oxygen deprivation."

Sparky finished maneuvering the boulder into place just as the last fireball's muffled *whump* echoed out from inside, sealing the cave entrance shut with a final, grinding scrape of stone against stone.

"Good boy," Elliana murmured, patting his snout, and settled down against a nearby rock to wait.

Fifteen minutes in, muffled shouting began filtering faintly through the sealed boulder—confused, alarmed, the sound of men realizing something was very wrong but not yet understanding what.

By the twenty-five minute mark, the shouting had shifted into something more frantic, more desperate, before gradually, steadily, tapering off into an unsettling quiet.

Elliana waited. Patient. Legs crossed, chin propped on one hand, watching the moon climb slowly higher through the gaps in the canopy above.

By the forty-five minute mark, there was nothing at all.

"Alright," she said, standing, brushing dirt off her clothes. "Sparky, if you would."

Sparky heaved the boulder aside with a low, effortful rumble, and Elliana stepped back into the cave, sword drawn out of habit more than any real expectation of resistance.

Inside, the chamber was exactly as she'd hoped: thirty-some bandits, sprawled across the floor in various states of collapse, unconscious, chests rising and falling slowly, but very definitely, thoroughly incapacitated.

She cackled, the sound echoing off the stone walls, and walked deeper into the cave without a single moment of caution now that the actual danger had been so thoroughly, bloodlessly neutralized.

The hoard, when she found it, was genuinely impressive—stacks of gold coin, chests of jewelry, crates of weapons and armor, all the accumulated spoils of two months of successful caravan raiding piled up against the chamber's far wall.

She picked through it with the discerning, faintly bored eye of someone who had, admittedly, considerably higher standards than the average adventurer.

The gold, she left where it sat. She was a princess. She had her own considerable income from boar-hunting and, more recently, from a small but growing side business selling captured Thunderbird feathers to alchemists who paid absurd prices for anything that still held a faint electric charge. Gold was, at this point, more of a formality than an actual need.

The jewelry, she picked through with slightly more interest, holding up a few pieces to catch the torchlight—a gaudy ruby necklace, a set of gold bangles, a tarnished silver circlet—before setting each one back down, unimpressed. Her own jewelry collection back at the palace, gifted over the years by various dignitaries and foreign delegations, put every single piece here to shame.

The weapons and armor were, frankly, junk—cheap steel, poorly maintained, the kind of gear a desperate bandit picked up wherever he could rather than anything worth a princess's attention.

She was beginning to feel faintly disappointed, in fact, when something at the very back of the chamber caught the torchlight and made her stop dead in her tracks.

A fang.

Not a normal fang. A massive, curved thing, easily five times thicker than her own arm, stretching nearly three meters from base to tip, its surface a deep, mottled ivory that seemed to catch and hold the torchlight in a way that felt distinctly, unnervingly unnatural.

Elliana gasped, actually gasped, crossing the chamber in a few quick strides to get a better look.

"Jackpot," she breathed, running one hand along the fang's curved length, marveling at the sheer, absurd scale of it. Whatever creature this had come from, it had to have been enormous—easily large enough to make even Sparky look modest by comparison. "Whatever you were," she murmured, to the fang, to whatever long-dead monster it had once belonged to, "you were clearly built different."

She hefted it—heavier than it looked, but manageable, especially with three months of Grayer's conditioning behind her—and carried it carefully back out toward the cave entrance, already turning over possibilities in her head. A weapon, obviously, once properly appraised and worked by a skilled smith. A bow, maybe, if the material could be shaped that way. Or a spear—something suitably dramatic, suitably her.

Before leaving, though, there was still the matter of thirty-some unconscious bandits and one very lucrative bounty poster.

She settled cross-legged just outside the cave mouth, pulled a spare scrap of parchment and a traveling quill from the small pack she kept strapped to Sparky's harness, and composed a short, deliberately vague note.

Bandit camp cleared. Blackthorn Gang, Forbidden Forest, eastern ridge near the old quarry road. All hostiles incapacitated, none killed. Recommend immediate collection before they wake up and scatter.

— Anon

She folded the note carefully, called Hera down from her patient circling overhead, and tied the message securely to the Thunderbird's leg with a length of spare cord.

"Guild hall or the guard barracks," she instructed, pointing generally in the direction of the capital's distant lights. "Whichever's closer, whichever's got the authority to actually deal with this many bandits at once. Go on, girl."

Hera let out a sharp, understanding cry and took off into the night sky, wings crackling faintly with static as she banked toward the capital.

That left one final, entirely unnecessary, entirely irresistible piece of business.

"Sparky," Elliana said, grinning wide enough to hurt, "I need you to dig. Thirty-some holes, if you would. Deep enough to bury our new friends up to the neck."

Sparky, who had long since stopped questioning the specific logic of his princess's more creative decisions, set to work immediately, powerful claws tearing through the packed earth outside the cave with methodical, almost cheerful efficiency.

One by one, Elliana dragged the unconscious bandits out into the moonlight—not an easy task, given her size relative to most grown men, but three months of conditioning under Grayer's instruction had left her considerably stronger than she looked, and stubbornness, as always, made up the rest of the difference—and settled each one into their designated hole, packing the earth back in around them until only their heads remained visible above ground, lined up in neat, orderly rows like a very strange garden.

Thirty-five, in total, once she'd finished counting. A few more than the bounty poster's estimate.

She produced a small pot of ink and a worn quill from her pack, and worked her way carefully down the line, writing a single word in neat, deliberate script across each unconscious bandit's forehead.

LOSER.

By the time she reached the thirty-fifth and final forehead, she was cackling so hard she nearly smudged the lettering, forcing herself to slow down, steady her hand, and finish the job properly.

She stepped back, surveying her handiwork with immense, undisguised satisfaction—thirty-five heads poking up out of the earth in neat rows, each one marked, each one destined to wake up sometime before dawn to find themselves buried up to the neck with a single insulting word inked across their foreheads and absolutely no memory of how they'd gotten there.

"See," she said, to no one in particular, mostly to Sparky, who rumbled in what might have been agreement or might have simply been the low, contented sound he made after digging. "This is why you use science instead of just charging in swinging like some generic idiot protagonist who thinks main-character plot armor is a real thing. Efficient. Bloodless. Considerably more entertaining." She patted the fang, now loaded carefully onto Sparky's back, secured with the same rope harness she'd once rigged for two very unwilling Thunderbirds. "And I got a souvenir out of it."

She glanced up at the moon, gauging the hour, and swung herself back up onto Sparky's back, Zeus resettling onto her shoulder the moment she'd found her balance.

"Alright," she said, patting Sparky's neck. "Let's get this fang home before anyone notices I'm gone. I've got an appraisal to schedule, a weapon to commission, and probably several extremely uncomfortable conversations to have with my mother once she finds out about tonight."

Sparky rumbled, already turning back toward the tree line, toward the distant lights of the capital, carrying his princess and her improbable prize home through the quiet dark of the Forbidden Forest.

Behind them, thirty-five bandits slept on, unaware, buried to their necks beneath a single word each, waiting for a dawn that was going to be considerably more confusing than any of them could possibly anticipate.
