Chapter 7: THE DIVINE PUNCHLINE

The Grand Temple of Talius had never held this many people at once.

Every five-year-old in the capital and the surrounding villages had been brought before the altar this season—dozens of children in total, nobles in silk and commoners in their best (and only) good clothes, all clustered together at the center of the nave beneath the vaulted ceiling. Their parents packed the space behind them in a solid wall of anxious, hopeful faces. High above, on a raised dais tucked into the temple's east corner, High Priest Gregorius stood before the altar, flanked by two junior priests holding ceremonial incense burners that trailed thin ribbons of smoke toward the frescoed ceiling.

"Every year," Gregorius intoned, his voice carrying effortlessly across the packed hall, "the gods look down upon a new generation and choose what they shall become. Some are given strength. Some are given wisdom. Some are given gifts so rare that history itself remembers the day they were bestowed." He let that hang in the air, his gaze sweeping briefly—just briefly—toward the royal family. "Today, we gather to receive what has already been given. We do not choose our blessings, children. We only discover them."

It was, Elliana thought, an extremely long-winded way of saying *stand there and glow.*

She said as much, under her breath, and Rosaria elbowed her without even looking up from adjusting her glasses.

"Behave," Rosaria murmured. "For once."

"I am behaving. Behaving is a spectrum."

Beside them, Lilia was practically vibrating.

"Do you think it'll be a sword?" she whispered, clasping her hands together. "Or a bow? Or—ooh, what if it's a shield, like Sir Aldous in the stories, the one who held the pass at Brennmoor for three days—"

"It's not going to be a shield," Rosaria said, not unkindly. "Statistically, most blessings manifest as minor utility marks. Improved memory. Slightly enhanced stamina. The chance of receiving a named, story-worthy blessing is—"

"Rosaria."

"I'm just saying the math doesn't support your hopes."

"You could be nice about it."

"I *am* being nice. I'm managing your expectations. That's the nicest thing a sister can do."

Elliana, wedged between them, reached out and pinched Lilia's arm.

"OW! Elliana!"

"Reflex," Elliana said serenely.

"You did that on PURPOSE—"

"I contain multitudes." She reached over and pinched Rosaria too, for symmetry.

"I will end you," Rosaria said, without heat, rubbing her arm and going right back to watching the altar.

The line of children shuffled forward as families were called by rank, exactly as protocol dictated: royal family first, then the noble houses in order of standing, then, once the sun had fully cleared the eastern windows, the commoners of the capital. Lilia bounced on her toes, clearly ready to march up to that altar the second anyone gave her permission, blessing or no blessing.

"Their Highnesses," a junior priest called, and the crowd parted.

"The royal princesses," Gregorius said, "please step forward."

Lilia surged ahead immediately—and then stopped dead, because Rosaria's hand had closed around one of her sleeves and Elliana's hand had closed around the other.

"Wait," Rosaria said.

"Wait for what? He *just said* to step forward—"

"Who goes first?" Gregorius asked, mild curiosity in his ancient voice. Royal-blooded triplets were rare enough that even the protocol scrolls hadn't bothered specifying an order.

Rosaria and Elliana looked at each other.

A silent, entire conversation passed between them in under a second—the kind that only sisters who have shared a womb and, subsequently, a crib, a nursery, and roughly nineteen kidnapping-adjacent incidents can have.

Then, in perfect unison, they shoved Lilia forward.

"ME?!" Lilia stumbled up the temple steps, caught herself, and turned back with wide, betrayed eyes. "You could've just—a *warning* would've been—"

"You wanted to go first anyway," Elliana called out helpfully.

"That's not the POINT—"

"Princess Lilia," Gregorius said, gesturing her the rest of the way up, and whatever protest Lilia had left died in her throat.

She approached the altar. Behind it, carved from pale stone taller than the High Priest himself, stood the temple's ancient statue of the guardian angel, wings outstretched, sword lowered in blessing. Its eyes had always looked, to Elliana, faintly bored, the way statues that have watched five hundred years of children shuffle past tend to look.

Gregorius placed one weathered hand against Lilia's shoulder and the other against the altar stone.

"Gods above," he intoned, "reveal what you have given this child."

The stone beneath his palm began to glow, faint gold light crawling up through the carved veins of the statue's wings.

Lilia's breath caught.

The light built—slow at first, then all at once, blooming outward in a column that swallowed her whole. For a moment she was just a silhouette inside the brightness, arms half-raised as if bracing for impact.

Then it snapped inward, and a mark burned itself into the base of her neck, just below her hairline—a sword wreathed in a pair of small, sharp wings, glowing white-hot for half a second before fading to a soft, permanent shimmer against her skin.

Lilia touched it, wonderingly. "It's—it's warm. It doesn't hurt. It's just—"

"THE BLESSING OF THE HEROINE," Gregorius announced, and his voice, for the first time all morning, wasn't performing formality. It was genuinely, visibly stunned.

The temple went up like a struck bell.

"A hero—did he say—"

"That's the Heroine's Mark, I've SEEN it in the old tapestries—"

"Seven hundred and sixty years," an elderly noblewoman near the front breathed, clutching her husband's arm. "Seven hundred and sixty years since the last one. The FOUNDER carried that mark—"

"The founder of the KINGDOM had that mark—"

"A new hero, in the royal line itself—"

The murmuring rolled backward through the crowd like a wave, commoners and nobles alike craning for a look, and Lilia stood frozen at the center of it, hand still pressed to her own neck, eyes bright and disbelieving.

Queen Marianne had both hands pressed over her mouth. King Aldric's jaw had gone slack in a way that would have been undignified on any other day; today, no one was looking at him anyway.

"My baby," Marianne whispered. "My baby is a *hero*."

"She's going to be insufferable," Elliana muttered to Rosaria.

"You're one to talk."

Lilia practically floated back down the temple steps, glowing nearly as bright as her new mark, and threw her arms around both her sisters at once. "Did you SEE that?! Did you—I have a MARK, Rosaria, an actual—"

"I saw it. Congratulations. Please stop crushing my ribs."

"Princess Rosaria," Gregorius called, once the temple had settled—somewhat—back into anticipatory silence. "If you would."

Rosaria smoothed her skirts, adjusted her glasses one final time, and inclined her head with the composure of someone who had clearly rehearsed this moment in her mind at least a dozen times.

"Excuse me," she said politely to no one in particular, and walked up with the unhurried gait of a girl who refused to let a divine ceremony rush her.

The ritual repeated. The statue's eyes glowed. The light gathered and poured down—this time not around her whole body, but concentrated, narrowing itself to a bright thread that wound around her left wrist like a bracelet before searing itself into her skin: an intricate sigil, circular, layered with runes that seemed to shift and rearrange themselves even after the glow had faded.

"THE MAGUS MARK," Gregorius said, and this time there was no mistaking the awe in his voice, because he actually took a step back from her.

A ripple went through the small cluster of black-robed academics standing near the dignitaries' section—the Celestia Academy delegation, headed by the elderly headmaster who'd been watching Rosaria all morning like a man watching a particularly promising crop come to harvest.

"The Magus Mark doubles the bearer's mana reservoir," one of the younger professors said, not quite managing to keep his voice down. "*Doubles* it. Do you understand what that girl will be capable of by twenty?"

"I told you," the headmaster murmured, sounding deeply, quietly satisfied. "I told the council it would be worth attending in person."

Rosaria examined the mark on her own wrist with the detached curiosity of someone reviewing a very good exam result. "Interesting," she said. "The rune density is higher than the historical average. I'll want to document this properly." She looked up, caught her mother's tear-streaked, radiant expression, and added, a touch more softly, "It's a good blessing, Mother. I'm pleased."

"You're pleased," Marianne repeated faintly. "You have a mark that hasn't been seen in generations and you're *pleased.*"

"I contain my enthusiasm efficiently."

"That's Elliana's phrase."

"I refuse to confirm or deny where I learned it."

The temple hadn't even fully settled from that revelation—professors were already murmuring, nobles were already recalculating exactly how favorably they ought to view the Valentine family—when Gregorius turned, and the entire room turned with him, toward the last of the three princesses.

"Princess Elliana," he said. "If you would step forward."

Something changed in the air. Elliana could feel it—the way five hundred pairs of eyes that had been aimed at her sisters with awe now swiveled toward her with something closer to *anticipation.* Because everyone in that room had heard the stories. The crocodile. The kidnappers. The six-year rampage of a girl who reorganized guard rotations and hunted boars for sport and once made two grown men beg for mercy using only two words repeated for two hours straight.

If the quiet one gets a hero's mark and the bookworm gets a genius mark,* she could practically hear them thinking, *what in the gods' names does the chaos gremlin get?

Elliana walked up those temple steps like she owned them.

Shoulders back. Chin up. That same smug, toothless-turned-not-toothless-anymore grin she'd been perfecting since she was three weeks old and riding an Electro Crocodile through a marble hallway.

"Ready?" Gregorius asked—and if she wasn't mistaken, there was the faintest note of nerves in the old priest's voice now, like a man who had just watched two miracles happen in a row and was no longer sure what the third one might do to his temple.

"Born ready," Elliana said. "Let's see what the gods cooked up."

Gregorius placed his hand against the altar stone.

"Gods above. Reveal what you have given this child."

The statue's eyes glowed.

And then...

Nothing.

One second passed. Two. The glow in the statue's carved eyes flickered, brightened, dimmed, like a candle caught in a draft—but no light gathered. No column. No thread of gold wound its way toward her. The altar just sat there, humming faintly, doing absolutely nothing.

Five seconds.

The crowd's murmuring—so loud, so reverent, just moments ago—dropped to a confused hush.

Seven seconds.

"Is it—" someone whispered. "Is it broken?"

Eight seconds.

Prince Lysander and Prince Caius, standing near the front with the rest of the family, exchanged a look—Lysander's brow furrowed in genuine concern, Caius's somewhere between worried and morbidly fascinated.

Nine seconds.

Lilia and Rosaria stared at their sister with matching expressions of confusion. Whatever else Elliana was—chaotic, exhausting, a menace to household pets and palace décor alike—she was, undeniably, gifted. It didn't seem *possible* for the gods to have simply forgotten her.

Ten seconds.

"Gregorius," King Aldric said, low and urgent, stepping forward despite himself. "What's happening. Why isn't it—"

"I don't—" The High Priest's composure, weathered by three generations of royal ceremonies, cracked for the first time that morning. "Your Majesty, I don't know. This has never— in all my years, the altar has never simply—"

Queen Marianne's hand found her husband's arm and gripped it hard enough to bruise. "Is something wrong with her? Is she cursed, is she—"

And then the altar detonated with light.

Not the slow, dignified bloom that had touched her sisters. This was a full, blinding *burst*, white-gold and violent, throwing every shadow in the temple into stark relief, forcing half the crowd to shield their eyes and gasp all at once.

"THERE IT IS!" Elliana crowed, delighted, not shielding her eyes at all.

The light didn't gather around her body. It gathered *above* her, hanging in the air in glowing, foreign shapes—symbols none of them had ever seen. Sharp little angular letters, nothing like the flowing script carved into every temple wall and every royal decree in the kingdom. They hung there, burning gently, spelling out something that made absolutely no sense to a single soul in that room.

Except one.

Gregorius stared up at the glowing letters, his mouth slightly open, his centuries of study utterly failing him. "I don't—these aren't— I have never seen this alphabet. It isn't in any sacred text. It isn't in any *language*—"

One of the Celestia professors actually gasped out loud, hand pressed to his chest, staring at the letters like they'd personally offended him.

"Mother," Caius said slowly, squinting up at the glowing shapes, "what does it say?"

"I don't know," Marianne said helplessly. "Gregorius, what does it—"

"I cannot read it, Your Majesty," the High Priest said, and for an ancient man who had spent his entire life being the single most authoritative voice in any given room, he sounded almost small. "There is no alphabet like this. Not in any archive. Not in any tongue, living or dead, that this temple has ever recorded."

But Elliana could read it.

Because it wasn't some divine, unknowable script. It was English. Plain, blocky, Comic-Sans-adjacent English, floating above her head in a Grand Temple full of people who had never once needed to invent the letter W.

BULLSHITERY

your actions are random, let chaos pierce your way

Elliana read it once. Read it twice.

And she started to laugh.

Not a polite laugh. Not even her usual cackle. This was a full-bodied, doubled-over, wheezing, borderline-unhinged laugh, the kind that made the surrounding priests take an instinctive step back, the kind that echoed off five-hundred-year-old marble and bounced right back at her twice as loud.

"Elliana?" Lilia's voice, tentative, worried. "Elliana, what does it say? Are you okay?"

Elliana could not answer. Elliana was laughing too hard to answer.

The light dropped down out of the air, spiraled once around her raised right hand like it had a mind of its own, and burned itself into the skin on the back of it—a small, grinning jester's mask, cap and bells and all, permanent and glowing faintly gold even after the rest of the light had gone out.

"WHAT DOES IT MEAN?" Duke Remington's voice rang out from somewhere in the noble section, equal parts scandalized and desperate. "SOMEONE TELL US WHAT IT MEANS!"

"Your Majesty," Gregorius tried again, turning to Marianne, visibly grasping for some shred of ceremonial dignity to hold onto, "perhaps the princess herself—perhaps she understands what has been—"

Marianne rounded on her daughter, the same expression she'd worn at the Big Booba Incident, at the crocodile, at the golden crib, at every single moment in five years when the universe had handed Elliana something and Elliana had immediately weaponized it for chaos.

"ELLIANA. What. Does it. Say."

Elliana wiped an actual tear from her eye, still wheezing, still grinning that grin that had made two grown kidnappers beg for mercy.

"You'll never know~" she sang, exactly the way she had after the six-seven incident, exactly the way she had every single time someone had asked her a question she had absolutely no intention of answering.

"ELLIANA EVANGELINE AURORA VALENTINE—"

"It's a GREAT blessing, Mother, don't worry about it!"

"THAT DOESN'T HELP!"

The entire Grand Temple of Talius had, by this point, fully abandoned any pretense of sacred solemnity. Nobles were whispering frantically to each other. Commoners were craning over each other's heads for a better look at the still-faintly-glowing mark on the princess's hand. One of the Celestia professors had produced a notebook from somewhere and was sketching the vanished letters from memory, muttering about *unprecedented linguistic phenomena* under his breath. Duke Remington looked like he might actually faint. High Priest Gregorius was standing very still, staring at the altar as though it had personally betrayed him after three generations of loyal service.

And in the middle of all of it, five years old, wearing a mark no one but her could translate, stood Elliana—laughing, and laughing, and laughing, utterly delighted with a blessing that answered exactly none of anyone's questions and had apparently been engineered by the gods themselves for the singular purpose of making her mother's life significantly harder.

She didn't explain what it meant.

She didn't even try.

She just kept laughing, straight through the chaos she'd caused without lifting a single tiny finger, because for the first time in five years, the universe itself had finally caught up to her sense of humor.

The chaos, by now, had a life of its own.

It rippled backward through the temple in waves—nobles turning to whisper to nobles, commoners turning to whisper to commoners, a hundred half-formed theories colliding in the space of a few seconds. Was this new? Had it ever happened before, in seven hundred and sixty years of Baptism Ceremonies, in all the scrolls Gregorius had spent his life studying? A blessing that refused to be read. A god with a sense of humor. Some of the older nobles looked faintly scandalized, as if the divine had committed a breach of etiquette. Some of the younger ones looked delighted, already composing the version of this story they'd tell at dinner parties for the next decade. Somewhere near the back, someone was already arguing that this had to be some kind of historic first, and someone else was arguing just as loudly that it absolutely was not, actually, a *good* thing, and did anyone remember what happened to the last kingdom whose royal line got cursed at the altar—

Elliana heard none of it. Or rather, she heard all of it, and found every single word of it hilarious.

She laughed until her legs gave out from under her, sinking straight down onto the temple's cold marble floor, and even that didn't slow her down—if anything, sitting down seemed to free up more of her lung capacity for the cause. She wrapped both arms around her stomach and just kept going, gasping for air between fits, tears streaming freely down her face, the little jester's mark on the back of her hand glowing faintly every time she moved it.

"ELLIANA!" Lilia was the first one moving, skidding to her knees beside her sister so fast her new hero's mark barely had time to finish settling into her skin. "Elliana, are you hurt? Say something!"

"She's LAUGHING, Lilia, she's not hurt," Rosaria said, arriving a half-second later with considerably more composure and considerably less patience, crouching down to inspect her sister with the clinical eye of someone checking a specimen for damage. "Elliana. Diagnostic question. Can you form a coherent sentence?"

"No," Elliana wheezed, delighted, and dissolved into another fit.

Caius arrived at a dead sprint, nearly tripping over his own ceremonial boots, and dropped down next to her with an expression that couldn't decide between concern and barely restrained glee. "Is she dying? Ellie, are you dying laughing? Actually dying? Because if that's how you go I need everyone to know it was worth it—"

"CAIUS!" Lysander was right behind him, considerably more composed, though even he couldn't quite keep the corner of his mouth from twitching. "Elliana. Breathe. Actually breathe, not—that's not breathing, that's just wheezing with extra steps—"

"I'm FINE," Elliana managed, between gasps, waving a small hand at all three of them as if shooing away an unnecessary fuss. "I have literally never been better in my life. Both of them. This is the best thing that has ever happened to me."

"You're on the FLOOR," Rosaria pointed out.

"By choice."

King Aldric and Queen Marianne reached her only seconds later, cutting through the ring of siblings with the particular urgency only parents possess, Marianne dropping to her knees in her ceremonial gown without a single thought for the fabric.

"Elliana. Elliana, look at me." Marianne cupped her daughter's face in both hands, scanning her over for any sign of actual harm and finding, infuriatingly, nothing but the widest, most unrepentant grin she had ever seen. "Sweetheart. Please. What is your blessing?"

Elliana wiped her eyes, took one long, steadying breath that immediately threatened to collapse back into giggles, and looked her mother dead in the eye.

Then she shrugged.

"I don't know," she said.

"You—" Marianne stared at her. "You don't *know?* Elliana, you can clearly read whatever that was, everyone saw your face, you *understood* it—"

"I said what I said." Elliana's grin didn't so much as flicker. "I don't know."

Marianne looked at her husband. Her husband looked back with the exhausted, resigned expression of a man who had learned, after five years, precisely how much ground he was going to gain by pushing further, which was to say: none.

There was no more time to press the matter regardless. Gregorius, visibly grateful for any excuse to move the ceremony back onto rails, had already cleared his throat and, in a voice that trembled only slightly, announced that the royal appraisals were concluded and the noble houses would now step forward. The line of children shuffled onward. The show, as it always did, went on—new families, new marks, new gasps of appropriately-sized awe, none of it anywhere near as interesting as what had just happened, and everyone in that temple knew it.

By the time the last commoner child had received a modest, perfectly ordinary blessing for steady hands and gone home with parents glowing with pride, the sun had shifted well past noon, and the story of Princess Elliana's mark had already outpaced the ceremony itself, spreading through the capital faster than any royal courier could have managed.

It became, within days, the single hottest topic in Valoria.

In the taverns near the palace gate, off-duty guards argued over whether it counted as a curse, and whether a cursed princess ought to still be allowed near an Electro Crocodile. In the noble salons, ladies fanned themselves and speculated in scandalized whispers that the gods themselves might be displeased with the girl's antics—though notably, none of them said this within earshot of the Queen. In the Celestia Academy's staff quarters, a cluster of professors debated late into the night over the meaning of an alphabet none of them recognized, one insisting it must be some forgotten primordial script, another insisting, more quietly, that perhaps it wasn't meant to be understood by mortals at all. Duke Remington told anyone who would listen that it was clearly a divine warning about the girl's conduct. Someone else, somewhere, floated the theory that it must simply be a *weak* blessing—nothing more than a minor curiosity, unworthy of all this fuss.

No one agreed. No one could. Because the one person who actually knew the answer had absolutely no intention of telling them, and found the entire spectacle of their guessing more entertaining than anything else that had happened all week.

The royal carriages rolled away from the Grand Temple of Talius as the afternoon light slanted gold across the capital's rooftops, the crowds still murmuring behind them, still arguing, still no closer to any real answer.

Inside, Lilia was chattering about her mark to anyone who'd listen. Rosaria had already produced a small notebook and begun sketching runic notes on her wrist blessing by memory. Caius was recounting the entire "she fell down laughing" incident for what had to be the fourth time, embellishing it a little further each round. Lysander sat with his arms crossed, looking like a man doing long division in his head, trying and failing to calculate exactly how much worse his life was about to get.

And Elliana sat by the window, chin propped on one small hand, watching the capital roll by outside the glass.

She was smirking. Not laughing anymore—just smirking, quiet and pleased with herself, the little glowing jester's mark resting on the back of her hand like a secret only she was in on.

Bullshitery,* she thought, turning the word over in her mind like a favorite toy. *Random actions. Chaos, pierce your way.

Honestly? It was, without question, the single most on-brand blessing the multiverse could possibly have handed her.

She grinned wider, watching the city lights begin to flicker on in the distance, already turning over a dozen delightfully chaotic ideas for what tomorrow might bring.

Something told her things were only going to get better from here.
