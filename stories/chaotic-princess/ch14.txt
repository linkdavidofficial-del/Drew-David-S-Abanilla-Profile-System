Chapter 14: THE TOWER, THE TOOTH, AND A TERRIBLE PROPOSAL

The far tower smelled the way it always smelled — parchment and dried herbs and the faint, lingering tang of ozone, the particular scent of a room where minor magical accidents had, over the years, become simply part of the décor rather than anything worth cleaning up after.

Fifteen minutes after leaving Elliana's bedchamber, the three princesses arrived at Sera's door in a small, mismatched procession: Elliana in front, dragon fang balanced over one shoulder like she was carrying nothing heavier than a loaf of bread; Rosaria close behind, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had long since stopped being surprised by anything but had not yet stopped being annoyed by it; and Lilia trailing last, casting nervous glances over her shoulder at every turn, as though half-expecting a squad of palace guards to materialize out of the tapestries and demand an explanation for the suspiciously large, cloth-wrapped object their sister was currently smuggling through the halls.

Sera answered the door before Elliana had even finished knocking, the Armored Anaconda draped over her shoulders lifting its head lazily to regard the three new arrivals with the same slow, ancient disinterest it regarded most things.

"Well, well," Sera said, eyes flicking immediately, unerringly, to the shape wrapped in cloth over Elliana's shoulder. "I did wonder when you'd finally bring that in for a proper look. Come in, come in — and bring the reinforcements, I see you've recruited the other two-thirds of the famous triplet set."

"They insisted," Elliana said.

"We did NOT insist," Rosaria said. "We were dragged."

"Enthusiastically dragged," Elliana clarified.

"There was no enthusiasm involved on our end whatsoever," Lilia added, hovering just inside the doorway with the particular caution of someone who had heard stories about this tower and its resident mage and wasn't entirely sure how many of them were exaggerations.

Sera laughed, delighted, waving all three of them further into the cluttered workspace with an expansive sweep of one arm. "Regardless of the acquisition method, I'm glad to finally meet the rest of you properly. Set it down there, dear," she added, nodding toward the cluttered central worktable, which Elliana cleared with one efficient sweep of her free arm, sending a cascade of old scrolls and half-finished potion ingredients tumbling unceremoniously to the floor.

"You could have just asked me to move those," Sera said mildly.

"This was faster."

"Was it, though."

Elliana unwrapped the fang with a flourish, laying it out across the worktable's full length — it hung over both ends by nearly a foot — and stepped back, arms crossed, wearing the expectant expression of a woman who already knew the answer to the question she was about to ask but wanted to hear it said out loud anyway.

Sera leaned over the fang for a long moment, saying nothing, her earlier easy humor draining slowly out of her expression and replaced by something considerably more focused. She reached out, ran two fingers along the ridged base, tilted her head to catch the light at a different angle, and then, very slowly, straightened back up.

"This," she said, "is a dragon's fang."

The reaction in the room was immediate and, from Sera's perspective, thoroughly gratifying.

Both Rosaria and Lilia's jaws dropped in perfect, synchronized unison — a rare enough sight on its own that Elliana genuinely regretted not having something on hand to commemorate it properly.

"Come again?" Lilia managed, after a long, stunned pause.

"A dragon's fang," Sera repeated, entirely serious now, no trace of her earlier playfulness remaining. "Genuine. Not a lesser wyrm, not a drake, not some overgrown lizard some enterprising merchant decided to market as one for a better price. An actual dragon."

"That's not possible," Rosaria said immediately, in the flat, reflexive tone of someone whose entire worldview had just been challenged and was scrambling to reassert order over it. "Dragons are — the last confirmed sighting in this region was over two centuries ago. The Celestia Academy's own archives list them as functionally extinct within the kingdom's borders. There hasn't been a verified specimen, living or dead, in living memory."

"And yet," Sera said, gesturing at the fang currently taking up the entire length of her worktable, "here we are."

Elliana listened to all of this with the particular, quiet satisfaction of a woman who had known she'd hit the jackpot the moment she'd laid eyes on the thing in that bandit cave, and was now simply enjoying watching everyone else catch up to that same realization.

"After hearing your whole story," Sera continued, turning to address Elliana directly, "about where exactly this came from — a bandit camp, buried under a pile of considerably less interesting loot — I don't think this was hunted. Not by them, certainly. Bandits raiding a wandering encampment of thirty-odd men do not casually acquire dragon parts as a side hustle. My best guess is they stole it. Off a merchant caravan, most likely — some trader moving it under heavy guard, probably commissioned by a noble house or a collector with more money than sense, and the Blackthorn Gang got lucky, or unlucky, depending on how you want to look at it."

"That would at least make some sense," Rosaria admitted, still staring at the fang like it might vanish if she looked away for too long. "A caravan robbery explains the provenance far better than assuming a wild bandit gang somehow tracked and killed an actual dragon on their own."

"Exactly my thinking." Sera nodded approvingly. "Smart girl."

Elliana, meanwhile, had already moved past the mystery of the fang's origin and directly into the part of the conversation she actually cared about, practically bouncing on her heels with barely restrained glee. "So! Since we've established it's real, and genuinely rare, and I have officially hit the jackpot of a lifetime — I'm going to turn it into a weapon. A spear, probably. Something with real reach."

Sera's expression shifted, the corner of her mouth curling into something that was equal parts amused and knowing — the specific look of a teacher who could see the enormous, obvious pothole in the road ahead and was, frankly, looking forward to watching her student walk directly into it.

"I don't doubt you'd love that," she said. "And believe me, I have no interest in stopping you, or telling your mother, or doing anything remotely resembling the responsible adult thing here — that's simply not a service I offer. But I do feel obligated, as your instructor, to point out a small, inconvenient problem with that plan."

"Which is?"

"Dragon material is absurdly durable," Sera said, tapping one finger against the fang's surface, which did not so much as scuff under the contact. "Even a single tooth like this one requires a working material capable of matching that durability just to shape it, let alone forge it into something functional. We're talking mithril, minimum. Possibly higher grades, depending on how deep this particular dragon's bloodline ran. And this kingdom—" she spread her hands, gesturing vaguely at the tower's stone walls, the palace beyond them, the capital beyond that "—simply doesn't have blacksmiths equipped to work material at that tier. Not outside the royal palace's own forges, and even those would need considerable modification, and considerable justification, to take on a project like this."

Elliana's face fell, just slightly.

"And," Sera added, her smile sharpening into something distinctly more mischievous, "let's not forget the other small problem: the moment you march this thing into the royal forges and ask a smith to work it, someone is going to ask exactly where a ten-year-old princess acquired a dragon's fang in the first place. Which would mean explaining your little unsupervised adventure into the Forbidden Forest three nights ago. Which I imagine your mother would have several — extremely pointed — questions about."

Elliana sighed, deflating slightly, arms crossing over her chest as she considered the sheer, unfair inconvenience of consequences existing at all.

"It couldn't be helped, I suppose," Rosaria said, though she didn't sound entirely displeased by the idea of her sister's secret nighttime bandit-clearing expedition finally catching up with her.

"But," Sera said, drawing the word out with obvious relish, watching all three sisters' heads snap back toward her in perfect unison, "there is one place. One place in particular, filled with people who know exactly how to work material like this, who wouldn't ask nearly as many uncomfortable questions, and who could actually do the job properly."

Three pairs of eyes locked onto her, identically sharp with interest, though for very different reasons.

"Dwarves," Sera said.

Rosaria blinked. "Dwarves."

"The finest craftsmen in the known world, bar none," Sera confirmed, warming to her subject now, staff tapping thoughtfully against the stone floor. "They're the only ones who consistently hoard rare metals and ores at the scale required for this kind of work — mithril, adamantine, the truly exotic alloys most human smiths only ever read about in theory. Most of the smaller clans are reclusive to the point of near-mythical — you could search for years and never find one that would agree to see you, let alone work with you. But there's one exception. A proper dwarven kingdom, up in the mountains, considerably more open to outside visitors than the rest of their kind. Trade caravans go there regularly. It's not exactly a tourist destination, but it's not a state secret either."

Rosaria's expression shifted rapidly from academic fascination to something considerably more alarmed as the practical implications of Sera's suggestion began to assemble themselves in her head.

"That would be dangerous," she said slowly. "I've studied the regional maps extensively — it's a two-week journey to the mountain range at a reasonable pace, assuming favorable weather and no significant delays. Two weeks there, two weeks back. A month, minimum, round trip. Through territory that isn't remotely close to fully mapped or patrolled."

Lilia made a small, strangled noise. "I've never even been outside the kingdom," she said, faintly. "Not once. Not even to the border towns."

"Neither have I," Rosaria admitted, with considerably less enthusiasm than she usually reserved for admitting a personal gap in her otherwise extensive knowledge.

Sera's smile, throughout this entire exchange, had only continued to widen, until finally she turned, quite deliberately, and pointed one finger directly at Elliana.

"I think," she said, "our resident chaos gremlin already has a rather different reaction in mind."

Both Rosaria and Lilia turned toward their sister.

Elliana was grinning.

It was not, by any reasonable measure, a reassuring grin. It was the specific, wide, slightly unhinged grin that every single person in this palace — servants, guards, siblings, parents, even the boars of the Forbidden Forest, if boars were capable of pattern recognition — had learned, over the course of a decade, to associate with the imminent, inevitable arrival of catastrophic chaos. Her eyes had gone distant and bright, already picturing open roads and unmapped mountains and a month of freedom stretching out ahead of her like the single most delicious meal she'd ever been offered.

She could already feel it — that old, familiar itch, the same one that had sent her rolling out of her crib at three weeks old to go find a wild crocodile in the palace moat, the same one that had put her on a boar-hunt with ten knights barely keeping pace, the same one that had cracked her ribs laughing at a kingdom's worth of overwrought royal ceremony because the universe itself had finally handed her a joke worth telling.

A whole month. Outside the walls. No supervision, no formal appearances, no Duke Remington lecturing her about decorum. Just open road, unknown territory, and whatever chaos happened to cross her path along the way.

"Elliana," Rosaria said, very carefully, the tone of a woman already bracing for impact, "please stop thinking like that."

"I have no idea what you mean."

"You have the face. The face you did over the tooth. The face you do right before something ends up on fire, or buried, or fundamentally altered against its will."

"I've genuinely never had a single original thought in my life," Elliana said serenely. "I am simply standing here."

Lilia had already begun backing, very slowly, toward the tower's exit, the specific instinct of a prey animal recognizing a predator's shift in posture a half-second before the actual pounce.

Sera, watching the entire scene unfold with the frank, unashamed amusement of a woman who had absolutely no intention of intervening, simply settled back against her worktable, arms crossed, thoroughly enjoying the show.

"I'm going," Elliana announced.

"Of COURSE you are," Rosaria said, pinching the bridge of her nose beneath her glasses in a gesture that was, by this point, less a reaction and more a full-body reflex.

"To the dwarven kingdom. In the mountains. To get my dragon fang forged into a spear."

"We heard you the first time."

"Great. Sera, thank you for your instruction, I'll write when I get there, try not to burn the tower down while I'm gone—"

"I make no promises," Sera said cheerfully, already turning toward a nearby shelf and pulling down a leather-bound notebook, jotting something down with visible satisfaction — likely, Elliana suspected, the beginning of whatever anonymous academic paper she'd threatened to write about her most chaotic student some weeks earlier.

"Anyway," Elliana continued, already turning toward her sisters, who had, by this point, both begun a slow, coordinated, entirely obvious retreat toward the tower door, "you two are coming with me."

They both froze mid-step.

"Absolutely not," Rosaria said.

"We were literally just leaving," Lilia added, entirely unconvincingly, one foot already halfway through the doorframe.

Elliana crossed the room in three quick strides and caught both of them by the shoulder — one hand each, effortless, practiced — and hauled them bodily back toward the center of the room with a strength that seemed to genuinely startle both of them every single time, despite a full decade of evidence that it shouldn't.

Lilia yelped, stumbling backward, her considerable athletic training doing absolutely nothing to prevent the retreat. Rosaria, who possessed roughly the physical strength of a well-read teacup, was pulled back even more easily, letting out an undignified squeak of alarm as her feet actually left the ground for half a second.

"What are you PLANNING," Rosaria demanded, once she'd regained her footing, voice climbing with genuine dread.

Elliana beamed at both of them with an expression of pure, radiant, entirely false innocence.

"Why are you both leaving?" she said. "Let's go together!"

"That is not — Elliana, that is not how invitations WORK, you cannot simply grab people by the shoulders and physically prevent their departure and call that an invitation—"

"I'm calling it enthusiasm."

"You're calling it KIDNAPPING—"

"I've been kidnapped before," Elliana said thoughtfully. "This is considerably more polite."

Rosaria opened her mouth, closed it, opened it again, visibly cycling through several different arguments before settling on the one she clearly considered most likely to succeed. "Elliana. Listen to me. We are ten years old. We are, in every meaningful sense, children. A month-long journey through unmapped mountain territory is not a reasonable undertaking for children, regardless of how many boars you personally have hunted."

"I mean, you're not wrong," Elliana said agreeably, "but also, that's never stopped me before, and I turned out fine."

"Did you, though."

"Debatable, but beside the point."

Lilia, meanwhile, had adopted a considerably more direct approach, planting her feet and attempting, with visible effort, to actually pull free of Elliana's grip. "Elliana. Please. Let go. I have SPARRING to get to. Actual, scheduled, supervised sparring, with Sir Baldric, in a training yard, inside the kingdom's walls, where nothing is likely to eat me."

"See, that's exactly the problem," Elliana said, tightening her grip with an ease that made Lilia's continued struggling look, frankly, a little embarrassing. "You've never gotten real practice, because there's nobody left in this palace who can actually push you anymore. Sir Baldric's good, but he's not exactly cutting his teeth against anything that fights back unpredictably. This is your perfect chance. Real terrain, real risk, real unpredictable opposition. You'll learn more in this one month than you would in a year of drilling forms in a training yard."

"I want SPARRING," Lilia wailed, with genuine, heartfelt anguish, "not adventuring into completely unknown lands with an unclear survival rate!"

"You'll be fine," Elliana said, waving this concern off with the easy confidence of a woman who had clearly given the matter approximately zero serious thought. "You're literally the Heroine. It's in your blessing. Bad things happening to you would be, frankly, poor narrative structure."

"That is NOT how blessings WORK—"

"We are TEN," Rosaria repeated, with increasing desperation, apparently deciding that if one argument wasn't landing, sheer volume and repetition might eventually succeed where logic had failed. "Ten years old, Elliana. Nothing more. We have no business marching into unmapped wilderness for a month over a WEAPON."

Elliana simply kept cackling, entirely unmoved, dragging both her still-protesting sisters a step closer to the tower door with each fresh wave of objection. Lilia made one more genuine, determined attempt to twist free — actually managed to slip half out of Elliana's grip for a triumphant half-second — before finding herself neatly recaptured, with an ease that suggested Elliana hadn't even been trying particularly hard the first time.

"No can do, sisters," Elliana announced, grin wide and utterly unrepentant. "We are going, and we are going my way."

Sera, still watching from beside her worktable, let out a soft, delighted laugh, covering her mouth with the back of one hand, the Armored Anaconda around her shoulders shifting lazily in what might have been amusement of its own.

"Before you three head off into certain chaos," she said, once her laughter had settled, "let me give you something first."

She crossed to a locked cabinet in the far corner of the tower, produced a small brass key from somewhere within the folds of her robes, and withdrew three small, unassuming objects — smooth, palm-sized stones, each etched with a faint, glowing rune and strung on a simple leather cord.

"Totems," Sera said, pressing one into each of their hands in turn. "My own work, and before you ask, no, I won't be making more of these anytime soon — they take considerable time and considerably rare materials to craft properly. Wear them. If any of you ever find yourselves in a genuinely life-threatening situation — the kind where the next hit would actually kill you — the totem absorbs the blow instead. It'll crack, and shatter, and you'll walk away from something that should have ended you. But it's strictly one-time use. Once it's gone, it's gone, so don't go treating it as an excuse to be reckless."

"No promises there either," Elliana said, already looping hers around her neck.

"I assumed as much," Sera said dryly, before turning her attention, more seriously now, to all three girls at once. "One more thing, while I have you. Your blessings — all three of yours — are exceptionally rare. Genuinely once-in-a-generation gifts, each of them, and that's saying something in a kingdom that's produced its fair share of talented children over the centuries." Her eyes settled, finally, on Elliana, and lingered there with an expression that was equal parts fond and genuinely curious. "Especially yours. A mark no scholar in this kingdom, myself included, has ever seen catalogued anywhere, in any archive, granted in a language nobody but you can even read. Only you know what it does, or what it means, or what it's capable of. That makes you a complete wild card — to your enemies, certainly, but also, frankly, to the rest of us."

Elliana puffed her chest out with obvious, entirely unearned pride, grinning wide enough to show teeth.

"We haven't AGREED to this," Rosaria attempted, one last time, with the particular, doomed energy of a woman who already knew the argument was lost but felt professionally obligated to make it anyway.

"You wanted to travel outside the palace walls, once," Elliana reminded her, not unkindly. "You've said as much, more than once, over dinner. Something about wanting to see the Academy's off-site archive expeditions someday, or visit the ruins near the old capital."

"I wanted to do that," Rosaria said, with the exhausted precision of someone correcting a very specific, very important detail, "at sixteen. Perhaps eighteen. With proper preparation, an official escort, and considerably more advance notice than 'we're leaving in fifteen minutes.' Not at ten years old, dragged along by my sister on what is, by any reasonable definition, an unsanctioned smuggling mission disguised as a blacksmithing errand."

But it was, by that point, already far too late.

Elliana was already moving, cackling with unrestrained glee, one sister firmly secured under each arm, hauling both of them bodily toward the tower door and out into the corridor beyond, her laughter echoing off the stone walls loud enough that a passing servant two floors down paused mid-step, glanced upward with an expression of weary, familiar resignation, and simply kept walking, because whatever was happening up there, it was clearly not his problem to solve.

"Goodbye, and take care, dearies!" Sera called after them, waving cheerfully from the tower doorway, entirely confident — with the easy, well-earned confidence of a woman who had spent the last several months personally training exactly one of the three girls currently vanishing down the corridor — that they would, against all reasonable odds, come back in one piece. Probably. Elliana was leading, after all, and if there was one thing Sera had learned about her most chaotic student in their brief time together, it was that the universe itself seemed reluctant to bet against her.

She turned back into her tower, humming faintly to herself, and had barely settled back into her chair before a soft knock sounded at the door.

A young palace servant poked her head in, looking faintly harried. "Excuse me — have you seen the triplet princesses? Her Majesty was expecting them for their afternoon lessons, and no one seems to know where they've gotten to."

Sera blinked at her with an expression of perfect, radiant, entirely manufactured innocence.

"Can't say that I have, dear," she said. "Haven't seen a single princess all day."

The servant scratched her head, looking unconvinced but clearly unwilling to press the matter further, offered a quick bow, and hurried back out to continue her search elsewhere.

The moment the door clicked shut, Sera dissolved into quiet, thoroughly delighted giggling, already reaching for a fresh sheet of parchment.

This, she thought, uncapping her ink, is absolutely going in the paper.
