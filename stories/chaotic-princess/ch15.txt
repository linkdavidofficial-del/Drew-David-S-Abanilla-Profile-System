Chapter 15: THE WORLD'S MOST CONVINCING LIE

"This is a terrible idea," Rosaria said, for what had to be the ninth time in as many hours, staring out at the pre-dawn courtyard with the specific, bitter resignation of a woman who had exhausted every reasonable argument against a plan and was now simply narrating her own doom for the record.

"Well, we're already here," Elliana said cheerfully, adjusting a strap on Sparky's harness with the easy, practiced motion of someone who had done this exact task a hundred times before, "might as well commit to it!"

"That's not helping."

"It wasn't meant to."

The three of them stood gathered in the palace's rear courtyard, the sky overhead still holding onto the last deep blue of night, the first thin edge of dawn just beginning to bleed color back into the world along the eastern wall. Somewhere beyond the palace grounds, roosters were beginning their halfhearted morning complaints, entirely unaware of the scale of the mischief currently assembling itself only a few hundred feet away.

It had taken three full days to get to this point — three days of preparation, three days of Rosaria drafting and redrafting supply lists with the meticulous, borderline-obsessive precision of a woman who genuinely believed that sufficient planning could compensate for the fundamental insanity of the plan itself, three days of Lilia alternating between grim determination and quiet, muttered despair, and three days of Elliana breezing through every single logistical concern with the airy unconcern of someone who had never once let a minor detail like "adequate preparation" slow her down before and saw no reason to start now.

But here they were, finally, standing in the courtyard in the grey hush before sunrise, packed and armed and, in Rosaria's case, dressed for what she had clearly decided was going to be the single most academically rigorous camping trip in recorded history.

She wore a deep red traveling robe, hemmed short enough to clear her ankles, inscribed edge to edge with a dense lattice of protective runework she'd spent the better part of the second night carving into the fabric by hand — resistance wards, minor deflection glyphs, a stabilization rune along the collar that, she'd explained at great length, would help regulate her mana output during extended casting sessions. Beneath it, sturdy tactical boots, clearly chosen for function over any aesthetic consideration whatsoever, and a wide leather belt lined, edge to edge, with small glass vials — potions she'd brewed herself over the preceding two days, sorted with obsessive precision by color and function. A satchel of magic theory books hung from one shoulder, because apparently the possibility of imminent death by mountain wildlife had not, in Rosaria's estimation, been sufficient reason to leave her reading material behind.

"Hey," Elliana said, catching sight of the runework along Rosaria's collar and grinning, "thanks for that, by the way." She tapped the fur trim of her own hood, which now carried a faint, near-invisible shimmer where Rosaria's inscriptions had been worked into the fabric. "Fire and ice resistance. Very thoughtful. I appreciate you channeling all that misplaced fury into something useful."

"If we are doing this," Rosaria said, with the weary dignity of a woman who had made peace, however reluctantly, with her fate, "then we are doing it PROPERLY. I refuse to walk into unmapped mountain territory without adequate protective measures simply because my sister has the foresight of a caffeinated squirrel."

"That's fair," Elliana said, entirely unbothered by the comparison, adjusting her own hood with visible satisfaction.

She herself was dressed in a manner that had prompted more than one passing stablehand to do a genuine double-take that morning — full leather armor beneath a thick, fur-trimmed traveling robe, boots lined with more of the same fur, an axe strapped across her back alongside her sword, and a scattering of rolled parchment scrolls tucked into a satchel at her hip that she had, when pressed, admitted might possibly, technically, have been "borrowed" from a locked cabinet in the court magicians' private study without anyone's express permission. Sparky waited patiently beside her, scaled hide gleaming faintly in the pre-dawn light, with Zeus and Hera already perched on her shoulders, feathers occasionally crackling with soft, restless static.

She looked, in total, considerably less like a princess and considerably more like a barbarian mercenary who had wandered in from an entirely different, considerably grittier story — an impression only reinforced by the wide, faintly unhinged grin currently splitting her face in two.

"You look ridiculous," Rosaria informed her.

"I look AWESOME," Elliana corrected. "There's a difference."

Lilia, meanwhile, stood off to one side, groaning under the weight of the supply pack strapped to her back — which, upon closer inspection, appeared to consist almost entirely of food, alongside a heavy canvas tent bag lashed on top for good measure. She wore her own practical armor, engraved along the joints and seams with Rosaria's runework, her sword secured at her hip, hair pulled back into a tight, resigned ponytail that did absolutely nothing to disguise the general air of a girl who had been dragged, entirely against her will, into an adventure she had wanted no part of.

"I hate this," Lilia announced, to no one in particular. "I want it on record, formally, that I hate every part of this, and that I am only participating because my sister physically will not let me leave."

"Noted and appreciated," Elliana said.

"That wasn't a compliment."

"I choose to receive it as one."

Rosaria sighed — the long, deep, put-upon sigh of a woman who had spent three straight days assembling every conceivable contingency for a journey she had never once actually agreed to — and cast one final, critical eye over their combined supplies, tallying it all up in her head with the same ruthless efficiency she applied to spell-lattice calculations.

Three days. It had genuinely taken three days to get everything ready — food, water, medicine, wards, weapons, a tent, spare clothing suited to mountain cold rather than palace comfort — and somehow, against every reasonable expectation Rosaria had walked into this with, they had actually managed it. Properly. Thoroughly. She allowed herself a small, private flicker of professional satisfaction at that, buried somewhere beneath the much larger, much louder sense of impending doom.

"Alright," Elliana said, tapping the cloth-wrapped fang now secured across Sparky's back, spear already resting in one hand — a fresh addition, borrowed from the training yard's rack, since her actual dragonfang weapon was, after all, the entire point of this trip and did not yet exist. "I think we're set."

"Finally," Rosaria muttered.

"So why," Rosaria continued, her practical mind already several steps ahead, "are we leaving at dawn? My understanding of basic tactical planning would suggest night travel is preferable for a departure like this — less visibility, fewer witnesses, considerably lower odds of being noticed and stopped before we've even cleared the gate."

Elliana clicked her tongue, shaking her head with the exaggerated, faintly condescending patience of a seasoned professional correcting a promising but inexperienced apprentice.

"Rookie mistake," she said.

"Excuse me?"

"Night travel works great for short jobs," Elliana explained, swinging up onto Sparky's back with easy, practiced grace, "covering a short distance where you can slip out, do your business, and slip back home before anyone's even finished their first watch shift. It does NOT work for a month-long trip. People are going to notice we're gone eventually, regardless of when we leave — the only question is whether they notice with an explanation already in place, or without one."

"How are we going to do it, then?" Lilia asked, adjusting her pack with a wince.

Elliana's grin widened, sharp and delighted, the particular expression of a woman about to unveil a plan she was extremely proud of.

"We'll make a fake official announcement."

Both her sisters blinked at her in near-perfect unison.

"That's it?" Rosaria said. "That's the plan? We simply announce a lie and hope for the best?"

"Not just any lie," Elliana said, swinging Sparky around toward the gate with obvious relish. "A GOOD lie. Give it fifteen minutes. Watch and learn."

• • •

Fifteen minutes later, the three princesses stood in the short queue forming at the palace's main gate, where the morning shift of guards had already begun their usual routine — checking travel papers, questioning departing merchants and servants, the low, familiar rhythm of a gate that saw enough traffic that most of its comings and goings barely registered as noteworthy.

At Elliana's quiet, deliberate cue, Lilia stepped forward first, shoulders squared, doing her level best to project confidence despite the visible tremor working its way through her hands.

"Oh — Princess Lilia," the gate guard said, straightening immediately, surprise evident in his voice as he offered a quick, respectful bow. "I wasn't expecting to see you out here this morning. You so rarely leave the palace grounds."

Lilia laughed — a slightly too-bright, slightly too-nervous laugh that, fortunately, seemed to read to the guard as simple morning cheer rather than the barely-contained panic it actually was — and nodded, launching into the explanation she and Elliana had rehearsed twice the night before.

"I wanted to head out and get some real training in," she said. "The wilderness, I mean — actual field conditions, not just the training yard. I've been thinking about it for a while now. A whole month, ideally, to really get some proper experience under my belt."

The guard considered this for a moment, nodding slowly, the explanation clearly settling into place against everything he already knew about the kingdom's future Heroine.

"That makes sense," he said, "given your status, Your Highness. Would it be safer, though, to bring along an armed escort? Even a small one?"

Lilia shook her head, doing her best to appear breezily unconcerned rather than actively terrified. "My sisters and I are doing it together," she said, gesturing back toward Rosaria and Elliana.

Rosaria, for her part, had adopted an expression of serene, scholarly composure that gave absolutely no indication of the frantic calculations currently running behind her eyes — while Elliana simply grinned, waved cheerfully with one hand, and hefted an entirely unnecessary amount of visible luggage with the other, looking, for all the world, like a woman with nothing whatsoever to hide.

The guard's gaze drifted between the three of them, and something in his posture visibly relaxed.

"If it's the three of you together," he said, "I suppose there's not much to worry about." His eyes settled, almost automatically, on Elliana. "Especially with the Boar Slayer along. Can't think of many things in this kingdom foolish enough to trouble the three of you at once."

Elliana puffed her chest out with obvious, entirely unearned pride at the title, while both her sisters shot her identical, deeply unimpressed glances.

"And the luggage?" a second guard asked, noticing, belatedly, the sheer volume of supplies currently strapped across Sparky's back and Lilia's shoulders. "That's rather a lot for a training exercise."

Rosaria stepped smoothly into the gap before either of her sisters could say something regrettable, her voice calm, measured, effortlessly convincing. "Camping equipment," she said. "A tent, provisions, the standard necessities for an extended stay outside the walls. We intend to camp properly and return once we've gained a full month's worth of field experience. Nothing more."

The guards exchanged a brief glance, nodded, and — with a final round of respectful bows and a genuine, if slightly bemused, well-wish for their training trip — waved the three princesses through the gate without further question.

The gate swung shut quietly behind them.

Lilia let out a breath she had, apparently, been holding for the better part of the last five minutes, her shoulders sagging with a relief so total it was almost comical.

"That actually worked," she said, in a tone of pure, disbelieving wonder. "I genuinely did not expect that to work."

Elliana laughed, guiding Sparky forward along the road with easy confidence. "Told you. With a reasonable enough explanation, it's not that hard to sell. Especially when it's coming from you and Rosaria instead of me — nobody questions the two of you the way they'd instantly question anything I said on my own."

"That's rather insulting," Rosaria said, though she didn't actually sound offended by it. "Also entirely accurate."

"You're the Heroine," Elliana added, glancing at Lilia. "You've got far more favor with the palace guard than I do. When you say something with a straight face, people listen. When I say something with a straight face, people start checking the perimeter for fires."

"That's fair," Lilia admitted, some of the earlier tension finally beginning to drain out of her posture as the palace walls fell steadily further behind them. "I really thought someone would stop us. I thought FOR SURE someone would stop us."

Rosaria, walking alongside Sparky with her satchel of books held close, allowed herself a small, private moment of genuine, grudging amazement — the specific amazement of a scholar forced to admit that a plan considerably less rigorous than any she herself would have designed had, nonetheless, worked flawlessly on the first attempt. Even accounting for delays, even if they ended up returning a week or two behind schedule, it would take the palace an enormous amount of time and manpower to properly search the entire Forbidden Forest and the roads beyond it before anyone even began to suspect they'd left the kingdom's borders entirely. By the time anyone thought to check the mountain roads, they'd likely already be halfway home.

It was, infuriatingly, a genuinely good plan.

She glanced sideways at Elliana — grinning, relaxed, riding easy atop Sparky's back with two Thunderbirds perched on her shoulders like she hadn't just talked her way past an entire gate garrison with nothing but confidence and a mildly plausible cover story — and felt, not for the first time, the small, uncomfortable, entirely unwelcome realization creeping back up on her.

She often forgot, in the day-to-day chaos of crocodile rides and buried bandits and honey smeared inside ceremonial wigs, that her eldest sister was, underneath all of it, genuinely, formidably intelligent. Stronger, sharper, and considerably more strategic than she generally let on, hiding behind boredom and mischief the way other people hid behind manners.

"Of course it worked," Elliana said, catching the look and grinning wider, entirely too pleased with herself. "I'm the prodigy princess, remember? No one questions me."

"More like you're too chaotic to be questioned," Rosaria said. "There's a considerable difference between the two."

Elliana laughed, unbothered, tipping her head back to look up at the brightening sky, the last stars fading fast now against the coming dawn. Ahead, the road stretched out long and open toward the tree line, the distant, hazy shape of the mountains barely visible on the horizon, weeks of unknown territory waiting somewhere beyond them.

Behind her, Lilia sighed, adjusting the weight of her heavy pack for what had to be the tenth time in as many minutes, still gripping the hilt of her sword like it might offer some form of moral support.

"We are dead, aren't we," she said, with the flat, resigned certainty of a woman who had already begun composing her own eulogy somewhere in the back of her mind.

"We are so dead," Elliana agreed cheerfully.

"Are we 'so dead' as in getting killed by monsters and bandits along the road," Rosaria clarified, with the precise, methodical need to categorize even her own impending doom, "or dead because our mother is eventually going to find out we're gone and personally end us herself?"

"Both," Elliana said, without a hint of hesitation.

"That's what I was afraid of."

"Look on the bright side," Elliana added, patting Sparky's neck affectionately as he broke into an easy, ground-eating pace along the open road. "At least you'll both die having actually left the palace grounds for once. Very few princesses can say that."

"That is not the comfort you seem to think it is."

"I contain multitudes."

Lilia groaned, long and theatrical, letting her head fall back with a dramatic sigh directed squarely at the brightening morning sky. "I want it noted, for the historical record, that I predicted this entire disaster from the very beginning."

"Noted," Rosaria said dryly. "For the record, so did I."

"And yet," Elliana said, grin utterly unrepentant, urging Sparky into a slightly faster pace as the palace walls shrank steadily smaller behind them, the tree line of the Forbidden Forest rising up ahead, "here you both are anyway. Funny how that keeps happening."

Neither of her sisters had a satisfying answer for that, mostly because it was, infuriatingly, entirely true.

High above, Zeus and Hera peeled away from Elliana's shoulders in a sudden burst of crackling static, wings beating hard against the cool morning air as they rose, banked, and surged ahead of the group, scanning the open road and the darker treeline beyond with the same sharp, patient focus they brought to every hunt. Sparky rumbled beneath them, electricity humming faintly along his scaled hide, carrying his princess and her two thoroughly reluctant sisters forward, out past the last familiar landmarks of home, toward a month of open road, uncertain terrain, and — knowing Elliana as well as they both now did — an entirely inevitable, entirely unavoidable amount of trouble.

Somewhere behind them, back within the palace's high stone walls, the morning bells rang out for the start of another ordinary day — servants beginning their rounds, tutors preparing lessons for three princesses who would not, as it turned out, be attending any of them for quite some time, and a Queen who, blissfully, had not yet had any reason whatsoever to reach for her wine.

That particular reckoning, Elliana knew with total, cheerful certainty, was still weeks away.

She intended to enjoy every single one of them.

• • •

They made camp that first night at the edge of the Forbidden Forest, just far enough past the tree line that the palace's distant lights had finally, fully vanished behind the hills, swallowed up by darkness and distance alike.

Lilia collapsed the moment the tent was up, flat on her back atop her bedroll, staring up at the canopy of stars visible through the gap in the branches overhead like she genuinely could not believe they were real, or that she was the one currently looking at them from outside a set of palace walls for the very first time in her life.

"It's so QUIET," she said, wonderingly. "No servants. No bells. No Sir Baldric yelling about footwork."

"Enjoy it," Elliana said, poking at the small campfire she'd built with the easy, practiced efficiency of a girl who had done this dozens of times before, even if her sisters hadn't. "It won't stay quiet. Things out here have a tendency to notice when I show up."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to be accurate."

Rosaria, meanwhile, had already unpacked a small folding writing desk from somewhere within her considerable luggage — an object Elliana was fairly certain had not been on the agreed supply list, and which she chose, wisely, not to comment on — and was busy transcribing the day's route into a leather-bound travel log by the light of a small, self-sustaining mage-light hovering obligingly over one shoulder.

"Documenting our impending doom?" Elliana asked, settling down across the fire from her.

"Documenting our progress," Rosaria corrected primly, not looking up from her notes. "Should something happen to us, I would like whoever eventually finds this journal to have an accurate, scholarly account of exactly how far we made it, and precisely which of your decisions were responsible for our downfall."

"Very thoughtful. Very you."

"I try."

Somewhere off in the darkness beyond the firelight, Zeus let out a low, warning chirp, and Sparky's head lifted from where he'd been dozing at the edge of camp, one large eye cracking open, tracking something out in the trees that neither Rosaria nor Lilia could see or hear.

Elliana didn't so much as flinch. She simply reached over, patted Sparky's snout once in silent acknowledgment, and returned to poking the fire, entirely unbothered.

"What was that?" Lilia asked, sitting up sharply, one hand already dropping to the hilt of her sword.

"Nothing that's going to bother us tonight," Elliana said, with the calm, unshakeable confidence of a girl who had spent a decade learning to read exactly that kind of silence. "Sparky would be up and moving if it mattered. Relax. Eat something. We've got a long walk ahead of us tomorrow, and an even longer one after that."

Lilia settled back down, only partially reassured, while Rosaria simply made a small note in her travel log and continued writing, unbothered.

Above them, the stars stretched out in every direction, brighter and more numerous than either of her sisters had ever seen them from inside the palace walls, and Elliana found herself, for just a moment, entirely still — watching the fire, listening to the forest breathe around them, feeling the particular, familiar thrill of open road and unknown territory settle warm and comfortable into her chest.

Somewhere out there, weeks ahead, lay a dwarven kingdom, a forge capable of shaping dragonbone, and — knowing her own particular luck — an entirely predictable, entirely unavoidable amount of trouble along the way.

She couldn't wait.
