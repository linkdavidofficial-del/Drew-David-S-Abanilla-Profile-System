Chapter 8: FIVE YEARS OF CHAOS, COMPOUNDING INTEREST

Five years had passed since the Baptism Ceremony, and the Grand Temple of Talius had long since stopped being the most interesting thing anyone talked about at dinner. The kingdom, as kingdoms do, had moved on. New scandals. New harvests. New wars threatening at the borders and then, thankfully, not happening after all.

The Valentine triplets, meanwhile, had each found their own particular gravity.

The training yard behind the eastern barracks rang with the clash of wooden practice swords—and, increasingly, real ones—every morning before the sun had even finished clearing the palace's outer wall. It was here, more mornings than not, that ten-year-old Princess Lilia could be found.

"Again," she said, wiping sweat from her brow, already resetting her stance.

Across from her, Sir Baldric—a broad-shouldered instructor appointed personally by the King, who had trained under three separate sword-masters before ever being trusted near royal children—shook his head, not in refusal, but in something closer to disbelief.

"Your Highness, we have gone through this drill eleven times already this morning."

"Twelve, if you count the one where you said my footwork was 'passable.'" Lilia rolled her shoulders. "I don't do passable."

Baldric sighed, the particular sigh of a man who had trained a great many royal children in his career and had never once had this specific problem before. "Very well. Again."

She was fast. That was the first thing anyone noticed, sparring against her—faster than a girl her age had any right to be, faster even than boys twice her size. But it wasn't just speed. It was the way she moved like she already knew, somewhere in her bones, exactly where the blade needed to go before her mind had finished catching up.

It had taken the instructors exactly four months to stop treating her like a princess playing at swordplay, and start treating her like a genuine prospect.

That morning, five of the palace's newest recruits—rank five knights, freshly graduated from the officer's academy, already carrying the faint shimmer of sword aura around their blades—had been assigned to run supervised bouts against her, purely as a formality, purely so the King could note in his ledger that his daughter trained "against real opposition."

She'd beaten all five before the sun was fully up.

Then, at Baldric's raised eyebrow and the recruits' wounded pride, she'd gone on to beat five more.

"Ten," Lysander said later, arms crossed, watching his youngest sister towel off with the calm satisfaction of someone who had just balanced a particularly stubborn ledger. He was seventeen now, tall and composed in a way that made grown nobles forget he'd once been the boy hiding his face in his hands over his siblings' antics. "Do you understand what that means, Lilia?"

"It means I'm getting better?"

"It means you beat ten rank-five knights in a single morning. Rank five isn't nothing. They've already manifested sword aura—do you understand what that is?"

"Sort of? The glowy sword thing?"

Lysander pinched the bridge of his nose, an old and familiar gesture. "The glowy sword thing. Yes. Let me explain it properly, since apparently no one has." He held up a hand, counting off on his fingers. "Rank one is barely a swordsman at all—most farmhands who pick up a blade for the first time start there. Rank two is a trained soldier. Rank three, a seasoned one. Rank four is where real skill begins—most knights spend their entire careers trying to reach rank four and many never do. Rank five is the first tier where the body's own mana responds to the blade unconsciously. That's the aura. It doesn't fully manifest until the body and the sword have become, in a sense, one and the same."

Lilia's eyes had gone very wide, very slowly, over the course of this explanation. "So the boys I beat—"

"Already have that. Yes."

"And I don't have that yet."

"No. You don't."

"And I still beat them."

"...Yes."

Lilia let out a whoop so loud it startled a pair of pigeons off the barracks roof. "I'm going to be rank four by twelve! Rank five by fourteen! I'm going to—"

"You're going to fall in the mud if you don't watch your footing," said a cheerful voice from directly behind her, and then something solid hooked around her ankle.

Lilia hit the training yard's mud pit face-first with a sound that could only be described as *splort.*

Caius, fifteen now and every bit as unrepentant as he'd been at seven, stood over her with his hands on his hips, positively glowing with pride at his own handiwork. "There it is. The real lesson. Never celebrate before you've checked your surroundings."

"CAIUS—" Lilia spat out a mouthful of mud, glaring up at her brother from the ground. "I am going to KILL you—"

"You'll have to catch me first, rank-four-by-twelve." He was already backing away, laughing, wooden practice sword slung lazily over one shoulder. "Besides, you can't be a proper knight if a little mud takes you out of the fight. Consider this training."

"THIS ISN'T TRAINING, THIS IS ASSAULT—"

Lysander watched his younger siblings dissolve into a genuine chase around the training yard, Lilia mud-streaked and shrieking threats, Caius cackling and just barely staying out of reach, and allowed himself, for just a moment, a real smile.

At least this fight, he thought, was one he didn't have to break up.

Across the palace, in a tower wing that had slowly, over the years, filled with more shelves, more scrolls, and more faintly humming magical apparatus than the architects had ever intended it to hold, Princess Rosaria sat cross-legged on a cushioned dais, both hands raised, weaving a lattice of glowing blue light between her fingers with the unhurried precision of someone assembling a puzzle she'd already solved in her head.

"Steady," murmured Archmage Yolanda, the senior-most of the court magicians, watching the spell-lattice take shape without so much as a flicker. "Now the binding clause."

Rosaria's fingers moved, and the lattice folded neatly in on itself, locking into a stable, glowing sphere that hovered a few inches above her palms.

"Rank four containment ward," Rosaria said, mostly to herself, checking her own work with a critical eye. "Stable. No leakage. Mana expenditure was—" she paused, calculating "—eleven percent lower than my attempt yesterday."

Archmage Yolanda didn't answer immediately. She was too busy exchanging a look with the two other court magicians standing nearby, all three of them wearing the exact same expression: the specific, slightly dazed look of professionals watching a ten-year-old casually do something most apprentices didn't manage until their late teens.

"Your Majesty," Yolanda said, as King Aldric stepped into the tower chamber, drawn by curiosity and, if he were honest, a fair amount of paternal pride. "Your timing is excellent. We were just about to discuss her progress."

"And?" Aldric asked, watching his daughter dissolve the containment sphere with a small, satisfied flick of her wrist.

"She is casting rank four spells with full proficiency," Yolanda said, and there was no exaggeration in her voice, only plain fact. "At ten years old. I have taught battlemages twice her age who still fumble the binding clause on a ward that simple."

"The Magus Mark helps," Rosaria said, holding up her left wrist, where the intricate sigil still shimmered faintly beneath her sleeve. "Doubled mana capacity means I can afford more failed attempts before I run dry. I fail forward efficiently."

"She has failed forward directly into being the most promising court mage candidate this kingdom has seen in a generation," Yolanda said dryly.

Queen Marianne arrived not long after, drawn by the same magnetic pull that always seemed to bring the whole family together whenever one of the triplets was doing something worth watching, and her face lit with unmistakable warmth at the sight of her daughter mid-lesson.

"There's my scholar," Marianne said, settling into a chair near the dais.

"Mother." Rosaria inclined her head politely, then paused, glasses catching the tower's ambient light. "You don't usually come up here during lessons. Is something wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. Quite the opposite." Marianne's smile widened, and she produced a folded letter from her sleeve, sealed with a wax crest Rosaria recognized instantly—an open book wreathed in a ring of stars, the sigil of Celestia Academy of Arcane Arts. "This arrived this morning. Headmaster Aldwyn has personally requested that you be enrolled the moment you come of age. Sixteen, as tradition dictates—but he's already arranged for early academic privileges. Access to the restricted archive. A private research mentor. Placement directly into the advanced theory track, skipping the standard entry examinations entirely."

For exactly one second, Rosaria's composure cracked. Her eyes went wide, her mouth opened slightly, and something that might, in a less controlled child, have been an actual squeal escaped before she caught it.

Then she cleared her throat, adjusted her glasses, and folded her hands primly in her lap.

"That is..." she said, very evenly, "acceptable news."

Marianne raised an eyebrow. "Acceptable."

"Gratifying, even."

"You just squeaked."

"I did not squeak. That was a controlled exhalation."

"Rosaria."

"...I have wanted to attend Celestia Academy since I was four years old and Rosaria pretended I didn't already know that," Rosaria admitted, in a very small rush, before immediately regaining her composure like a glass of water settling after a ripple. "Six years is a long time to wait. But I am capable of patience. I will use the time productively."

Marianne laughed, delighted, and reached out to smooth a stray strand of hair back from her daughter's face. "I have no doubt you will."

All the while, across the palace grounds, past the hedges, past the fountains, past the edge of the manicured gardens entirely and well into the tree line beyond—

Elliana was being Elliana.

"Where is she," Queen Marianne asked, later that same afternoon, in the particular flat, resigned tone she'd perfected over the last decade specifically for this question.

The two guards posted at the garden gate exchanged a glance, then sighed in near-perfect unison.

"The forest, Your Majesty," one of them said.

"Of course," Marianne said. "Of course it's the forest." She pressed two fingers to her temple, where a headache had already begun setting up permanent residence. "Send for her tutors. I want a full report."

The tutors arrived with the particular weariness of people who had, over many years, come to accept their fate.

"Your Majesty," said the mathematics tutor, a thin, ink-stained man named Master Corwin, "I must report that Princess Elliana has, once again, completed this week's advanced arithmetic in—" he checked his notes "—eleven minutes. The problems were designed to occupy a student for approximately four hours."

"And?"

"And she solved three of them using a method I have never seen before and cannot entirely explain, but which is, as far as I can determine, correct. She called it—" he consulted his notes again, frowning at his own handwriting "—'showing her work in binary,' whatever that means."

Marianne closed her eyes briefly. "Of course she did."

The etiquette tutor, a severe woman named Madame Aurelia who had successfully instructed three generations of nobility in the fine art of not embarrassing themselves at state dinners, stepped forward next, looking somehow both proud and deeply unsettled.

"Her mannerisms are, structurally, flawless," Madame Aurelia said. "Posture, address, the correct fork for each course, the correct depth of curtsy for each rank of visiting dignitary—she performs all of it with a precision that, frankly, some of the noble houses could stand to learn from."

"That's wonderful," Marianne said, brightening slightly for the first time all conversation. "So she's finally—"

"She performed it flawlessly for the full duration of the lesson," Madame Aurelia continued, "and then walked out of the room laughing maniacally the moment it concluded. I asked her why. She said, and I quote, 'because I *can* be normal, I just choose chaos,' and then she left before I could formulate a response."

Marianne opened her mouth. Closed it. Rubbed her temple harder.

"Is she at least *safe* out there?" she asked, finally, because at some point in the last decade she'd learned to triage her concerns by severity, and Elliana's continued survival ranked, generally, somewhere above her general conduct.

"Ten knights, Your Majesty," the garden guard offered. "All rank six. Standing overwatch at a distance, as per standing orders. Captain Helena is personally supervising."

"Captain Helena. Personally."

"She insists on it whenever the Princess goes boar hunting, Your Majesty. Says she doesn't trust anyone else to keep up."

Marianne pressed both hands over her face. "She is ten years old and she has a standing military escort for *hunting.*"

"To be fair, Your Majesty," the guard added, with the air of a man who had thought carefully about whether saying this next part was a good idea and had decided to say it anyway, "the boars have started actively fleeing the eastern forest whenever they sense her approaching. The gamekeepers say the local population is down considerably this season."

King Aldric chose that exact moment to walk in, catching the tail end of the conversation, and nodded along with the mild, settled approval of a man reviewing a particularly good harvest report.

"At least she's productive," he said.

Marianne lowered her hands just enough to stare at her husband over her fingertips. "Aldric."

"What? She sells the boars to the butchers in the lower district. Have you seen her ledger? She's turned a hunting hobby into an actual income stream. At this rate she'll be financially independent before she's twelve." He considered this with visible admiration. "If she keeps at this pace, she could register with the Adventurers' Guild the moment she's of age. Might even make a name for herself before Lilia does."

"That is NOT the metric I am measuring success by right now, Aldric."

"I'm just saying—"

"I KNOW what you're saying." Marianne exhaled slowly, walked to the tall window overlooking the eastern tree line, and stared out at the distant smudge of forest where her chaos gremlin of a daughter was, at this very moment, presumably doing something that would require several new entries in the palace's incident ledger.

Somewhere out there, she knew, Elliana was riding an Electro Crocodile the size of a small carriage through the underbrush, hunting boars for sport and profit in equal measure, guarded by ten of the kingdom's finest knights and one increasingly resigned Captain of the Guard, and having, without question, the time of her life.

Marianne sighed—a long, deep, decade-tempered sigh—and turned back to the room.

"Hire a swordsman," she said. "A real one. An expert—someone who's actually reached the upper ranks, not just a palace instructor. And a high-ranking mage as well, retired or otherwise. Check the Adventurers' Guild first. If there's no one suitable there, look further."

The assembled guards and servants exchanged confused glances.

"Your Majesty," one ventured, "for—for Princess Lilia's continued training? Or Princess Rosaria's—"

"For Elliana," Marianne said.

The confusion in the room deepened. Aldric, however, simply nodded slowly, understanding dawning across his face the way it always did, one careful beat behind his wife's decisions but never far.

"If our daughter is going to keep doing *this*—" Marianne gestured vaguely toward the window, toward the forest, toward the entire concept of her third daughter, "—whether we approve or not, whether we schedule it or not, whether we hire ten knights or a hundred to watch her do it—then we might as well make certain she's doing it *properly*. If she's going to be a menace, she will be a *well-trained* menace. If she's going to hunt boars and tame monsters and terrorize the countryside, she will do it with actual instruction behind her, from people who know what they're doing, so that the day she finally does something that outmatches ten rank-six knights, someone besides Captain Helena has a plan for it."

She paused.

"Also," she added, "bring me wine."

It was less than an hour later that the front gates of the palace announced, with considerably more commotion than usual, the return of Princess Elliana.

The guards on duty heard her before they saw her—a whooping, triumphant cry rolling down the main road well ahead of her actual arrival, followed by the unmistakable crackle of electricity and the low, thunderous rumble of Sparky's footfalls, now grown into something closer to a small, scaled mountain than the six-foot river demon he'd been a decade ago.

Then they saw her.

Riding atop Sparky, hair wild and windblown, grinning the exact same grin she'd worn since she was three weeks old, Elliana crested the hill toward the palace with an entourage that defied easy description.

Trailing behind Sparky on sturdy rope harnesses—harnesses that Elliana had apparently rigged herself, out in the forest, with whatever materials had been on hand—came two enormous Thunderbirds, their feathers crackling faintly with stored static, wings half-mantled in a display that looked equal parts irritated and, bizarrely, cooperative. And behind all of that, dragged along on a network of makeshift sleds that ten knights were currently half-pushing, half-escorting down the last stretch of road, came the boars.

All one hundred and thirty of them.

"HONEY, I'M HOME!" Elliana bellowed, loud enough that servants three floors up leaned out of windows to see what the commotion was. "Get the butchers! Get the smiths for these harnesses! And somebody find these two—" she jerked a thumb back at the Thunderbirds "—a proper aviary, because Zeus and Hera are NOT sleeping in my room, no matter how much Hera pouts about it!"

The gate guards stared. One of them opened his mouth, closed it, and simply stared some more.

"You heard the princess," Captain Helena said, riding up behind the whole procession looking, as she always did after one of these excursions, several years older than she had that morning. "Get the boars to the butchers. Get the birds to the menagerie master—carefully. And somebody wake up the stablehands, because I am not personally responsible for feeding two apex predators tonight."

"They're not predators, they're PETS," Elliana corrected, sliding down off Sparky's back with the easy confidence of someone who had been doing this since before she could properly walk. "Well. Sparky's a pet. Zeus and Hera are more like... business partners. We have an understanding."

"What understanding," Captain Helena asked, in the voice of someone who had learned, over ten years, that asking these questions rarely improved her day but felt obligated to ask anyway.

"They don't electrocute me, and I don't make them fight Sparky for territory. Mutually beneficial. Very civilized." Elliana beamed. "Anyway! Butchers! Boars! Chop chop, everyone, this meat isn't going to process itself, and I have a very lucrative arrangement with three separate shops in the lower district who are expecting delivery by sundown!"

Up in the palace's high window, Queen Marianne watched the entire spectacle unfold below—the crackling Thunderbirds, the mountain of boars, her ten-year-old daughter directing the whole chaotic operation with the brisk, practical efficiency of a woman twenty years her senior running a trading company—and said absolutely nothing for a long moment.

Then, finally, quietly, mostly to herself:

"Add it to the list of new instructors." She took a slow sip of the wine she had, wisely, already poured. "And find a druid. Somewhere on the outskirts, retired, doesn't matter. Because if she keeps this up, our daughter is going to be the most dangerous tamer this kingdom has ever produced, and I would very much like her to learn how to do that *without* accidentally starting a war with the local wildlife population."

Behind her, Aldric, still reviewing the ever-growing ledger of Elliana-related expenses and Elliana-related income—which, he noted with some satisfaction, was finally beginning to tip in the right direction—simply nodded.

"I'll draft the job posting," he said. "Should I mention the Thunderbirds in it, or let the applicants find out during the interview?"

Marianne considered this with the weary patience of a woman who had long since stopped being surprised by anything her third daughter did.

"Let them find out during the interview," she said. "It builds character."

Down below, Elliana's laughter rang out across the courtyard once more, bright and unrepentant, as one hundred and thirty boars were carted off toward the butchers and two Thunderbirds squabbled over who got the better perch in their brand-new, hastily-requisitioned aviary.

Five years in, and the chaos hadn't slowed down even slightly.

If anything, it was only just getting started.
