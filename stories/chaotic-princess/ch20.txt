Chapter 20: THE CITY OF HUGUR

The next few days passed without incident significant enough to warrant its own dramatic retelling — a small mercy that Rosaria, for one, was profoundly grateful for, given that her travel log's "unplanned combat encounters" tally had, by her own careful count, climbed to a genuinely alarming five entries in less than a week.

It was on the seventh day of travel that she finally lowered her map, squinted out at the horizon, and allowed herself a small, satisfied smile.

"According to my calculations," she announced, "we should be arriving at Hugur sometime within the hour. It's the last major settlement before the mountains proper — assuming my measurements are accurate, and given our pace over the last two days, I have every confidence that they are."

"A CITY?" Lilia perked up immediately, sitting straighter atop Sparky's back. "An actual city? With actual buildings, and actual baths, and actual BEDS?"

"A proper city, yes."

"Oh thank the gods."

When Hugur finally rose up out of the foothills ahead of them — walls of grey stone rising in tiered rings up the lower slope of the mountain itself, chimneys and forge-smoke threading up into the sky in dozens of thin grey columns, the distant clang of hammer on anvil audible even from a considerable distance — Lilia let out an audible, delighted gasp.

"Whoa," she breathed, staring up at the sprawling, sprawling scale of the place as they approached the main gate. "This is HUGE. I thought this was supposed to be some little trading post."

"It was," Rosaria said, "originally. A small mining village, according to the historical records — barely worth marking on most maps two centuries ago. But its position, directly at the base of the eastern mountain range, made it an ideal staging point for ore extraction. Blacksmiths and mining companies flocked here once the deposits were properly surveyed, and the settlement simply... grew. It's now one of the wealthiest trading hubs in the region for mineral and stone products. Practically every quality weapon or piece of armor sold in the capital passes through Hugur's forges at some point in its production."

"You know an unreasonable amount about a city we've never actually visited," Elliana observed, guiding Sparky through the gate behind them.

"I read," Rosaria said primly. "It's a skill I'd recommend you cultivate."

"I read plenty. Mostly bounty postings and stolen scroll labels, but still."

As they passed through the gate and into the city proper, Rosaria's earlier composure slipped, just slightly, into something closer to genuine, wistful longing. Market stalls lined every visible street, piled high with raw ore samples, polished gemstones, and — most temptingly of all, as far as Rosaria was concerned — small, glowing shards of what were unmistakably refined mana crystals, sold by the dozen alongside racks of finished, enchantment-ready weapons.

"It really is a shame we're on such a tight schedule," she said, gazing longingly at a nearby stall practically overflowing with rare ore samples, each one labeled in neat, precise script with its mana conductivity rating. "Under different circumstances, I would have loved to spend an entire day here. Mana crystals like these are difficult to source even through the Academy's own supplier networks. And the ore selection alone—"

"We could shop," Elliana said, smirking, already eyeing the same stall with the calculating look of a woman doing quick mental math on exactly how much of their winnings from the poker game were still burning a hole in her coin purse. "If we end up with extra time, I mean. No harm in checking."

Lilia, meanwhile, had drifted a few steps away, staring thoughtfully up at the mountain looming behind the city's furthest wall. "Hey," she said, "if this place is this close to the mountains, and it's this big on mining ore and stone... are we, um. Are we accidentally stealing ore from the dwarves? Is that going to be a problem when we show up asking for a favor?"

Rosaria shook her head, already producing her map to double-check the exact positioning. "No, actually — the dwarven kingdom sits considerably further along the range, past the ridge you can see in the distance there. Hugur draws entirely from its own local deposits. Different mountain, functionally speaking, even if they share the same broader range." She paused, brightening slightly. "Which reminds me — I did some additional reading before we left, on the route itself. There's apparently a griffin transport service that operates directly out of the city, connecting Hugur to several of the more accessible dwarven settlements. If it's still operational, it would save us considerable time and effort compared to hiking the rest of the way ourselves."

"A GRIFFIN transport service?" Lilia's eyes lit up. "Like — we just get in, and it flies us straight there?"

"Essentially, yes. There's a fee involved, naturally, but given our current funds—" Rosaria glanced pointedly at the coin pouch still hanging heavy at Elliana's hip, "—I don't imagine that will pose much of an obstacle."

"See, THIS is the kind of travel I can get behind," Lilia said, already looking considerably more energized than she had in days.

They found the transport facility easily enough — a sprawling complex near the city's northern edge, all open-air stables and wide, sand-packed landing strips, the air alive with the distant shrieking calls of griffins circling overhead in slow, patient loops. Elliana watched, genuinely fascinated, as one enormous specimen touched down nearby, folding its wings and allowing a handler to help a half-dozen passengers climb down from an elaborate, multi-seat saddle strapped across its back.

Huh, she thought, watching the whole operation with growing appreciation. So it's basically a fantasy version of a taxi service. I did not expect this world to have infrastructure this developed. Somebody's clearly been putting actual thought into commercial griffin logistics.

"This is incredible," she said aloud, grinning. "I want to ride one so bad. Can we get the good seats?"

"Focus," Rosaria said, already striding toward the ticketing counter with brisk, businesslike purpose, her sisters trailing behind her.

The woman behind the counter — a weathered, no-nonsense sort with calloused hands and an ink-stained ledger open in front of her — looked up as they approached, took in the sight of three young girls traveling without any visible adult supervision, and offered a polite, if slightly bemused, nod.

"Afternoon," she said. "Where are you three headed?"

"The dwarven kingdom," Rosaria said, already reaching for her coin purse. "Blackstone, specifically, if your service runs that far. We'd like passage for three, as soon as availability allows."

The woman's expression shifted, apologetic. "I'm sorry to say we can't take that route right now, miss. Not to Blackstone, not to any of the settlements past the northern ridge."

Rosaria's hand froze halfway to her purse. "Why not?"

"Monster's taken up residence somewhere along the direct flight path," the woman explained, grimacing. "Big one. Been causing enough trouble that we've had to suspend the whole northern route until it's dealt with. Can't risk the griffins, and can't risk the passengers. I'm sorry — truly. If you need to reach Blackstone, you'll have to take the ground route around the eastern pass. Adds a fair few days to the trip, unfortunately."

Rosaria stood very still for a long moment.

Then she turned, walked calmly out of the facility, made it exactly to the middle of the street outside, and began, with genuine and considerable volume, to shout.

"OF COURSE there's a MONSTER," she yelled, to the sky in general, hands curling into fists at her sides, sparks of pure, frustrated static crackling faintly at her fingertips. "OF COURSE there is! We have fought BANDITS, and ZOMBIES, and a MANTICORE, and now there is a MONSTER blocking the ONE convenient shortcut that would have saved us DAYS, because OF COURSE THERE IS, WHY WOULD ANYTHING GO SMOOTHLY—"

"Rosaria, please, you're going to attract attention—" Lilia grabbed at her sister's arm, glancing nervously at the growing number of startled pedestrians giving them a wide, cautious berth. "Please don't cast anything in the middle of the street, PLEASE—"

"I AM NOT GOING TO CAST ANYTHING, I AM SIMPLY EXPRESSING MY FEELINGS—"

"Your feelings are producing VISIBLE STATIC, Rosaria—"

Elliana, meanwhile, had gone quiet, staring up past the city's northern wall toward the jagged, snow-capped peaks rising beyond it, arms crossed, a slow, considering expression spreading across her face. Whatever creature was causing enough trouble to shut down an entire commercial griffin route wasn't likely to be anything small. But then again — she'd fought a manticore in a swamp two days ago and come out ahead. She'd fought thirty-five bandits alone in a cave and buried the lot of them. She'd bonded a wild Electro Crocodile as an infant. Somewhere along the way, over the course of ten increasingly chaotic years, "big scary monster in the way" had stopped registering to her as an obstacle and started registering as, well.

An opportunity.

A slow, familiar grin began spreading across her face.

"Hey," she said.

Rosaria, mid-rant, and Lilia, mid-attempt to physically restrain her, both stopped dead and turned toward her at once.

"I've got an idea," Elliana said.

The silence that followed was long, heavy, and entirely, thoroughly unconvinced.

"No," Rosaria said flatly, before Elliana had even finished the sentence.

"You haven't even heard it yet."

"I don't need to hear it. Your ideas have, without exception, resulted in either violence, property destruction, or both, every single time you have used that exact tone of voice to introduce one."

"This one's different."

"They are NEVER different."

"Just hear me out," Elliana said, grin widening into something distinctly, unmistakably dangerous, already turning to look back up at the mountain peaks looming in the distance. "We climb it ourselves. We deal with whatever's blocking the pass on our own terms, on our own timeline, and we open the route back up in the process. Consider it a favor to the local economy. Maybe the dwarves even throw in a discount for services rendered."

"That is," Rosaria said, very slowly, "an absolutely terrible plan."

"Is it, though?"

"YES."

"We've beaten everything else this trip has thrown at us so far," Elliana pointed out, entirely reasonably. "Bandits, zombies, a manticore. What's one more monster?"

Lilia, who had gone very quiet during this entire exchange, closed her eyes, took a long, steadying breath, and let out a groan of pure, resigned despair.

"I already know we're doing this," she said. "I can feel it. I can feel it happening whether I want it to or not."

"That's the spirit!" Elliana said brightly.

"That was NOT enthusiasm, that was SURRENDER—"

"Same energy, really."

Rosaria opened her mouth to argue further, considered the sheer, exhausting futility of arguing with her sister about anything at this point in the journey, and instead simply pinched the bridge of her nose beneath her glasses, took a long, deep breath, and turned to survey the looming mountain range with an expression of profound, weary resignation.

"Fine," she said. "FINE. But if we die, I want it noted, for the historical record, that I objected. Repeatedly. In writing, if necessary."

"Duly noted," Elliana said, already turning to lead the way toward the city's northern gate, practically vibrating with anticipation. "Come on. Let's go pick a fight with whatever's up there."

Behind her, Rosaria and Lilia exchanged one final, long-suffering glance — the specific, weary glance of two sisters who had, somewhere over the course of the past week, simply stopped being surprised by anything — and, with identical sighs, fell into step behind her.

• • •

Before leaving the city entirely, however, even Elliana couldn't quite resist a brief detour through the market district — a fact that Rosaria, despite her lingering annoyance over the entire mountain-climbing scheme, found herself quietly grateful for, given the sheer, tempting array of mana crystals and refined ore samples still calling to her from every stall they passed.

"Ten minutes," Elliana said, already eyeing a nearby weaponsmith's stall with obvious interest. "We've got time before we lose the light properly, and honestly, we should probably restock a few things before heading up into wyvern territory anyway."

"I am not going to argue with a chance to browse mana crystal vendors," Rosaria admitted, already drifting toward the nearest stall with poorly disguised eagerness.

Lilia, for her part, found herself drawn toward a stall selling simple, practical traveling gear — thick woolen cloaks, fur-lined gloves, the sort of unglamorous but essential equipment that three girls about to climb a snow-capped mountain range were, upon reflection, sorely lacking.

"We really should have thought of this before we decided to climb a mountain," she said, holding up a heavy fur-lined cloak and eyeing it critically.

"In our defense," Rosaria called over from the neighboring stall, not looking up from the mana crystal she was currently examining against the afternoon light, "the mountain-climbing was a spontaneous decision made approximately twenty minutes ago. We can hardly be faulted for insufficient advance planning."

"I feel like that's exactly the kind of thing we SHOULD be faulted for, given how this entire trip has gone so far."

"A fair point. Buy the cloaks."

By the time they'd finished their brief, efficient shopping spree — three heavy winter cloaks, several pairs of thick gloves, a fresh supply of dried rations, and, courtesy of Rosaria's considerable restraint despite obvious temptation, only a modest handful of new mana crystals rather than the entire stall's worth she'd clearly wanted — the sun had begun its slow descent toward the western horizon, casting long, golden shadows across Hugur's busy streets.

"Alright," Elliana said, shouldering her share of the fresh supplies with satisfaction, "NOW we're ready to go pick a fight with a mountain."

"That is not a sentence that should ever sound as casual as you just made it sound," Lilia said.

"I contain multitudes."

"You keep SAYING that—"

"Because it keeps being TRUE."

They made their way toward the city's northern gate as the last of the daylight began to fade, the guards there offering only a brief, curious glance at three young girls heading out toward the mountain pass so late in the day, before waving them through without further question — Hugur's citizens had, it seemed, long since grown accustomed to a certain amount of unusual traffic passing through their gates, adventurers and travelers of every stripe drawn by the promise of the dwarven trade routes beyond.

"You know," Rosaria said, glancing back at the city's glowing lights receding behind them as the mountain trail began to steepen underfoot, "for a city we only spent a few hours in, I find myself rather hoping we get the chance to visit again properly. Under better circumstances. With actual time to browse."

"Add it to the list," Elliana said. "Right after 'apologize properly to Mother' and 'never mention the golem incident to Duke Remington.'"

"That list is going to be considerably longer than expected by the time we get home."

"Most good lists are."

The trail wound steadily upward as the last light faded from the sky, the temperature dropping noticeably with every passing hour, and somewhere far above them, hidden among the peaks and drifting snow, whatever creature had shut down the entire northern griffin route waited, entirely unaware of the three small, stubborn figures steadily climbing toward it.

They made a brief, cold camp a few hours into the ascent, tucked into the lee of a rocky outcropping that offered at least some shelter from the increasingly biting wind.

"I want it on record," Rosaria said, huddled deep inside her new cloak with her knees drawn up to her chest, "that this is, without question, the single coldest I have ever been in my entire life."

"Noted," Elliana said, feeding another piece of kindling into their small, carefully sheltered fire. "For what it's worth, it's only going to get colder the higher we climb."

"That is not the reassurance you seem to think it is."

"It wasn't meant to be reassurance. It was meant to be a warning, so you're not surprised later."

"I appreciate the warning. I do not appreciate the underlying reality it describes."

Lilia, wrapped in her own cloak with her new gloves pulled up as far as they'd go, glanced between her sisters with a small, tired smile. "You know, for two people who fight like this constantly, you really do seem to actually enjoy each other's company."

"We contain multitudes," Rosaria said, deadpan, and Elliana burst out laughing so hard she nearly knocked over their careful pile of kindling.

"Did you just—" Elliana wheezed, delighted. "Did you just steal my line?"

"It seemed appropriate given the context."

"I'm SO proud of you right now, you have no idea."

"Please don't make this a bigger moment than it needs to be."

"Too late. This is going in my memory forever. Rosaria, master of sarcasm, finally embracing her inner chaos."

"I regret everything," Rosaria muttered, though the small, genuine smile tugging at her mouth suggested otherwise.

Lilia, watching the two of them settle back into a comfortable, companionable silence, found herself smiling too, despite the biting cold and the long, uncertain climb still ahead. Whatever waited for them further up the mountain — and given the trip so far, she had no illusions it would be anything gentle — she found, somewhat to her own surprise, that she wasn't dreading it quite as much as she probably should have been.

"Get some sleep," Elliana said finally, banking the fire down to a low, steady ember to conserve their remaining fuel. "Real climb starts at first light, and something tells me tomorrow's going to be a long one."

She wasn't wrong.

Rosaria, already burrowed as deep into her cloak as physically possible, cracked one eye open toward her sister. "Do you actually know what's up there," she asked, "or are we simply climbing toward vague, ominous mountain noises and hoping for the best?"

"A bit of both," Elliana admitted. "Whatever it is, it's big enough and dangerous enough to shut down an entire commercial griffin route. That narrows things down somewhat. Probably not a simple pack of wolves."

"That is not narrowing it down nearly as much as you seem to think."

"It's a work in progress."

"Everything with you is a work in progress."

"And yet," Elliana said, settling back against the rock with a satisfied grin, "here we all are, alive, mostly warm, and about to go find out exactly what it is together. Works out eventually."

Rosaria, too tired to argue further, simply grumbled something noncommittal and closed her eyes.

The three of them settled in for what little rest the cold, exposed mountainside would allow, bracing themselves for whatever waited further up the ascent.

CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE: ICE, FIRE, AND A VERY BAD FEELING

"This is a TERRIBLE idea!" Rosaria shouted, for what had to be the fifteenth time since they'd left Hugur's northern gate that morning, both arms wrapped tightly around herself as she trekked up the increasingly steep, snow-choked mountain path, her breath coming out in short, visible puffs against the biting cold.

"You've said that already," Elliana called back, entirely unbothered, riding easy atop Sparky's back despite the crocodile's obvious discomfort with the plummeting temperature, electricity crackling faintly along his hide in what might have been an attempt at generating extra warmth. "Multiple times. I'm keeping count."

"I intend to keep saying it until it either stops being true or we die, whichever comes first."

"Bit grim."

"I am cold, and exhausted, and increasingly convinced that we are all going to freeze to death on this mountain over a WEAPON, Elliana."

Lilia, walking point at the front of their small group, one hand resting on the hilt of her sword, glanced back over her shoulder with an expression that suggested she agreed entirely with her sister's assessment but had, at this point, simply run out of energy to argue about it further.

"She's not wrong," Lilia said. "This is genuinely one of the worse ideas you've had. And I include the crocodile in that ranking."

"Sparky was the BEST idea I've ever had," Elliana said, sounding genuinely wounded.

"Sparky is wonderful. Sparky is also currently the reason half the palace's east wing needed structural repairs, twice."

"Unrelated incidents."

The climb, in the hours before the wyverns finally showed themselves, had been slow and grueling in a way that none of them had fully anticipated back in the relative warmth of Hugur's market district. The new cloaks helped, certainly, but the wind still cut through every gap and seam it could find, and the snow — deep, uneven, treacherous underfoot — turned what should have been a straightforward ascent into a genuine test of endurance.

"How much further do you think it is?" Lilia asked, breath fogging heavily in front of her as she trudged along at the front of their small formation.

"I have no reliable way of estimating that," Rosaria admitted, squinting up at the featureless grey sky. "Whatever's causing the disruption could be anywhere along this ridge. We're essentially climbing blind, hoping we stumble across it before it stumbles across us."

"That's a deeply unsettling way to phrase our current strategy."

"I'm simply being accurate."

"You could be LESS accurate. For morale."

"Morale is not really my department," Rosaria said, though there was a faint, dry humor beneath the words that suggested she wasn't entirely serious.

Elliana, riding a short distance ahead atop Sparky, glanced back over her shoulder at both of them with a small, private smile. Whatever tension had lingered from the earlier bickering about the wisdom of this entire expedition seemed to have settled, for now, into something more companionable — three sisters, cold and tired and thoroughly out of their depth, but moving forward together regardless.

It was, she reflected, one of the things she genuinely treasured most about this life, chaos and all. In her old one, she'd have been lucky to get a coworker to cover a shift for her. Here, she had two people who would — however much they complained about it along the way — climb an entire mountain range at her side without a second thought.

She was still turning that thought over, uncharacteristically sentimental, when both Thunderbirds let out their sharp, simultaneous warning caw, and every trace of quiet reflection vanished instantly beneath the sudden, urgent need to fight for their lives.

Elliana opened her mouth to offer some further, entirely unconvincing reassurance — something about how the odds of anything actually going wrong were, statistically, quite manageable, given their track record so far — when both Thunderbirds, circling in slow, patient loops overhead, let out a sharp, simultaneous caw that cut through the cold mountain air like a blade.

Elliana's expression shifted instantly, all traces of playful bravado dropping away in a single heartbeat. She raised a closed fist, and the entire group froze in place.

"Positions," she said, voice suddenly flat and serious in a way that made both her sisters straighten immediately, all lingering complaints forgotten. Her sword and spear came up in a smooth, practiced motion.

Rosaria's hands were already glowing faintly, mana gathering at her fingertips. Lilia dropped into a low, ready stance, sword drawn, eyes scanning the snow-covered slopes ahead.

They emerged from the swirling snow in a loose, coordinated formation — five ice wyverns, pale-scaled and long-winged, each easily the size of a small carriage, frost curling visibly off their jaws with every breath. They circled once, twice, sizing up the three small figures below with cold, calculating patience, before the largest of them let out a shrieking cry and dove.

The battle that followed was, without question, the single hardest fight any of them had faced since leaving the palace.

Lilia moved first, plunging forward through snow that came up nearly to her knees, each step a fight in its own right against the sheer, unforgiving terrain. She timed her leap perfectly regardless, catching the lead wyvern's dive at just the right angle to land squarely astride its neck, driving her sword down into its skull with a strength that would have dropped nearly anything else outright.

It wasn't enough. The wyvern shrieked, thrashing violently, and though her blade struck true — again, and again, three solid hits in rapid succession — the creature refused to go down, twisting hard enough mid-flight to fling her clear off its back and send her tumbling, hard, into a deep drift of snow twenty feet below.

"LILIA!" Rosaria's voice cracked with alarm, but there was no time to check on her sister directly — two more wyverns were already banking toward her position, and her hands were moving before her mind had even fully caught up, fireball after fireball arcing out across the snow, forcing both creatures to scatter and wheel away from their approach.

She didn't stop there. Both hands came together, glyphs spinning rapidly into existence beneath the two wyverns still grounded, and with a sharp, muttered incantation — "Flame Pillar" — twin columns of scorching fire erupted directly beneath them, engulfing both creatures in a blinding, roaring blaze that sent scorched feathers scattering across the snow.

Elliana, meanwhile, had thrown herself directly into the thick of the remaining chaos, laughing with wild, unrestrained delight as Sparky charged forward beneath her, jaws snapping shut around a wyvern's leg with bone-crushing force. At the exact right moment, Hera swept down, caught Elliana bodily by the shoulders, and flung her skyward with a burst of powerful wingbeats — and mid-air, weightless for one suspended heartbeat, Elliana hurled her spear with perfect, practiced precision directly into a diving wyvern's eye.

She didn't stop there either. As the wounded creature reeled, she used the momentum of her own fall to hook her axe deep into the base of its wing, using the leverage to swing herself bodily onto its back, and in one smooth, brutal motion, drove her sword deep along its exposed flank.

"THIS IS GREAT!" she bellowed, entirely too delighted for the circumstances, as the wyvern beneath her screeched and thrashed and plummeted toward the snow below.

"IT IS NOT GREAT!" Lilia shouted back, hauling herself up out of the drift she'd been flung into, sword raised once more, blood trickling from a shallow cut along her hairline. "IT IS TERRIFYING, WHY ARE YOU HAVING FUN—"

"Lilia! Support Rosaria!" Elliana called down, already leaping clear of her downed wyvern as it crashed heavily into the snow, rolling smoothly back up onto her feet. "I've got the other two, just keep the pressure on and don't let them regroup!"

Lilia didn't argue — there wasn't time to — simply gritted her teeth, planted her feet, and launched herself a full fifteen feet into the air to intercept a wyvern bearing down on Rosaria's exposed flank, sword catching its outstretched claw with a shower of sparks and driving it hard off-course.

"Thank you!" Rosaria called, already weaving her hands through a considerably more complex pattern now, sweat beading on her forehead despite the biting cold, mana pooling thick and heavy around her raised palms. "I need — just a little longer, I'm building something bigger—"

"How much longer?!" Lilia shouted, parrying another wild swipe, boots skidding across packed snow.

"AS LONG AS IT TAKES, KEEP THEM OFF ME—"

Elliana, meanwhile, had ended up entirely surrounded — three wyverns converging on her position at once, snow and ice kicking up in violent flurries with every clashing blow, her sword and axe moving in a blur almost too fast to track. She took hits — a raking claw across her shoulder, a glancing blow from a whipping tail that sent her briefly sprawling — but every time she went down, she came back up laughing, wilder and more focused with every fresh wound, until finally, with one last, brutal flurry of strikes, she managed to drop the third wyvern to its knees in the snow, bleeding and gasping but, deliberately, still alive.

"Get down!" Rosaria's voice rang out across the battlefield, sharp with warning, both hands finally releasing the spell she'd been building.

A meteor, blazing and enormous, tore down out of the grey mountain sky.

Every single one of them dove for what cover the terrain offered, and the resulting impact shook the entire mountainside, throwing up a towering column of pulverized snow and rock that blotted out the sky for one long, thundering moment.

When it finally settled, all five wyverns lay unmoving in the snow, and Rosaria dropped to her knees, gasping, mana reserves scraped nearly bare.

Elliana straightened up out of the drift she'd thrown herself into, grinning wide despite the fresh blood trickling from a cut above her eyebrow, and let out a triumphant, echoing roar of victory that rolled out across the mountainside.

"WE WON!" she bellowed, punching one fist into the air. "That's five! FIVE ICE WYVERNS! Do you understand how much THAT would've fetched at the Adventurers' Guild—"

"I can't believe we're still alive," Lilia said, sinking heavily onto a nearby rock, chest heaving, sword still clutched in one shaking hand. "I genuinely — I thought that last one had me, I really did—"

Rosaria, still kneeling in the snow, said nothing at all.

Elliana, still riding the high of victory, was already moving on, striding toward the nearest fallen wyvern with the enthusiastic energy of a woman already planning her next move, entirely oblivious to the tightly coiled tension radiating off her sister's hunched shoulders.

"—and honestly, we should probably harvest what we can before we keep moving, wyvern scales are worth a fortune on their own, not to mention the venom glands, and if we—"

"STOP."

The word cracked out across the mountainside like a whip, sharp enough that even Lilia flinched.

Elliana turned, blinking, genuinely startled by the sheer, raw fury in her sister's voice.

Rosaria was on her feet now, robes torn and singed, hair plastered with melting snow, hands trembling — not from exhaustion alone, Elliana realized belatedly, but from something considerably closer to barely contained rage.

"We have traveled," Rosaria said, voice shaking, "for over a WEEK. We have fought bandits. We have fought THE DEAD. We have fought a MANTICORE. And now we have nearly DIED, all five of us including your beasts, to a pack of ICE WYVERNS, on a mountain that we CHOSE to climb, entirely voluntarily, because YOU decided a commercial flight delay was an acceptable reason to gamble our lives instead."

"Rosaria—"

"I am not FINISHED." Rosaria's voice cracked, but she pushed through it, chest heaving. "We are TEN. We came out here because of a WEAPON, Elliana. A weapon you wanted, for reasons that still, even now, mostly amount to 'it would be cool.' And I have gone along with it, because I love you, and because some absurd, misplaced part of me genuinely believed nothing could actually touch you — but I am DONE pretending that isn't insane. This isn't a game. We are not invincible. We are GAMBLING with our lives, over and over, and I am TIRED of it."

Elliana opened her mouth, closed it, and — for possibly the first time in her entire second life — genuinely did not have an immediate, glib response ready.

"We're still alive," she managed, finally, quieter than her usual tone.

"We SURVIVED," Rosaria said. "That is not the same thing as this being ACCEPTABLE." She drew in a long, shuddering breath, wiping angrily at her eyes with the back of one snow-crusted sleeve. "I don't like this. I don't like any of it. I am not having fun. I am scared, Elliana, all the time, every single day of this trip, and I am tired of pretending otherwise just because you never seem to be."

She turned, then, without waiting for a response, and began trudging back down the slope toward the distant, hazy outline of Hugur's walls.

"Rosaria, wait—" Elliana was already moving after her, all her usual confidence suddenly, visibly rattled. "I'm sorry, okay? I didn't— I genuinely thought this would be, I don't know, good for us. A sister thing. An adventure. I didn't think about—"

Rosaria didn't stop. Didn't turn around. Simply kept walking, shoulders rigid, refusing to acknowledge the apology at all.

Lilia, still seated on her rock, watched the whole exchange with wide, uncertain eyes, unsure whether to intervene or simply let it play out.

It was in that exact moment of tense, unresolved silence that one of the Thunderbirds — still circling watchfully overhead even after the battle's end — let out a sound none of them had heard before.

Not the sharp, alert caw that signaled an approaching threat.

Something considerably more urgent. More frightened.

Elliana's head snapped up instantly, all thoughts of the argument forgotten, scanning the sky and then, slowly, turning toward the ridge looming above them, where the caw seemed to be directed.

Her eyes went wide.

"TAKE COVER!" she screamed, already moving, already reaching for Rosaria.

A massive, churning ball of snow crested the ridge above them, growing larger with terrifying speed, blotting out the grey sky entirely as it thundered down the slope toward their position.

Rosaria froze — one instant of pure, paralyzing horror, eyes locked on the oncoming wall of white death bearing down on her.

Elliana didn't hesitate. She closed the distance in a single, desperate sprint, and shoved her sister bodily out of the avalanche's path a bare half-second before the full, crushing weight of it slammed into the space where Rosaria had just been standing.

The resulting explosion of snow and ice swallowed the entire mountainside whole.

Lilia threw both arms up over her face, bracing hard against the ferocious blast of wind and flying ice that followed, digging her boots into the packed snow to keep from being swept off her feet entirely. Through the swirling white chaos, she thought she heard her sisters' names — her own voice, she realized a half-second later, screaming them both at the top of her lungs.

She took one stumbling step forward, ready to charge blindly into the settling wall of snow after them, when something made her freeze in place instead.

A shadow.

Not the shadow of a cloud, or a passing bird. Something enormous, spreading slowly across the snow at her feet, growing larger and larger as whatever cast it drew closer.

Lilia turned, very slowly, and looked up.

Towering above her, cresting the ridge where the avalanche had originated, stood a construct unlike anything she had ever seen described in any book, any lesson, any tale Rosaria had ever recited over dinner. A golem — but colossal, easily three times the height of the palace's tallest tower, built from massive, interlocking slabs of dark, polished stone reinforced with veins of glowing blue light that pulsed like a heartbeat across its entire hulking frame. A single wyvern, she realized with dawning, sick horror, would have looked genuinely small cradled in the palm of one of its massive hands.

"A — a GOLEM?!" she gasped, backing away on legs gone suddenly, uselessly weak.

Two enormous, glowing blue eyes swept downward, fixed with sudden, unmistakable purpose directly on her small, exposed figure standing alone in the snow, and the massive construct raised one colossal fist high overhead.

Lilia scrambled backward, boots slipping uselessly on the packed snow, certain in that single, terrifying heartbeat that there was no possible way she could move fast enough to escape what was coming.

She didn't have to.

Zeus and Hera dropped out of the swirling sky in perfect tandem, talons catching her beneath the arms with bruising force, hauling her bodily off the ground and out of the fist's crushing path a bare instant before it slammed down into the very spot where she'd been standing, sending a fresh shockwave of snow and stone fragments blasting outward in every direction. They carried her a safe distance further up the slope before setting her down, none too gently, in a fresh drift of snow.

She'd barely caught her breath before a second shape burst up out of the snow nearby — Sparky, shaking a thick coating of powder off his scaled hide, equipment and supply bags still miraculously, entirely intact where they'd been strapped across his back.

"Sparky!" Lilia scrambled upright, sword already back in hand despite the fresh terror still hammering through her chest. "Find them! Find Elliana and Rosaria, GO!"

The crocodile let out a low, urgent rumble and surged off through the snow in the direction of the avalanche's wake, and Lilia turned back to face the massive, looming construct now rotating slowly, deliberately, to track her once more with its glowing, unblinking blue gaze.

She swallowed hard, gripping her sword with both hands, and stared up at the sheer, impossible scale of the thing looming over her.

"How the fuck," she muttered, "am I going to beat this guy?"

CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO: FLESH WOUND

Rosaria woke slowly, the world swimming back into focus in disorienting fragments — cold, blurred whiteness above her, a dull, ringing pressure in her skull, and, cutting through all of it, a voice, close and familiar and increasingly frantic, calling her name over and over.

"Rosaria. Rosaria, come on, wake up. Rosaria!"

Her eyes fluttered open properly, and the first thing she registered was Elliana's face hovering directly above her, snow-dusted and streaked with blood, but grinning — that same wide, stubborn, unstoppable grin she always wore, even now, even here.

"Good," Elliana said, relief flashing briefly across her expression before the grin reasserted itself. "You're alive."

Rosaria blinked, still fuzzy, already opening her mouth to ask what had happened — and then her gaze dropped, and the question died in her throat entirely.

A deep, ragged wound stretched across Elliana's abdomen, the fabric of her leathers torn and soaked through with blood, dark and glistening even against the white snow beneath her.

Rosaria felt every last trace of grogginess vanish in an instant, replaced by a cold, plunging wave of pure panic.

"ELLIE!" She shot upright so fast her head spun, hands scrambling toward her sister's side, fingers hovering uselessly over the wound as though afraid that touching it at all might make things somehow worse. Her face had gone bone-white, stomach lurching with a nausea that had nothing to do with the head injury. "Oh gods — oh gods, what HAPPENED, you're — you're BLEEDING, you're really badly hurt, we need — I need to—"

"I'm fine," Elliana said, waving off the panic with an ease that did absolutely nothing to actually reassure her sister. "It's just a flesh wound." She punctuated this with an entirely inappropriate wink.

"THAT IS NOT A FLESH WOUND, THAT IS A HOLE IN YOUR STOMACH—"

"It's mostly closed already. Mostly."

"WHY," Rosaria demanded, voice cracking, hands still hovering helplessly over the injury, "did you do that? Why did you throw yourself in front of an AVALANCHE for me, you could have — you could have DIED, Elliana, you could have actually died, and for WHAT, so I could—"

Elliana reached up with one hand, still visibly weakened, and patted her sister's head with a gentleness that somehow made the whole situation worse rather than better.

"It's my job to be the big sis," she said simply.

Rosaria's jaw tightened, fury and terror tangling together into something that came out, when she finally found her voice again, as a low, shaking growl.

"You IDIOT," she snapped, gritting her teeth so hard her jaw ached, "you absolute, reckless, self-sacrificing IDIOT—" She was on her feet a moment later, blinking back tears she refused to let actually fall, forcing herself to focus, to think, to be useful instead of simply falling apart at her sister's side.

It was only then, taking in their surroundings properly for the first time, that she registered how far they'd actually been carried from the point of impact — Sparky must have dragged them both clear of the avalanche's worst, she realized, hauling them a considerable distance up a rocky outcropping while she'd been unconscious. From their current elevated vantage point, she could see clearly out across the ruined slope below, where a small, frantic figure was sprinting back and forth across the snow, getting repeatedly, violently flung aside by massive, thundering impacts.

"Lilia," Rosaria breathed, horror crashing back over her in a fresh wave.

She raised one still-shaking hand, murmuring a quick, focused incantation, and a shimmering lens of light gathered in front of her eye, sharpening her vision across the considerable distance to the towering, glowing-eyed construct dominating the valley below.

Her breath caught.

"That's too big," she said slowly, studying the golem's smooth, impossibly refined surface with growing, clinical alarm, "and far too polished to be a natural construct. Golems that occur naturally — from mana-saturated stone, ambient magical buildup, that sort of thing — they're rough. Asymmetrical. This one is engineered. Deliberately built. And it doesn't have visible gears, or joints, or any of the standard mechanical linkages you'd expect from a constructed golem of that scale. Whatever's powering its movement isn't conventional."

Elliana, wincing as she pushed herself up into a sitting position against Rosaria's protests, frowned. "Well, that's bad," she said. "You think somebody's doing this on purpose? Like, actually built that thing and set it loose here deliberately?"

"Possibly," Rosaria said grimly, lowering her hand and letting the scrying lens dissipate. "I don't have enough information to say for certain. But something about the timing — a griffin route conveniently shut down, an engineered construct conveniently blocking the only alternate path — feels less like coincidence and more like deliberate obstruction. I don't know why. I don't know by whom. But for now, we need to stop it, before it kills Lilia, or worse."

"Welp," Elliana said, glancing down ruefully at her own bloodied side, "I'm out of commission for the melee portion of this fight, that's for sure."

"Then what are we supposed to do?" Rosaria demanded, glancing back down at the chaos unfolding below with rising panic. "I cannot fight something that size on my own, Elliana, not while also keeping Lilia alive—"

"See that core there?" Elliana pointed, wincing at the movement, toward the massive golem's chest, where a huge, pulsing orb of blue light was clearly visible even from this distance, embedded deep within its stone frame. "That's what's actually driving it. Standard construct theory — you don't need to break the whole thing down, you just need to rip that out. No core, no golem."

"EASY for you to say! HOW, exactly, are we supposed to accomplish that?!"

Elliana's grin, despite the blood loss, despite the obvious pain still etched across her face, sharpened into something distinctly familiar. "The bigger they are," she said, "the harder they fall. All you have to do is—"

• • •

Down in the valley below, entirely unaware of the tactical conversation currently taking place on the ridge above her, Lilia was running for her life.

"THIS IS THE WORST IDEA EVER!" she screamed, sprinting flat-out across the snow as the golem's massive fist came down behind her, the impact shaking the entire mountainside and sending a fresh shockwave of packed snow rippling outward.

She stumbled, caught herself, and had barely regained her footing before a second, considerably more alarming problem announced itself: the impact had dislodged a massive shelf of snow further up the slope, and it was now thundering down toward her position in a fresh, roaring wall of white.

"OH COME ON—" She was running again before she'd even fully processed the danger, boots pounding through deep snow, lungs burning, screaming wordlessly at the sheer, relentless unfairness of it all.

An ice wall rose abruptly out of the ground directly in her path — not something she'd conjured, she realized with a jolt of confusion, glancing up in time to catch the fresh avalanche slam harmlessly into the newly formed barrier and scatter away to either side.

She looked up toward the distant peak, and there, silhouetted against the grey sky, stood Rosaria — pale, clearly exhausted, but on her feet, one hand still extended toward the ice wall she'd just conjured across an impossible distance.

"LILIA!" Rosaria's voice carried, faint but sharp, down across the valley. "Keep it distracted! Draw its attention, don't let it focus on any one spot!"

Whatever fear had been threatening to overwhelm her evaporated instantly, replaced by something steadier, sharper — the simple, clarifying relief of having an actual plan, and someone else fighting alongside her again instead of facing this monstrosity entirely alone.

"Got it!" she shouted back, and spun on her heel, dashing directly across the golem's front, waving her sword overhead, shouting every insult she could think of at the top of her lungs to keep its massive, glowing eyes locked firmly on her.

Up on the ridge, Rosaria's hands moved in a rapid, complex pattern, mana pooling thick around her fingertips despite her already-depleted reserves. The ground beneath the golem's left foot shifted, softened, and sank several feet in an instant — the massive construct lurched, stumbling hard, one arm swinging out wildly to catch its balance.

Rosaria didn't let up. Gesturing again, sharper this time, she reached out with her magic to seize the very stone and earth surrounding the staggering golem, wrenching it upward in thick, gnarled tendrils that wrapped tight around its neck and shoulders, hauling downward with relentless, grinding force until the massive construct crashed forward onto all fours, pinned and struggling in the snow.

"NOW!" Rosaria screamed, voice raw. "TAKE OUT THE CHEST!"

Lilia blinked, skidding to a stop. "WHAT?!"

"THE ORB! LILIA! THE ORB!"

"OH!" Understanding crashed into place all at once, and Lilia was moving again instantly, sprinting directly beneath the golem's massive, straining frame, craning her neck up to spot the glowing blue orb embedded in its chest, still pulsing steadily despite the construct's current, pinned struggle.

She leapt, sword raised high, driving the blade with every ounce of strength and momentum she had directly into the glowing core.

It didn't so much as scratch the surface. Her high-grade steel blade — already weakened from the fight against the wyverns — shattered on impact, sending shards spinning off into the snow.

"OH NO!" She stared down at the ruined hilt still clutched in her hand for exactly one panicked half-second before instinct took over entirely. She dropped the broken sword, lunged forward, wrapped both arms around the massive, glowing orb, and heaved with every last reserve of strength her body had left.

The core resisted, held fast within its stone housing — and then, with a final, desperate wrench and a loud, cracking pop, it tore free entirely, sending Lilia tumbling backward into the snow with the massive orb clutched tight against her chest.

Above her, the golem's glowing blue eyes flickered, dimmed, and finally winked out entirely, the enormous construct freezing mid-motion, still pinned in its half-collapsed kneeling posture by Rosaria's earth constructs, utterly, permanently still.

For a long moment, nothing moved at all.

Then Rosaria, still standing on the distant ridge, sank slowly to her knees in the snow, and began, quietly, to laugh — a shaky, disbelieving, thoroughly exhausted sound.

"We did it," she managed, between breaths. "We actually did it."

Lilia scrambled upright, cradling the massive core carefully against her chest, and half-ran, half-stumbled her way up the slope toward her sister, hauling Rosaria back to her feet with her free arm the moment she arrived.

Together, the two of them made their way back to where Elliana still sat propped against the rocky outcropping, and both let out identical, relieved gasps at the sight of her sitting upright, grinning, clearly battered but very much alive.

"You're alright!" Lilia said, dropping to her knees beside her.

Elliana's grin widened, though it came with a visible wince. "Mostly," she admitted. "I may have stolen three high-tier elixirs from the court magicians' private stores before we left. Figured they might come in handy."

Rosaria's expression went instantly, thoroughly flat. "You STOLE from the court magicians."

"In my defense, I was RIGHT to steal from the court magicians."

"That doesn't make it BETTER, Elliana, that makes it WORSE, we are going to be in SO much trouble—"

Lilia, meanwhile, had gone visibly pale at the mere thought of the reception awaiting them back home. "We are going to be in so much trouble when we get back," she said faintly.

Elliana laughed, wincing at the movement, and reached up to tug at the cord around her neck, where the small, rune-carved totem Sera had given her hung, cracked clean through the middle.

"Well, at least THIS did its job," she said. "Shattered the moment the avalanche hit. Would've been a lot worse than a flesh wound without it, honestly."

Rosaria's stomach dropped. She reached for her own totem with suddenly trembling fingers, and found it, too, cracked straight through.

"Mine broke as well," she said quietly. "During the fall, I suppose. I didn't even notice."

Lilia checked hers last, turning the smooth stone over in her palm — fully intact, unmarked, exactly as it had been the day Sera gave it to them.

The three of them sat with that in silence for a long moment.

"We were on the verge of death," Rosaria said finally, very quietly. "Both of us. If Sera hadn't given us those—"

"Then I guess I owe Teacher Sera a proper thank-you when we get home," Elliana said, softer now than her usual bravado, some of the earlier weight of Rosaria's outburst on the mountain clearly still sitting with her.

She glanced past both her sisters, toward where Sparky lay curled protectively nearby, Zeus and Hera settling down beside him, all three of her beasts exhausted but, thankfully, entirely uninjured.

"They're okay," she said, relief plain in her voice. "Just tired." She turned back to Rosaria, and for once, the easy grin faded into something considerably more sincere. "Hey. I'm sorry. For all of it — dragging you both out here, the golem, nearly getting you killed. I really did think this would be some great sister-bonding adventure. I didn't think enough about what it'd actually cost if things went wrong."

Rosaria studied her for a long moment, exhaustion and lingering fear and something gentler, warmer, all tangled together behind her glasses.

Then she sighed, shook her head, and allowed herself a small, tired smile.

"It's fine," she said. "I'm sorry too — that you got hurt, saving me. I don't ever want you doing that again. But it's fine."

Elliana grinned, some of her usual brightness returning. "No promises there."

"Of course not."

And with that — bruised, exhausted, one sister significantly worse for wear than the other two, but all three of them undeniably, gratefully alive — the sisters had, against every reasonable expectation, successfully defeated the golem.

Elliana glanced back over her shoulder at the massive, frozen construct one final time as they began preparing to move on, a familiar, calculating glint returning to her eye despite everything.

"Tsk," she said. "Shame we can't carry that thing with us. I wonder how much it'd fetch as scrap metal alone."

"Come on," Rosaria said, already hauling her bodily up by the arm, entirely too exhausted to properly roll her eyes. "Let's go."

She did, however, keep the core.

It rode carefully wrapped in spare cloth for the remainder of the journey, tucked safely into Rosaria's own pack rather than left behind with the rest of the wreckage — a high-quality, massive golem core, fully intact, humming faintly with residual mana even now. Rosaria found herself turning the possibilities over in her mind with growing, private excitement as they resumed their climb: a construct of her own design, built and bound to her personal magic, engineered properly this time rather than left to whatever dark purpose had originally animated this one. She would need to consult with the court magicians. Perhaps her tutors at the Academy, once she finally enrolled. Perhaps, if the opportunity presented itself here in the dwarven kingdom, their smiths as well.

A slow, distinctly wicked little smile crept across her face as she walked, entirely too pleased with herself.

Beside her, Elliana and Lilia exchanged a wary glance, and — by silent, mutual agreement — both took a small, deliberate step further away from whatever dark, calculating aura their sister had suddenly begun radiating.

"I think," Lilia murmured, "we may have created a monster."

"Please," Elliana said. "I invented that particular family tradition. She's just borrowing it."

• • •

They made camp that evening considerably further down the mountain, at last putting real distance between themselves and the fallen golem's frozen, hollowed-out shell, and for the first time since the fight against the wyverns, the mood around their small fire settled into something genuinely, quietly peaceful.

"I've been thinking," Rosaria said, cradling a cup of hot tea between both hands, steam curling up into the cold night air, "about what I said. On the mountain. Before all of this."

"You don't have to bring it up again," Elliana said, though she was watching her sister carefully. "I meant what I said. I heard you."

"I know. I just — I want to make sure you understood it properly. I wasn't angry that we were in danger. I chose to come on this trip. I could have refused, could have made you drag me the entire way kicking and screaming if I'd genuinely wanted to. I didn't. I came because some part of me wanted this too, even if I complained about it every single step of the way." She paused, turning her cup slowly in her hands. "What frightened me wasn't the danger itself. It was watching you throw yourself in front of it for me without even hesitating. I don't know what I would have done, if that avalanche had actually—"

"Hey." Elliana's voice softened, all trace of her usual teasing gone. "It didn't. I'm here. You're here. Lilia's here, and so is a very large, very dead golem back up that mountain that isn't going to bother anyone else ever again."

"I know. I just needed you to understand why I reacted the way I did."

"I do understand," Elliana said. "And for what it's worth — I'm not going to promise I'll stop doing reckless things. I probably will keep doing them, honestly, it's kind of my whole personality at this point. But I heard you. I'm not going to pretend your fear doesn't matter just because I'm not always scared of the same things you are."

Rosaria studied her for a long moment, then nodded slowly, something in her shoulders finally easing.

"That's fair," she said. "That's genuinely fair. Thank you."

Lilia, watching the exchange from across the fire, felt something warm settle in her chest despite the day's exhaustion. "You know," she said, "I think this might actually be the sister-bonding trip Elliana originally promised us. Just took nearly getting crushed by a golem to get there."

"I stand by my original pitch," Elliana said, grinning, the earlier heaviness already lifting from her voice. "Excellent bonding experience. Ten out of ten. Would recommend."

"You are never allowed to plan another trip like this again," Rosaria said, though there was no real heat behind it anymore.

"No promises."

"I really did stop expecting any."

The fire crackled on into the night, and for the first time since the avalanche, all three sisters slept soundly, their totems — broken and whole alike — hanging safely around their necks, a quiet, permanent reminder of exactly how close the mountain had come to taking everything from them.

CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE: THE KINGDOM OF BLACKSTONE

Two more days of travel — considerably slower than any of them would have liked, given Elliana's still-healing wound and Rosaria's badly depleted mana reserves, but blessedly, gratefully free of any further avalanches, wyverns, or engineered golems — finally brought the three sisters to the very top of the mountain range, and their first proper glimpse of the dwarven kingdom of Blackstone.

It was, even from a considerable distance, an unmistakably impressive sight.

The kingdom itself had been carved directly into the mountain's living rock — sheer basalt walls rising in tiered, fortified rings, reinforced at every visible seam with heavy black ironwork that gleamed dully in the thin mountain sunlight. Enormous defensive cannons lined the outer perimeter at regular intervals, each one easily large enough to make a grown man look like a child by comparison, their barrels angled outward across the surrounding slopes with grim, watchful purpose. Flanking the massive main gate, twin colossal statues of armored dwarven warriors stood eternal guard, carved with such precision that individual rivets and chain links were visible even from where the three sisters stood at the base of the final approach.

"Finally," Rosaria breathed, adjusting her glasses as she took in the full scale of the fortress-kingdom looming above them. "We've arrived."

"Whoa," Lilia said, craning her neck back to try and take in the sheer height of the walls. "So THIS is the dwarven kingdom. It's so much bigger than I imagined."

"To be fair," Elliana said, still favoring her injured side slightly as they began the final climb toward the gate, "you imagined a village. This is basically a fortress carved directly into a mountain. Slight difference in scale."

"I stand corrected. Extremely corrected."

The line at the main gate, when they finally reached it, stretched considerably longer than any of them had anticipated — merchants, traders, and the occasional adventuring party all queued up beneath the watchful eyes of the gate guards, submitting to what appeared to be a thorough, methodical security screening. Fortunately, the line moved at a brisk enough pace that it took them less than half an hour to reach the front.

The guard stationed there was, in a word, imposing — a broad, heavily armored dwarf standing barely taller than Lilia despite his sheer, dense bulk, flanked by four massive stone-and-iron defense golems standing at rigid, silent attention, and a half-dozen additional armored sentries positioned along the gate's inner approach.

His sharp eyes settled immediately on the three of them — three young, foreign girls, travel-worn and clearly battered, one visibly injured, accompanied by a full-grown Electro Crocodile and two enormous Thunderbirds — and he raised one gauntleted hand, calling the group to a halt.

"State your business," he said, voice a low, gravelly rumble.

Rosaria and Elliana, by unspoken, near-instantaneous agreement, both took a small step backward, leaving Lilia standing alone at the front, blinking in sudden, startled confusion.

"Wait, why me—" she started, before catching the pointed, expectant looks from both her sisters and realizing, with a small, internal groan, that this particular burden had officially been passed to her.

Panic set in almost instantly. Lilia, drawing on some deep, half-remembered instinct from every dramatic tale she'd ever devoured as a small child, planted her broken sword hilt-first into the packed snow with as much dramatic flourish as she could muster, struck what she hoped was an appropriately serious, commanding pose, and began speaking in a rush of words that came out considerably more garbled than intended.

"I am — Princess Lilia Rosemary Valentine, of the Kingdom of Valoria, uh — the Hero of Humanity, and — Princess of the Kingdom—" She stumbled slightly over her own already-stated title, cheeks flushing, but pressed on regardless with sheer, stubborn determination.

The reaction from the assembled guards was immediate, and considerably more impressed than Lilia's mangled delivery had any right to produce.

"A foreign princess," the lead guard said, straightening with visible surprise, exchanging a quick, startled glance with his fellow sentries. "And the Hero of Humanity, no less — traveled all this way, on foot, through the mountains?"

Flustered, mentally scrambling to salvage whatever dignity remained in her introduction, Lilia simply rolled forward with the momentum she'd already built, deciding — with the particular, reckless confidence of someone too embarrassed to stop talking — that if she was already committed to this bit, she might as well commit fully.

"We defeated the rogue golem," she added, with as much authoritative gravity as she could manage, "the one that's been blocking the pass near Hugur. It's been dealt with."

The effect was instantaneous, and dramatic.

The lead guard's eyes went wide. Behind him, several of the other sentries broke formation entirely, murmuring urgently amongst themselves.

"The golem," the guard repeated, disbelief plain in his voice. "The one near the eastern ridge? The one throwing ice boulders at every caravan that dares the pass?"

"That's — yes, that one," Lilia confirmed, increasingly unsure of exactly how large a reaction she'd just triggered.

"We've had reports of that thing for WEEKS," the guard said, practically vibrating with barely contained relief. "It's been throwing entire boulders of packed ice at anything that gets within range — far too large and durable for our standard sentries to handle safely. The King's own war council has been drafting expedition plans, preparing to send out THOUSANDS of dwarves and golems to deal with it. There was even serious discussion of deploying the Titan Golems — and now you're telling me three young girls handled it on your own?"

"...Yes?" Lilia said, growing more uncertain by the second about whether this admission was going to earn them a warm welcome or a full interrogation.

It turned out, gratifyingly, to be the former.

"This is Princess Rosaria," Lilia continued, gesturing back toward her sister with rather more confidence now that the initial hurdle had been cleared, "and Princess Elliana. My sisters."

The guards' expressions shifted from impressed to something bordering on outright astonished.

"THREE princesses," one of the other sentries murmured, awestruck. "Three royal princesses of a foreign kingdom, traveling entirely unescorted through wyvern territory, and clearing our most dangerous open threat along the way?"

Rosaria, sensing an opportunity to steer the conversation away from Lilia's increasingly flustered, rambling delivery and toward something more efficient, stepped forward smoothly.

"We did encounter some travel difficulties along the way," she said, with brisk, practiced diplomacy, "but our original purpose in coming here was to make a formal request of your kingdom's craftsmen. We've traveled a considerable distance specifically to seek an audience."

The lead guard drew himself up straighter, some visible internal calculation clearly resolving itself behind his eyes.

"Given that you've already rendered our kingdom a considerable service," he said, "I believe this more than qualifies you for an audience with the King himself. Please — follow me."

Lilia, trailing behind as they were escorted through the gate and into the kingdom proper, let out a small, disbelieving breath. "We're going to see the KING? Already? That fast?"

"Seems that golem was a genuine, ongoing headache for them," Elliana said, grinning despite her still-healing wound, taking in the sprawling, torch-lit grandeur of the underground city stretching out ahead of them — vast, cavernous halls carved directly into living stone, glowing veins of embedded mana crystal threading through the ceiling far overhead like captured starlight. "Lucky timing, I guess."

"Convenient," Rosaria agreed, equally impressed despite herself, "though I suspect 'lucky' undersells the sheer amount of trouble that golem must have been causing them."

They proceeded through the kingdom without further incident, escorted by their guard through wide, echoing corridors lined with elite, silent sentries in ceremonial plate, until finally arriving at a massive, vaulted throne room, its walls inlaid with veins of gold and precious ore, the entire chamber humming faintly with ambient magical resonance.

Upon the throne itself sat a broad, powerfully built dwarf, his long, thick beard braided with intricate metal rings, dark hair pulled back in equally elaborate braids, dressed in ceremonial armor that gleamed dully in the chamber's ambient light.

"I am Durga Stonebraid," he announced, voice booming warmly across the chamber as they approached, "King of the Dwarven Kingdom of Blackstone. It is not often we host royalty from Valoria within these halls — and never, in my recollection, quite so unexpectedly." His sharp eyes swept across all three of them with obvious, genuine interest. "The three famed princesses, if my guard's report is accurate. Lilia, the Heroine. Rosaria, the Magus." His gaze settled last, and lingered longest, on Elliana, a slow grin spreading beneath his thick beard. "And Elliana... the Boar Slayer."

Both her sisters shot her an immediate, pointed look.

Elliana, entirely unbothered, puffed her chest out with visible pride. "It seems my reputation precedes me," she said.

King Durga let out a booming, genuinely delighted laugh that echoed off the chamber's high stone ceiling. "Indeed it does, young princess. Word travels even to mountain halls such as these — the crocodile rider, the lightning of the Valentine kingdom, and no small number of other titles besides."

"Wow," Elliana said, grinning wider, "I should really start writing those down. I'm losing track."

"Ellie!" Rosaria hissed, mortified, elbowing her sharply in the ribs.

The King, far from taking offense, simply laughed harder, clearly delighted by her brazen confidence rather than put off by it. It was evident, plainly, that whatever minor embarrassment his princess-guests might feel on her behalf, he was simply, thoroughly pleased that the golem plaguing his kingdom's trade routes had finally been dealt with, and in no mood to dwell on formalities.

Lilia, deciding this seemed like an appropriate moment to reassert some sense of royal decorum, stepped forward and offered a proper, respectful bow. "We are princesses of Valoria, Your Highness," she said. "It is our duty to help others where we're able. We didn't set out intending to encounter your golem — but once we did, we couldn't very well leave it be."

King Durga nodded approvingly, stroking his braided beard. "A fine sentiment, and finer still that you backed it with action. It seems the Hero grows well into her title."

He shifted forward on his throne then, addressing the matter of proper gratitude with the brisk, businesslike efficiency of a ruler well-practiced in the diplomatic weight of debts owed. "You have rendered my kingdom a considerable service, Princesses. It is only right that Blackstone offer you a reward befitting the deed."

Elliana's hand shot up immediately, practically vibrating with anticipation. "Actually," she said, "we came here for exactly this reason!"

She turned, gesturing to where Sparky had been allowed to wait just outside the throne room's main entrance under careful dwarven supervision, and a moment later, two attendants carried in the cloth-wrapped dragon fang, setting it down before the throne with visible effort given its considerable weight.

King Durga's eyes widened the instant the cloth was pulled away, his easy humor giving way to something sharper, more focused.

"A dragon's fang," he said, voice hushed with genuine reverence, leaning forward to study it more closely. "Genuine, and freshly harvested besides. It has been decades since anything of this quality has passed through these halls."

"I want your finest craftsman to forge it into a bow," Elliana said, already producing a folded, hand-drawn blueprint from her satchel with obvious pride. "I've been designing it for weeks. I love archery, and I figure, why settle for an ordinary bow when I could have a dragon-fang one instead?"

The King studied the blueprint for a long moment, clearly impressed despite himself, before nodding slowly. "This can be done. Our craftsmen would relish the challenge, in truth — dragon material of this quality rarely crosses our threshold." He paused, considering. "But a single commissioned weapon, forged from material you yourselves provided, is too small a reward for a deed of this magnitude. I would offer more."

He turned his attention to Lilia. "Princess Heroine. Is there something Blackstone's smiths might provide you as well?"

Lilia blinked, startled by the direct question, before glancing down at the shattered remains of her sword still hanging uselessly at her hip. "My sword broke," she admitted. "During the fight with the golem, actually. If it wouldn't be too much trouble — a replacement would mean a great deal."

"Done," King Durga said immediately, without a moment's hesitation. "You shall have the finest blade our forges can produce."

His gaze shifted last to Rosaria, who straightened immediately, recognizing — with the sharp, practical instincts of a woman who had clearly been waiting her entire turn for exactly this moment — that this was not an opportunity to be wasted.

"Your Majesty," she said, "we successfully recovered the golem's core intact during the fight. I would request your kingdom's assistance in helping me craft a construct of my own, using it as the foundation."

King Durga's eyebrows rose with genuine, delighted surprise. "You salvaged the core? Intact, even after the construct's destruction?" He let out another booming laugh, clearly impressed. "Most who face a foe of that scale think only of survival, and rightly so — but you had the foresight to consider what might be salvaged from the wreckage besides. That speaks well of you, young Magus."

"The frame itself is still largely intact as well," Rosaria added, warming to the King's evident approval, "left behind on the mountainside. I imagine its remaining materials could prove useful in the construct's reconstruction, should your craftsmen wish to retrieve them."

"An excellent suggestion, and one we shall act on swiftly." The King nodded, clearly delighted by the sheer practicality of the request. "All three of your requests shall be honored, Princesses. My finest smiths and artificers will begin work immediately."

He gestured then to a nearby attendant, instructing that the three sisters be shown to one of the kingdom's finest guest estates to rest and recover while the requested work was completed, and with a final round of formal, grateful bows, the audience concluded.

The guest estate, when they finally arrived, proved to be a genuinely luxurious affair — carved directly into the mountain's stone like everything else in Blackstone, but lined with plush furnishings, warm hearths, and, most importantly as far as Lilia was concerned, actual, proper beds.

She collapsed onto hers the moment they were shown inside, letting out a long, blissful groan of pure relief.

"I have never," she announced to the ceiling, "in my entire life, appreciated a bed this much."

"You say that after every single inn we've stayed at," Rosaria pointed out, settling more decorously onto her own bed nearby, already pulling her travel log free to begin cataloguing the day's events.

"And I mean it MORE every single time."

Elliana, easing herself carefully down onto the room's third bed with a wince — her wound still healing, however much the elixirs had accelerated the process — allowed herself a rare, genuinely contented sigh.

"Not gonna lie," she said, "that went a lot smoother than I expected. Free weapons, free construct parts, a warm bed, and we didn't even have to fight our way in."

"We fought our way in TWICE, on the road HERE," Rosaria said dryly. "I believe we more than earned the smooth part."

"Fair point."

The three of them lay there for a long while in comfortable, companionable silence, exhaustion and lingering adrenaline slowly giving way to something warmer — the simple, hard-won satisfaction of having made it, against genuinely considerable odds, all the way to their destination in one piece.

"You know," Lilia said eventually, staring up at the carved stone ceiling with a small, thoughtful smile, "as absolutely terrifying as most of this trip has been, I don't actually regret it. Not really."

"Even the zombies?" Elliana asked.

"...Even the zombies. Mostly."

"Even nearly getting flattened by a mountain-sized golem?" Rosaria added.

Lilia considered this for a long moment. "Ask me again once I've actually slept in this bed for eight straight hours," she said finally, "and I'll give you a real answer."

Elliana laughed — a genuine, warm sound, free of her usual wild edge — and let her eyes drift closed, the low, steady murmur of her sisters' continued conversation fading gently into the comfortable dark.

Tomorrow would bring new weapons, new gear, and — knowing their luck so far — very likely new chaos to go along with it.

But for tonight, at least, all three of them slept soundly, warm, safe, and, for the first time in over a week, entirely, blessedly undisturbed.

• • •

The following morning, while the kingdom's smiths continued their work, King Durga himself insisted on personally showing his three young guests through some of Blackstone's more impressive public halls — a gesture of hospitality that Lilia, in particular, embraced with unrestrained enthusiasm.

"This entire hall is carved from a SINGLE piece of stone?" she asked, craning her neck up at the soaring, vaulted ceiling of the kingdom's Grand Forge Hall, where dozens of dwarven smiths worked in perfect, practiced synchrony at rows of glowing furnaces.

"Carved, and then hollowed, over the course of nearly three centuries," King Durga confirmed, clear pride evident in his voice. "My ancestors believed that a kingdom's strength should be reflected in the permanence of its halls. Stone does not rot, does not burn, does not fade with time, so long as it is properly maintained."

"It's incredible," Rosaria said, genuinely awed, already making rapid notes in her travel log regarding the architectural principles at play. "The structural engineering alone must be extraordinary, to support a span this wide without any visible support columns."

"Ah, a scholar's eye," the King said, clearly delighted. "If you've an interest in it, I could have one of our senior architects walk you through the load-bearing principles later, before you depart. It's not often we host a foreign princess with a genuine appreciation for dwarven engineering."

"I would love that. Genuinely."

Elliana, trailing a short distance behind, found herself less interested in the architecture and considerably more interested in the smiths themselves — watching, with real fascination, the practiced, rhythmic precision with which they worked, hammer and tongs moving in perfect coordination, molten metal flowing exactly where each craftsman intended it to go.

"You could learn a thing or two here," King Durga observed, catching her watching. "If you've any interest in the craft itself, beyond simply commissioning the finished product."

"Maybe someday," Elliana said, surprising herself slightly with how genuine the consideration actually was. "I've got enough on my plate right now, between sword training and magic lessons and generally causing chaos. But I wouldn't rule it out."

"The offer stands, whenever you find the time. Blackstone's halls are always open to those who've earned our respect, and you three have more than earned it."

They spent the better part of the morning touring the kingdom's public halls — the Grand Forge, the sprawling underground markets, even a brief, careful glimpse into the royal archives, where Rosaria very nearly had to be physically dragged away after discovering an entire section dedicated to historical golem construction theory.

"We could stay another week," she said, only half-joking, as they were finally, gently steered back toward the guest estate for lunch. "Just to properly go through that archive."

"We have a schedule," Lilia reminded her.

"I KNOW. I'm allowed to mourn the loss regardless."

By the time evening fell on their fourth and final day in Blackstone, all three sisters had grown genuinely, unexpectedly fond of the mountain kingdom and its gruff, generous king — a fondness that made the following morning's farewell, gear finally complete, considerably more bittersweet than any of them had quite expected.

CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR: THE GRAND, CHAOTIC HOMECOMING

It took Blackstone's finest smiths and artificers the better part of four days to complete all three commissions — four days that the sisters spent, much to Lilia's open, unrestrained delight, doing very little besides resting, eating extraordinarily well, and exploring the kingdom's more accessible public halls under a polite, watchful dwarven escort.

But when the finished gear finally arrived, laid out with obvious pride across a long ceremonial table in the guest estate's main hall, every last moment of the wait proved thoroughly, completely worth it.

Lilia reached her new sword first, and let out a squeak of pure, unfiltered joy the moment her fingers closed around the hilt.

"It's so LIGHT," she breathed, lifting the blade and turning it slowly, watching the way the mountain sunlight filtering through the hall's high windows caught along its flawless, gleaming edge. "And it's warm — is it supposed to feel warm?"

"Pure mythril," the attending smith explained, watching her reaction with evident, professional satisfaction. "A hundred percent, no alloy. Conducts mana beautifully, which explains the warmth you're feeling — it's already resonating faintly with your own reserves. You'll find it holds an edge considerably better than your previous blade, and it won't shatter nearly so easily under strain."

"I love it," Lilia declared, swinging it in a few quick, testing arcs, grinning from ear to ear. "I love it so much. I'm never putting it down. This is the best sword in the history of swords."

Rosaria, meanwhile, had drifted toward the far end of the hall, where her own commission stood waiting — and went very quiet, staring up at it with an expression of pure, undisguised awe.

The construct towered above her, easily matching the destroyed golem's original, colossal height, but where that creature had been blunt, brutal stone and iron, this one was something else entirely — an elegant, unmistakably feminine silhouette, built with the same deliberate, regal grace as a chess queen carved from pale, polished stone and gleaming silver-blue metal. A long, elegant spear rested in one massive hand, a broad, ornately engraved shield strapped to the other arm, its featureless, serene face somehow conveying an impression of calm, watchful majesty despite having no visible eyes at all.

"She's beautiful," Rosaria breathed, reaching out to rest one hand reverently against the construct's polished leg. "Truly beautiful. Thank you — this exceeds anything I could have imagined."

"The core you provided was of exceptional quality," the lead artificer said, inclining his head respectfully. "And the salvaged frame material gave us considerably more to work with than we typically have on hand for a commission of this scale. She'll answer to your will alone, bound directly to your own mana signature. Should you wish to name her, the binding ritual allows for it."

Rosaria considered this for a long, thoughtful moment, gazing up at the construct's serene, watchful face.

"Seraphine," she said finally, softly. "I'll call her Seraphine."

Last of all, Elliana approached her own commission — and went very, very still.

The bow resting on the table before her was sleek, pale, almost luminous white, its curved limbs carved with fine, flowing patterns that seemed to catch and hold the light in ways that shifted subtly with every small movement. She lifted it carefully, testing its weight, and found it considerably heavier than she'd expected — dense, tightly compressed, humming faintly with barely contained power the moment her fingers closed around the grip.

"A hundred platinum arrows come included with the commission," the smith added, gesturing toward a neatly stacked quiver resting nearby, "free of charge, given the circumstances of your visit."

Elliana said nothing for a long moment, simply turning the bow over in her hands, feeling out its balance, its weight, the faint, electric hum of stored potential thrumming just beneath its polished surface.

Archery was just a hobby I picked up after work, in my old life, she thought, a small, genuine smile tugging at the corner of her mouth, entirely unlike her usual wide, mischievous grin. Some overpriced range near the office, twice a month, whenever I actually had the energy left after a twelve-hour shift to bother showing up. Never thought it'd end up mattering. Never thought any of it would.

She nocked one of the offered platinum arrows, drew the string back with smooth, practiced ease despite the bow's considerable weight, and let it fly toward a distant practice target set up at the hall's far end.

The arrow didn't simply hit the target. It punched straight through it, continued on, and obliterated a considerable section of the reinforced stone wall behind it in an explosion of dust and shattered rock, leaving the surrounding smiths and attendants gaping in stunned, appreciative silence.

I can definitely use this, Elliana thought, grinning now, wide and genuine. Definitely, definitely use this.

"I love it," she said aloud, turning the bow over once more with visible, reverent care. "This might be the single best thing anyone's ever given me. And I've been given a golden crib."

King Durga, who had arrived partway through the presentation to personally oversee the final delivery, laughed heartily at the display, clearly delighted by both the successful demonstration and the sheer, obvious joy radiating off all three princesses.

"It pleases me greatly to see our craftsmanship so well received," he said. "You are welcome to remain in Blackstone as long as you wish, Princesses. Enjoy our halls, our hospitality — you have more than earned it."

It was, admittedly, a tempting offer. But Rosaria, ever mindful of the schedule they'd already stretched considerably thinner than intended, stepped forward with a polite, grateful bow.

"Your hospitality has been more than generous, Your Majesty," she said, "but our primary purpose here is complete. I'm afraid we really must begin our journey home — our family will already be worried enough as it is."

The King accepted this with good grace, and after a final, warm round of farewells — and one enthusiastic, if slightly overwhelming, embrace from Elliana that visibly startled the dignified monarch — the three sisters set off from Blackstone the following morning, considerably better equipped than they'd arrived, and considerably more famous throughout the dwarven kingdom besides.

They caught a griffin taxi back to Hugur — the northern route freshly reopened now that its resident golem problem had, quite thoroughly, been resolved — while Seraphine, far too massive to comfortably fit any transport smaller than the mountain range itself, was instructed to make her own way home on foot, moving at a steady, ground-eating pace that would, according to Rosaria's calculations, actually beat their own return time by a fair margin.

The trip home, hopping from city to city by griffin and cart in turn, took considerably less time than the outbound journey had — a few days, rather than a full week, free of any further bandits, zombies, manticores, or engineered golems along the way, much to everyone's collective, quiet relief.

• • •

Back at the Valentine palace, meanwhile, an altogether different sort of chaos had been unfolding for over a week.

Queen Marianne had not slept properly in six days.

"SEARCH EVERY VILLAGE WITHIN A HUNDRED MILES!" she shouted, for what felt like the hundredth time that morning alone, pacing a frantic, well-worn groove into the war room's stone floor while a rotating cast of exhausted guards, scouts, and court magicians attempted, with steadily diminishing success, to keep pace with her increasingly desperate orders. "I want every road checked, every inn questioned, every forest searched top to bottom — my daughters have been MISSING for over a WEEK—"

"Your Majesty," one of the court magicians ventured cautiously, "we have, in fact, searched every—"

"SEARCH IT AGAIN."

King Aldric, seated nearby at the war room's central table, simply took another slow, measured sip of his morning coffee, watching his wife's frantic pacing with the particular, weary calm of a man who had, admittedly, done rather a lot of this exact same thing himself over the preceding days, and had by now largely exhausted his own capacity for panic.

"Perhaps," he offered mildly, "we might also consider the possibility that they are, in fact, entirely fine, and simply off doing something we would very much prefer not to know the details of."

"THAT," Marianne snapped, rounding on him, "IS NOT COMFORTING, ALDRIC."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to be realistic."

Prince Lysander, passing through the corridor outside the war room with Lady Celestine at his side, paused at the sound of his mother's raised voice, sighing with the particular, weary resignation of a young man who had heard some version of this exact argument at least once a day for the past week.

"Still no word?" Celestine asked gently, slipping her hand into his.

"None," Lysander said. "Though I confess I'm less worried than Mother is, at this point. If anyone in this family is capable of surviving whatever nonsense she's dragged herself into, it's Elliana. I'm considerably more worried about whatever's on the OTHER end of wherever she's gone."

From further down the corridor, Prince Caius — now a genuinely tall, broad-shouldered sixteen, though his grin remained exactly as mischievous as it had been at six — sauntered past with the easy, unbothered confidence of a young man entirely unconcerned by the palace's ongoing panic.

"Relax," he said, catching the tail end of the conversation. "They're fine. They're MORE than fine. Honestly, I feel worse for whatever they've been running into out there than I do for them."

"You seem remarkably certain of that," Lysander said dryly.

"I am. It's Elliana, Lysander. The universe itself has learned, at this point, not to bet against her."

• • •

Off to one side of the palace's sprawling training yard, largely unnoticed amid the general chaos of the ongoing search efforts, Sera stood watching the entire spectacle unfold with a small, private, entirely unrepentant smile, the Armored Anaconda draped comfortably across her shoulders as always.

"You knew," Grayer said, approaching from across the yard with his sword slung casually over one shoulder, sharp eyes fixed knowingly on the mage's carefully composed expression. "You knew where the young ladies went all along, didn't you?"

Sera shrugged, entirely unbothered by the accusation. "I may have had a general idea," she admitted. "I couldn't help myself, honestly. It's simply far too entertaining, watching my old friend the Queen work herself into a state like this. We've known each other since our own court days together, you know — I used to fluster her exactly this way over considerably smaller matters, long before either of us had children of our own to worry about."

Grayer chuckled, shaking his head slowly. "You're a menace."

"I prefer 'enabler of legends,' but I'll accept 'menace' as a reasonable substitute." She glanced up toward the eastern horizon, expression shifting into something considerably more thoughtful. "They should be returning right about now, actually, if my estimates are correct."

As if summoned directly by her words, a massive, ground-shaking thud rolled across the palace grounds, rattling window panes and sending startled birds scattering from the surrounding trees.

A guard came sprinting into the war room moments later, breathless, face pale with alarm. "Your Majesties! There's — there's something massive approaching the main gate! A construct, some kind of enormous golem, marching directly toward the palace!"

The reaction across the palace was immediate, and total.

Alarm bells rang out across the courtyard. Guards scrambled into defensive formation along the outer walls. Word spread with terrifying speed through the surrounding streets, and within minutes, the entire capital had descended into a swirling mixture of panic, confusion, and open, gawking astonishment — the construct's sheer, colossal scale visible even from streets clear across the city, looming steadily larger with every thundering step.

Nobles fled for cover. A guard atop the outer wall, catching his first full glimpse of the approaching giant, dropped his spear entirely, jaw hanging open in disbelief.

And then, as the massive construct drew close enough for its details to finally resolve properly against the afternoon sky, the panicked shouting shifted, almost instantly, into something else entirely.

Because perched comfortably atop the golem's right shoulder, waving cheerfully down at the gathered, gawking crowd below, sat three familiar, unmistakable figures.

"Hiiiii!" Lilia called out, waving both arms enthusiastically.

"WELCOME TO CHAOS!" Elliana bellowed, cackling loud enough to carry clear across the courtyard. "AHAHAHA!"

"Over here!" Rosaria added, considerably more composed, though even she couldn't quite suppress a small, satisfied smile at the sheer, overwhelming spectacle they were currently providing.

Sparky rode along on the construct's opposite shoulder, looking thoroughly pleased with himself, while Zeus and Hera wheeled in slow, lazy circles through the sky overhead.

King Aldric, midway through another sip of his coffee, choked violently, sputtering as his eyes went comically wide with disbelief.

The gathered crowd erupted into an absolute chaos of shouting, gasping, and utterly bewildered reactions. Several nobles fanned themselves in open, dramatic panic. A pair of guards atop the wall simply stared, unmoving, as the golem continued its steady, thunderous approach toward the main gate.

Queen Marianne, who had rushed out onto the palace's front steps the moment the alarm bells began ringing, took one long, disbelieving look at the sight of her three missing daughters casually riding a mountain-sized golem directly toward her front gate — and promptly fainted dead away, collapsing gracefully into the arms of two nearby, thoroughly startled maids, who immediately dissolved into their own frantic scramble to revive her.

Even Grayer, watching the whole spectacle unfold from across the training yard, allowed himself a rare, genuinely impressed whistle. Lysander, arriving at Celestine's side just in time to catch the full scene, let out the longest, deepest sigh of his entire seventeen years of life, and Celestine, ever gentle, simply patted his back soothingly, murmuring, "There, there," with obvious, barely-contained amusement of her own.

Caius, for his part, threw both fists into the air with unrestrained, delighted glee.

"THOSE ARE MY SISTERS RIGHT THERE!" he bellowed, loud enough to be heard clear across the courtyard. "WOOOOOOHOOOOOO! LOOK AT THAT THING! THAT'S AWESOME!"

And Sera — watching it all unfold from the sidelines with unrestrained, genuine delight, clapping both hands together in open, gleeful applause — simply laughed, long and loud, thoroughly satisfied with what was, without question, the single best entertainment she'd witnessed in years. Even her anaconda seemed to share the sentiment, head lifting from her shoulders to regard the approaching spectacle with something that might, in a snake, have passed for amusement.

"Told you they'd make it back in one piece," she said to Grayer, grinning.

"You didn't tell me they'd come back riding a GOLEM."

"Details."

As the massive construct finally came to a slow, deliberate stop just outside the main gate, kneeling with surprising grace to allow its three passengers to climb safely down, the triplets exchanged a quick, satisfied glance among themselves — bruised, weathered, considerably richer in both gear and experience than they'd been three weeks prior, and, despite everything, thoroughly, unmistakably pleased with how their journey had turned out.

"Well," Elliana said, grinning wide as she hopped down into the courtyard, already bracing herself for the inevitable, thoroughly deserved lecture waiting on the other side of the gate, "we're home."

"We are going to be in SO much trouble," Lilia said, though she was smiling too, despite herself.

"Worth it," Rosaria said simply, adjusting her glasses with a small, genuinely satisfied smile of her own, Seraphine's massive shadow settling protectively over all three of them as the palace gates swung open at last.

And with that, laughing, battered, gloriously alive, and trailing chaos behind them exactly as they always had, the three princesses of Valoria made their grand, thoroughly unforgettable return to the kingdom they called home.

• • •

It took the better part of an hour for the palace to fully recover from the shock of their arrival — Queen Marianne revived, eventually, by a combination of smelling salts and Aldric's rather sheepish insistence that everyone was, in fact, alive and unharmed — and considerably longer still for the resulting lecture to finally run its course.

"THREE WEEKS," Marianne said, pacing back and forth in front of all three of her daughters, who stood in a neat, slightly bedraggled row in the throne room, trying their best to look appropriately contrite. "THREE WEEKS, with NO WORD, NO WARNING, NOTHING but a story about a training trip that I now understand was a COMPLETE FABRICATION—"

"In our defense," Elliana started.

"THERE IS NO DEFENSE."

"...Fair."

"You could have DIED. Do you understand that? Do any of you understand that? Bandits, wyverns, a GOLEM, apparently, if the guards' reports of a massive construct dragging itself down the mountain roads behind you are to be believed—"

"It walked on its own, actually," Rosaria offered. "We didn't drag it."

"THAT IS NOT THE POINT, ROSARIA."

"Noted."

King Aldric, seated on his throne beside his still-furious wife, took a slow, measured sip of his tea, watching the proceedings with the particular, weary calm of a man who had, over the preceding week, largely exhausted his own capacity for outrage and settled instead into simple, profound relief that his daughters had returned in one piece.

"For what it's worth," he said, once Marianne had paused for breath, "they did come back with rather impressive new equipment. And apparently cleared a bandit gang, resolved a trafficking ring, and dealt with an engineered golem that had been disrupting trade routes near the mountains. That's a fairly productive three weeks, all things considered."

"ALDRIC."

"I'm simply providing context."

"YOU ARE NOT HELPING."

Lilia, watching her parents' familiar back-and-forth with a small, tired smile, found the courage to speak up. "Mother," she said, "I know we scared you. That wasn't fair to you, and I'm genuinely sorry. But I don't regret going. I learned more about myself in these three weeks than I have in the last five years combined."

Marianne's fury faltered, just slightly, at the quiet sincerity in her daughter's voice.

Rosaria stepped forward as well. "I have a construct now," she said. "A proper one, bound to my own mana, built with dwarven craftsmanship. I couldn't have gotten that opportunity any other way. I'm sorry for the worry we caused you. But I'm not sorry we went."

Elliana, last of the three, simply grinned — softer than her usual bravado, but no less genuine. "And I got my bow," she said, patting the sleek white weapon slung across her back. "Plus a story I'm going to be dining out on for YEARS. Worth every single lecture coming my way."

Marianne stared at all three of her daughters for a long, silent moment — exhausted, furious, and, underneath it all, undeniably, achingly proud — before finally letting out a long, shuddering breath and closing the distance to pull all three of them into a fierce, crushing hug.

"I am still furious with all of you," she said, voice muffled against Lilia's shoulder. "This conversation is NOT over. There WILL be consequences. But I am so, so glad you're home."

"We know," Elliana said, hugging her back just as tightly. "We're glad too."

Behind them, Caius let out another whoop of delight, Lysander finally allowed himself a genuine, relieved smile, and Sera, watching from the back of the throne room with quiet, satisfied pride, simply smiled to herself and turned to make her way back toward her tower, already looking forward to hearing the full story — every last, chaotic detail of it — from her favorite student.

The kingdom of Valoria, it was safe to say, had never quite been the same since the day Princess Elliana was born.

It was, everyone privately agreed, considerably better for it.
