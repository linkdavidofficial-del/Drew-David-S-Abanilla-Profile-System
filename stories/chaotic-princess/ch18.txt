Chapter 18: HIGH STAKES, LOW STANDARDS

The village of Ashwick Hollow was not, by any reasonable definition, a large settlement — a modest cluster of timber buildings arranged around a central well, a handful of farms spreading out along the surrounding hills, and a single, slightly lopsided tavern that appeared, from the outside, to be the settlement's undisputed social center.

It was, after four days of camping, foraging, bandit-adjacent violence, and one deeply traumatic zombie encounter, an absolutely magnificent sight.

"A real BED," Lilia breathed, staring at the village like a woman gazing upon paradise. "An actual roof. Walls that aren't made of canvas."

"Don't get your hopes up," Rosaria warned, though even she couldn't quite keep the relief out of her own voice. "It's a farming village. I doubt the accommodations will be luxurious."

"At this point I would sleep in a barn and consider it a five-star establishment."

Elliana, meanwhile, had already dismounted, handed Sparky's reins off to a bewildered stablehand with a cheerful "he's very friendly, mostly, just don't make direct eye contact for too long," and was making a beeline directly for the tavern with the single-minded focus of a woman on an important mission.

"Where are you going?" Rosaria called after her.

"Recon!" Elliana called back, without slowing.

"That is NOT what recon means, and I don't believe you for a SECOND—"

The inside of the tavern was warm, dim, and smelled pleasantly of woodsmoke and roasting meat — a welcome change from four days of trail rations — and it took Elliana approximately ninety seconds to locate exactly what she'd been hoping to find: a round table near the back, occupied by four grizzled, weathered men hunched over a game of cards, a modest pile of coin sitting in the center of the table.

"Ooh," she said, delighted, pulling up a stool uninvited. "Poker. I know this one."

The men glanced up, took in the sight of a small girl in fur-trimmed traveling leathers plopping herself down at their table with an axe strapped across her back, and exchanged a round of amused, faintly condescending looks.

"This ain't a game for children, little miss," one of them said, not unkindly, already reaching to gather the cards back up.

"I've got money," Elliana said, producing a small, respectable pouch of coin from her belt with the confident air of a woman who had absolutely done this before, in a different life, across a considerably different kind of table, with considerably higher stakes than farmhand pocket change. "And I know the rules. Deal me in."

What followed over the next hour could only be described, from the perspective of the four increasingly baffled farmhands, as an unmitigated massacre.

It was not that Elliana was cheating, exactly — though her ability to read the faintest flicker of hesitation in an opponent's shoulders had been honed over twenty-eight years of corporate meetings where reading a room correctly had, on more than one occasion, been the difference between keeping her job and not. It was simply that she was, without question, considerably better at this than four men who played the same weekly game against the same four opponents and had never once had their assumptions meaningfully challenged.

By the time Rosaria and Lilia finally tracked her down — having secured rooms at the village's single modest inn and grown suspicious of Elliana's extended absence — the pile of coin in front of her had grown to a size that had drawn a small, murmuring crowd of onlookers, and the four original players had been quietly replaced, one by one, by increasingly desperate challengers hoping to win back what their neighbors had lost.

"Elliana," Rosaria said, arriving at the table with an expression of pure, weary disbelief, "what, precisely, are you doing?"

"Winning," Elliana said cheerfully, laying down a hand that made the entire table groan in unison. "It's very fun. You should try it sometime. Loosen up a little."

"We are supposed to be RESTING. Not running an illegal gambling operation in the back of a rural tavern."

"It's not illegal, it's a friendly game—"

"It stopped being friendly approximately four rounds ago," Lilia muttered, eyeing the increasingly hostile stares from the surrounding crowd.

The trouble, as it turned out, arrived not from any of the actual players — who, to their credit, seemed to be taking their losses with a kind of grim, resigned good humor — but from the tavern's owner, a broad, oily-smiled man who had been watching the entire proceedings from behind the bar with a growing, calculating interest that had nothing to do with the money on the table.

"That's quite a haul you've got there, little miss," he said, sidling over once the current hand had finished, wiping his hands on a rag that did very little to actually clean them. "Traveling alone, are you? You and your two friends?"

"We're fine," Elliana said, not looking up from shuffling the deck for the next hand, some old, familiar instinct already prickling at the back of her neck.

"Course you are," the man said, smile widening in a way that made Rosaria's skin crawl. "Course you are. Just — three young girls, this far from anywhere, carrying that much coin. Seems like the kind of thing a man ought to look out for, don't it? Make sure nothing... unfortunate happens to you."

Elliana's eyes flicked up, slow and sharp.

"That an offer," she said, "or a threat?"

The man's smile didn't waver. "Just concerned hospitality, little miss."

It took approximately four more hours — and one extremely ill-advised attempt, well after dark, by the tavern owner and a half-dozen hired men to corner all three sisters in the alley behind the inn and "escort" them to a waiting cart — for the true nature of that concerned hospitality to become fully apparent.

"You've GOT to be kidding me," Lilia said, staring at the small, terrified cluster of captives already chained inside the cart's hidden compartment — three other travelers, none older than fifteen, all wide-eyed with the particular, hollowed-out fear of people who had already given up hope of rescue. "This is a TRAFFICKING RING. In a FARMING VILLAGE."

"Small settlements make for easy pickings," Rosaria said grimly, already summoning frost to her fingertips. "Isolated. Little oversight. Nobody watching too closely."

"Well," Elliana said, cracking her knuckles with an expression that had gone, very suddenly, considerably less playful, "good thing we happened to be watching closely tonight."

The fight that followed was short, brutal, and left the tavern owner and every single one of his hired men considerably worse for wear, sprawled unconscious across the alley and the yard beyond by the time the last of them stopped struggling. Lilia, in particular, had thrown herself into the confrontation with a ferocity that surprised even her sisters — every ounce of her earlier discomfort about the zombies channeled instead into something sharp and righteous and entirely without mercy for men who trafficked children for profit.

It was in the immediate aftermath — captives freed, chains broken, the tavern owner and his men trussed up and left for whatever passed for local authority in a village this size — that Lilia, adrenaline still roaring through her system and patience for the situation having entirely evaporated, rounded on the small, frightened crowd of villagers who had gathered at the edge of the commotion.

"You KNEW about this, didn't you?" she demanded, voice ringing out across the square, sword still in hand, hero's mark glowing faintly at the base of her neck. "Some of you had to know! People don't just vanish from a village this size without SOMEONE noticing! I don't care if you were afraid of him, I don't care what excuse you've got — nobody, ANYONE, deserves to be sold off like cattle, and if this happens again, if I hear ONE WORD that this village let something like this slide because it was easier than standing up to one greedy, disgusting man, I will hear about it, and I will come back!"

It was not, by her own later admission, a planned speech. It had simply poured out of her, fueled by exhaustion and adrenaline and four straight days of watching her sisters fight things twice their size without flinching, and by the time she'd finished, the entire village square had gone dead silent.

Then an elderly woman near the front — the village elder, as it turned out — stepped forward slowly, eyes fixed not on Lilia's face but on the faintly glowing mark at the base of her neck, and sank, with visible difficulty, to her knees.

"The Heroine's Mark," she breathed. "Gods above. A true hero, here, in our own square."

One by one, the gathered villagers followed her down, kneeling in the dirt with expressions of dawning, disbelieving awe.

Lilia went bright red from the collar up.

"You — you don't have to — please stand up, this is really unnecessary—"

"You freed our children," the elder said, voice trembling. "Three of those captives were from THIS village, Your Highness. We knew something was wrong. We suspected. But we had no proof, and no strength to move against him ourselves. You've done what none of us could."

"I just — I got annoyed," Lilia admitted, mortified. "That's really all it was."

"An admirable form of annoyance," Rosaria murmured, fighting valiantly and unsuccessfully to keep a straight face.

Elliana, watching her sister's ears turn an even deeper shade of red, decided — with the efficient, opportunistic instincts of a woman who never let a useful moment go to waste — that this was, in fact, an excellent time to pivot the conversation toward something considerably more practical.

"Elder," she said, stepping forward with her most disarming smile, "while we've got you — we're actually traveling toward the mountains. The dwarven kingdom, specifically. Would you happen to know of any shortcuts through this region? We're on something of a schedule."

The elder, still visibly overwhelmed by the events of the evening, nodded slowly, pointing a weathered hand toward the jagged silhouette of peaks visible even at this hour against the night sky. "There's an old miner's trail," she said. "Cuts through the eastern ridge, shaves the better part of two days off the main road, though it's steep, and not well maintained. Follow the river north until it forks, then keep to the left."

"Perfect," Elliana said, already committing the directions to memory. "Thank you. Genuinely. And, for what it's worth — you're welcome. For the whole trafficking ring thing."

The elder managed a tired, grateful smile. "Should you ever pass this way again, Your Highnesses, you will always find a warm welcome in Ashwick Hollow."

They left the following morning, well-rested for the first time in days, considerably richer thanks to Elliana's card game winnings, and armed with a genuine shortcut that would, according to Rosaria's calculations, save them the better part of two full days.

"You know," Lilia said, as the village shrank behind them, "for a day that started with you starting an illegal gambling ring, that actually turned out pretty well."

"Everything I do turns out well eventually," Elliana said serenely. "You just have to trust the process."

"The process was almost getting sold into human trafficking."

"Details."

Rosaria, riding along in thoughtful silence, allowed herself a small, private smile. Whatever chaos Elliana dragged them through — and there had, admittedly, been rather a lot of it in the past four days alone — there was, undeniably, a strange, lucky sort of momentum to it. Trouble found them, certainly. But somehow, every single time, they came out the other side of it having done something genuinely good, whether or not that had ever actually been the plan.

She made a note of it in her travel log that evening, right alongside the shortcut's directions, and did not mention it to either of her sisters, mostly because she suspected Elliana would be thoroughly, insufferably smug about it if she ever found out.

• • •

That evening's camp, made a comfortable distance outside Ashwick Hollow's boundaries, carried a noticeably lighter mood than any of the previous nights on the road — full stomachs courtesy of the village's grateful, if still slightly overwhelmed, hospitality, a genuine shortcut ahead of them, and, for once, no immediate threat looming on the horizon.

"I keep thinking about that elder," Lilia said, poking absently at the campfire with a stick, still visibly turning the events of the day over in her mind. "The way she looked at me. Like I was — I don't know. Something out of a story."

"You ARE something out of a story," Elliana pointed out. "Divine mark and everything. Kind of comes with the territory."

"I know, logically. It's just strange, actually experiencing it. Back home, everyone already knew me my whole life. I was just Lilia. Here, I said a handful of sentences and an entire village was ready to build me a shrine."

"Get used to it," Rosaria said, not unkindly, glancing up from her travel log. "That mark on your neck isn't going to stop meaning something just because we're a long way from home. If anything, I suspect it's going to matter considerably MORE, the further we travel. Fewer people who already know you as simply their neighbor's daughter. More people who only know the legend."

Lilia considered this with a small, thoughtful frown. "That's a strange thing to have to get used to."

"Welcome to being the chosen one," Elliana said cheerfully. "Free room and board wherever you go, occasional spontaneous kneeling, and the crushing weight of everyone's expectations resting permanently on your shoulders. It's a great deal, really."

"You make it sound WONDERFUL."

"I'm an optimist."

"You are the single least optimistic person I have ever met."

"I'm optimistic about THINGS I CARE ABOUT. Chaos. Boars. The eventual downfall of Duke Remington's dignity. Very selective optimism."

Lilia laughed despite herself, some of the day's lingering strangeness easing at the familiar, absurd rhythm of her sister's teasing.

"Can I ask you something else, though?" she said, once the laughter had faded. "Rosaria — has this changed anything, for you? About wanting to see the world someday? Or has it just confirmed every single worry you had about leaving the palace walls in the first place?"

Rosaria considered the question with real seriousness, setting her quill down against her open travel log. "Both, if I'm honest," she said. "I've been more frightened this past week than at any other point in my life. Genuinely, viscerally frightened, more than once. But I've also learned things no amount of reading in the Academy archives could have ever taught me. Theory is one thing. Actually casting a working spell under real pressure, with real consequences if I fail, is something else entirely. I don't think I understood the difference until now."

"So — worth it?" Lilia pressed.

"Ask me again once we've survived the mountain," Rosaria said, echoing her sister's earlier answer with a small, wry smile of her own.

"That's fair," Lilia admitted, laughing softly. "Consistent, at least."

"Consistency is underrated," Rosaria said primly, picking her quill back up. "I intend to remain consistently correct about the mountain being a terrible idea, right up until it either proves me wrong or proves me right."

"Optimistic phrasing."

"I contain multitudes," Rosaria said, deadpan, and this time it was Lilia who dissolved into laughter, loud enough that Elliana glanced over from where she was banking the fire, grinning at the sight of her two sisters finally, thoroughly enjoying themselves despite everything.

"You two are getting suspiciously good at borrowing my lines," Elliana called over. "I demand royalties."

"Absolutely not," Rosaria said. "It entered the public domain the moment you said it for the fortieth time."

"That's not how catchphrases work."

"It's exactly how catchphrases work. Overuse forfeits ownership."

"I'm telling Mother you're stealing my intellectual property."

"Please do. I'm sure she'll be thrilled to hear we've moved on from illegal gambling to trademark disputes."

Lilia, still laughing, buried her face in her hands, and even Rosaria allowed herself a small, satisfied smile at having successfully, if only briefly, gotten the last word.

She glanced skyward, where the first scattered stars had begun to emerge against the deepening blue, and let out a slow, contented breath, the tavern's earlier chaos and the alley confrontation that followed already settling into something more manageable in her memory — not forgotten, exactly, but no longer sitting quite so heavy in her chest as it had an hour ago.

"You know," she said, "I think I understand now why you like this so much. The traveling, I mean. Not just the fighting — the actual moving, seeing new places, meeting people who have no idea who we are until we tell them."

Elliana glanced over, genuinely pleased by the observation. "Yeah," she said, quieter than usual. "That's exactly it, actually. Back home, everyone already has an idea of who you're supposed to be before you've even said a word. Out here, you get to just... be whoever you actually are, in the moment. Nobody's already decided the story before you've had a chance to tell it yourself."

Rosaria, listening quietly from where she sat cross-legged near the fire, found herself surprised by the genuine weight behind her sister's words — a rare glimpse past the constant, performative chaos into something considerably more thoughtful underneath.

"I didn't expect to agree with you about anything regarding this trip," she admitted, "but I think I understand that, at least."

"See?" Elliana grinned, the earlier weight already melting back into her usual easy humor. "Sister bonding. Told you it'd be good for us."

"You told us that RIGHT before nearly getting us killed by a pack of wyverns."

"That hasn't happened yet. You're getting ahead of the story."

"I have a bad feeling about it regardless."

"You always have a bad feeling. It's part of your charm."

The fire crackled on into the deepening night, and the three of them settled in for what would prove to be one of their easier, more peaceful rests along the entire journey — a small, quiet mercy, given everything still waiting for them further up the road.
