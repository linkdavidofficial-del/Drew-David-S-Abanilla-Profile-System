Chapter 19: SWAMP THING

The miner's shortcut, as promised, cut a solid two days off their journey — right up until it deposited them squarely at the edge of a low, misty swampland that had not, as far as Rosaria's map was concerned, been marked on any chart she'd consulted before setting out.

"This isn't on the map," she said, frowning down at the parchment, then back up at the murky expanse of standing water and twisted, moss-draped trees stretching out ahead of them.

"Maps get outdated," Elliana said with a shrug. "Swamps happen. Rivers change course. It's fine, we'll just cut around the edge."

It was, in retrospect, an entirely reasonable plan that lasted almost exactly as long as it took for the first low, rumbling growl to echo out from somewhere within the mist.

"That didn't sound like a swamp noise," Lilia said, going very still.

"No," Rosaria agreed, equally still, "it did not."

The manticore emerged from the tree line without warning — a creature the size of a small horse, lion-bodied and bat-winged, its face disturbingly, unsettlingly human beneath a mane of matted fur, a long, barbed scorpion's tail arching high over its back and bristling with what were unmistakably venom-tipped spines. It regarded the three of them with an expression of calm, territorial hostility, and let out a second, considerably louder roar that sent a flock of startled birds exploding out of the surrounding canopy.

"Okay," Elliana said, already drawing her sword, "so that's a manticore. That's manticore territory, apparently. We're standing in manticore territory."

"Can we NOT be standing in manticore territory," Lilia said, already backing up a step.

"Kind of complicated at this point, given that it's currently looking directly at us."

The manticore lunged.

What followed was, by unanimous later agreement among all three sisters, one of the more genuinely difficult fights of the entire journey — not because the creature was faster or stronger than anything they'd faced so far, though it was certainly both of those things, but because the swampy, waterlogged terrain made every step a liability, boots sinking into soft mud, footing shifting unpredictably beneath every strike and dodge.

It was, ironically, that same treacherous terrain that ended up working entirely in their favor.

"The water!" Rosaria shouted, already raising both hands as the manticore's tail whipped past close enough to nearly clip Lilia's shoulder, sending up a spray of muddy water in its wake. "Elliana! The swamp is soaked through, it'll conduct—"

"Already on it!" Elliana was grinning now, wide and sharp, backing toward the deepest, muddiest stretch of standing water she could find, dragging the manticore's attention along with her. "Sparky! Zeus! Hera! LIGHT IT UP!"

Sparky surged forward into the shallow water with a low, delighted rumble, and the resulting discharge of electricity spread outward through the swamp in a rippling, crackling wave, catching the manticore mid-lunge and sending it seizing and screeching sideways into the mud. Zeus and Hera dove in tandem moments later, wings crackling with their own stored static, adding fresh jolts to the already electrified water with every pass.

"Now, Rosaria!" Elliana called.

Rosaria didn't need to be told twice. Both hands rose, mana gathering thick and visible around her fingertips, dark clouds bunching unnaturally overhead in direct defiance of the otherwise clear morning sky.

"Thunderstorm," she muttered, voice tight with concentration, and the sky above the swamp cracked open.

Lightning speared down in three successive strikes, each one finding the already-soaked, already-conductive water with unerring precision, the combined force of Rosaria's storm and Elliana's electric menagerie turning the entire swamp into a churning, crackling deathtrap for anything unfortunate enough to be standing in it.

The manticore, caught in the thick of it, thrashed and roared and, against every reasonable expectation, kept fighting — driven by pure animal fury and territorial rage, lunging blindly through the crackling water toward whatever target presented itself closest.

Which, more often than not, turned out to be Lilia.

"WHY IS IT ALWAYS ME," Lilia shouted, ducking under a wild swipe of one massive paw, rolling through the mud and coming back up in time to catch a venomous tail-strike on the flat of her blade, the impact hard enough to send her skidding backward a full three feet. "I am NOT the one electrocuting it, why does it keep coming for ME—"

"You're closest!" Elliana called back, unhelpfully, ducking around the manticore's flank to land a solid slash across its haunches. "Also you keep yelling! It probably finds you the most annoying!"

"THAT IS NOT HELPFUL INFORMATION RIGHT NOW—"

Lilia, through sheer stubborn persistence and a growing collection of bruises, absorbed hit after hit that would have felled a lesser combatant outright — the manticore's claws raking twice across her armored shoulder, its tail catching her a glancing blow across the ribs that left her wheezing but upright — buying, with every impact she weathered, precious seconds for Rosaria's spell to gather its final, devastating charge.

"One more!" Rosaria called, voice straining now with the effort. "Just — hold it there ONE MORE MOMENT—"

"EASY FOR YOU TO SAY—"

The final lightning strike, when it landed, struck the manticore square between its shoulders, arcing through water and fur and flesh alike in a blinding flash that left afterimages burned into all three sisters' vision. The creature convulsed once, twice, let out a final, strangled roar, and collapsed heavily into the mud, smoke curling faintly off its singed fur.

Silence fell over the swamp, broken only by the receding rumble of thunder and the ragged, exhausted breathing of three very tired, very muddy princesses.

"We got it," Lilia said, staring down at the fallen creature with something between disbelief and relief. "We actually got it."

"Of course we did," Elliana said, sheathing her sword and surveying the fallen manticore with visible, dawning interest. She crouched down beside it, running one hand appreciatively through the singed, matted fur along its flank. "Shame, though. This could've made a great new pet for my collection. Imagine the look on Duke Remington's face if I brought THIS one home."

"No," Rosaria said immediately, still catching her breath, mud dripping steadily off the hem of her robe. "Absolutely not. You are NOT getting a new pet. Sparky, Zeus, and Hera are already more than enough of a menagerie for one princess to responsibly manage, and I use the word 'responsibly' extremely loosely given the current circumstances. Three is enough. Let's go."

"But the fur!" Elliana protested, still stroking it fondly. "Manticore pelts sell for an absolute fortune, Rosaria, we're talking serious money, and the venom glands alone could probably fund an entire—"

"We are PRINCESSES," Rosaria said, cutting her off with the flat, exhausted finality of a woman who had simply run out of patience for this particular conversation. "We have plenty of money. We do not need to harvest a dead manticore for spare income. We need to get OUT of this swamp, get CLEAN, and get moving before something else decides to wander over and investigate all the noise we just made."

Elliana sighed, casting one last, longing glance at the fallen creature, before reluctantly rising and wiping the worst of the mud from her hands.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm at least taking a few of the tail spines. Those are genuinely useful for alchemy, and I refuse to let that go to waste on principle."

"Five minutes," Rosaria said. "Then we are LEAVING."

"Deal."

Lilia, meanwhile, had sunk down onto a nearby fallen log, sword resting across her knees, surveying the sheer volume of fresh bruises already blooming across her arms and ribs with a kind of tired, philosophical resignation.

"You know," she said, to no one in particular, "I used to think being the Heroine meant glorious duels and noble quests. Not getting repeatedly clawed by an oversized lion-scorpion-man-thing in a swamp because my sister decided it was faster to cut through 'unmapped territory.'"

"Character building," Elliana called over her shoulder, already busy harvesting spines with an efficiency that suggested considerable prior practice at exactly this sort of task.

"I've BUILT enough character for a lifetime at this point."

"There's always more character to build."

Lilia groaned, flopping backward against the log, and stared up at the swamp canopy overhead, where the storm clouds Rosaria had summoned were already beginning to thin and scatter, sunlight breaking back through in soft, hazy shafts.

"Two more weeks of this," she muttered. "At least two more weeks."

"Probably longer," Rosaria said unhelpfully, already wringing mud out of the hem of her robe with visible distaste, "given our current pace and Elliana's apparent inability to resist a single roadside distraction."

"That's fair," Elliana called back, entirely unbothered, tucking the last of the harvested spines carefully into a padded pouch. "But hey — look on the bright side. At least it's never boring."

"I would," Lilia said, with real, heartfelt sincerity, "genuinely enjoy some boring right about now."

"You say that now. You'll miss it once we're back to palace lessons and etiquette tutors."

Lilia opened her mouth to argue, considered the accuracy of that particular statement for a long moment, and then, finding herself unable to actually dispute it, simply closed her mouth again and sighed instead.

They pressed on within the hour, mud-caked, bruised, and considerably richer in manticore spines than any of them had anticipated when they'd woken that morning, cutting a wide, cautious berth around the rest of the swamp's murky expanse as they angled back toward the mountains rising ever closer on the horizon.

Behind them, the fallen manticore sank slowly into the mud, and the swamp settled back into its usual, undisturbed quiet — at least until the next unlucky travelers happened to wander through.

• • •

It took the better part of the afternoon to fully clear the swamp's boundary, and by the time solid, dry ground finally reasserted itself beneath their feet, all three sisters were thoroughly, uniformly exhausted — caked in drying mud from head to toe, nursing a fresh collection of bruises and shallow cuts, and, in Lilia's case, sporting a truly spectacular purpling welt along one shoulder where the manticore's tail had connected.

"I need a bath," Lilia announced, examining a particularly stubborn smear of mud on her forearm with visible despair. "An actual bath. With actual soap. I don't think I've been properly clean since we left the palace."

"There's a stream up ahead, according to the map," Rosaria offered, consulting her chart. "Not quite a bath, but considerably cleaner than what we're currently working with."

"I'll take it. I'll take ANYTHING that isn't swamp water."

They found the stream within the hour — clear, cold, and running fast enough over smooth river stones that it carried away the worst of the mud almost immediately once they waded in. Lilia let out a long, blissful sigh the moment the cold water hit her aching shoulder, easing some of the day's accumulated tension.

"Better?" Elliana asked, scrubbing at a stubborn patch of dried mud along her own forearm.

"Immeasurably." Lilia tilted her head back, letting the current wash over her properly. "You know, for someone who apparently used to hunt for FUN in her old life, you seem to be handling actual danger a lot more casually than the rest of us."

"I mean, boars don't have venomous tails or the ability to fly," Elliana pointed out. "Scale matters. But also, yeah — I think a decade of chasing increasingly dangerous things around a forest for sport probably calibrated my nerves a bit differently than yours."

"Must be nice," Lilia said, only half-joking, "having nerves that don't panic."

"They panic," Elliana admitted, more honestly than her usual bravado allowed. "Just after, generally, instead of during. I've gotten pretty good at shelving it until there's actually time to deal with it. Doesn't mean it's not there."

Rosaria, wringing mud from the hem of her robe a short distance downstream, glanced over with quiet interest. "That's actually a remarkably mature approach to fear management," she said. "Most people your age — most people any age, honestly — struggle considerably more with separating the reaction from the response."

"Twenty-eight years of corporate deadlines will do that to a person," Elliana said, then paused, seeming to catch herself. "...Metaphorically speaking."

Both her sisters gave her identical, faintly puzzled looks, clearly sensing there was more to that particular comment than she was willing to elaborate on, but — as had become something of a running pattern over the course of the trip — neither one pushed the matter further. There were, they had both learned by now, certain doors in Elliana's past that simply didn't open, no matter how curious either of them privately remained about what lay behind them.

"Anyway," Elliana said, deliberately steering the conversation elsewhere, "we should get moving again soon. I'd like to put a bit more distance behind us before we lose the light."

"Agreed," Rosaria said, letting the earlier moment pass without comment, though she filed it away carefully all the same, the way she filed away most things about her sister that didn't quite add up.

They dried off as best they could in the fading afternoon sun, and by the time they set off again, mud-free and considerably more comfortable, the mountains loomed noticeably closer on the horizon than they had that morning — a steady, encouraging reminder that, for all the detours and delays, they really were getting closer to their destination with every single day.

• • •

They made camp that night on a low, dry rise well clear of the swamp's edge, and for the first time in what felt like days, dinner passed without any looming threat interrupting the meal.

"So," Lilia said, poking at the fire with a stick while she picked contentedly at a strip of dried meat, "manticore fur. How much would that actually have sold for, hypothetically?"

Elliana brightened instantly. "A GOOD pelt, properly cured? Easily a few hundred gold on its own. Manticore hide's prized for armor lining — naturally resistant to poison, which is ironic given what usually kills the manticore in the first place. The venom glands alone would've fetched even more from any halfway competent alchemist."

"You really did think all of that through in the middle of a life-or-death fight."

"Multitasking is a skill," Elliana said, entirely serious. "You'd be amazed what you can plan out while simultaneously dodging a venomous stinger."

"I would NOT be amazed, actually, because I was there, and I distinctly remember you nearly losing an arm to that exact stinger about thirty seconds before you started planning your manticore fur business."

"I recovered gracefully."

"You got flung into a tree."

"Gracefully."

Rosaria, listening to the exchange with quiet amusement, finally looked up from her own dinner. "For what it's worth," she said, "I did actually consider keeping a sample of the venom glands. For research purposes. Purely academic."

Both her sisters turned to stare at her.

"You WHAT," Lilia said.

"I decided against it," Rosaria added quickly, "given that we were fleeing the immediate area at the time and I had rather more pressing concerns than sample collection. But I want it noted that the impulse was there."

"I feel like I'm surrounded by menaces," Lilia said, with real, heartfelt despair. "Both of you. Constantly calculating profit margins and research opportunities in the middle of mortal combat."

"You're the one who gets to be a legendary hero someday," Elliana pointed out. "We have to make our money somehow."

"That is NOT how heroism works—"

"It's exactly how heroism works. Ask any adventurer. Glory doesn't pay for new swords."

Lilia opened her mouth to argue further, found she genuinely didn't have a good counterpoint, and settled instead for a long, put-upon sigh, shaking her head slowly at both her sisters.

"For the record," she added, after a moment, "I'm keeping a tally. Of all the things you two have wanted to loot, harvest, or otherwise monetize off of something that was actively trying to kill us. I think we're at four so far."

"Five," Rosaria corrected. "You're forgetting the bandits' coin purses."

"That doesn't count, that's just standard looting."

"Looting is looting, Lilia. I don't see why the venom glands are held to a different moral standard than the coin purses."

"Because coin purses don't require you to get within arm's reach of a venomous stinger to collect them!"

"A fair distinction," Rosaria conceded, "though I maintain my point about the tally."

"Five, then." Lilia sighed again, though this time there was a genuine, fond smile tugging at the corner of her mouth. "I'm surrounded by menaces."

"You've said that already," Elliana pointed out.

"It bears repeating."

The fire crackled on into the gathering evening, the mountains looming ever closer against the darkening sky.

"Six," Rosaria added, just before Lilia finally drifted off. "I forgot the harvested tail spines."

"GO TO SLEEP, ROSARIA."

"I'm simply keeping the record accurate."

The three of them settled into their bedrolls not long after, already anticipating whatever fresh chaos the following day's travel would inevitably bring.

Above them, the sky had cleared enough to reveal a wide, unbroken scattering of stars, considerably brighter out here than anything visible from the palace's well-lit courtyards, and for a long moment before sleep finally claimed her, Lilia found herself simply staring up at them, marveling quietly at how much bigger the world felt out here than it ever had within the familiar boundaries of home.

It was, she decided, a fair trade for the bruises.

Mostly.

Beside her, she could hear Rosaria's breathing already evening out into sleep, and further off, Elliana's quiet, contented murmuring to Sparky as she settled him in for the night. Whatever tomorrow brought, Lilia thought, drifting off at last, at least she wouldn't be facing it alone.
