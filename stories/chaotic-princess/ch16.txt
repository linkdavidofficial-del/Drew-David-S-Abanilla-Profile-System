Chapter 16: THE SCENIC ROUTE, ALLEGEDLY

Morning broke cold and clear over the edge of the Forbidden Forest, and by the time the three princesses had broken camp, rolled their bedrolls, and argued their way through a breakfast of dried meat and traveling bread, the sun had already climbed well past the treetops.

"According to the map," Rosaria announced, holding a hand-drawn chart at arm's length with the brisk authority of a woman who had spent the previous evening cross-referencing three separate historical travel logs by candlelight, "the most efficient route follows the old trade road along the river, then cuts north through the lower foothills before the terrain steepens. It adds perhaps half a day compared to a direct line through the forest, but it avoids the deep woodland entirely, which — given everything we know about what tends to live in deep woodland — seems like the reasonable choice."

"Sounds boring," Elliana said, already climbing up onto Sparky's back.

"It sounds SAFE," Rosaria corrected. "There is a meaningful difference. I would like us to arrive at the dwarven kingdom with our full complement of limbs still attached."

"Fine, fine. Trade road it is. You're the navigator."

It was, in fairness, a perfectly reasonable plan. It accounted for terrain, water access, and the general likelihood of encountering anything more dangerous than the occasional wild dog. Rosaria had even factored in the reduced visibility near the riverbank and adjusted their planned pace accordingly. By any rational measure, it was the smart route.

Three hours later, the smart route delivered them directly into an ambush.

It happened fast — the kind of fast that left no time for anything resembling a strategy session. One moment the road was quiet, hemmed in on both sides by scrubby brush and the occasional cluster of boulders left over from some long-ago rockslide; the next, a low, sharp whistle cut through the air, and no fewer than a dozen men rose up from the brush on either side of the road, weapons already drawn, cutting off the path both ahead and behind.

"Well," Elliana said, drawing her sword with an expression of mild, almost pleasant interest, "that's new."

"THIS IS YOUR FAULT!" Lilia hissed, already backing up against Sparky's flank, sword coming up into a defensive stance.

"MY fault?!" Rosaria's voice climbed sharply, robes swirling as she spun to face her sister, one hand already glowing faintly with the beginnings of a spell. "I was trying to make this EASIER for us! I picked the SAFE route!"

"Well, congratulations, the safe route has BANDITS in it!"

Elliana, watching this exchange with the delighted, unbothered calm of a woman who had genuinely not expected her morning to include this particular flavor of chaos but was more than happy to accommodate it, tilted her head thoughtfully.

"Well, which is better," she mused aloud, entirely too cheerful for the circumstances, "the forest, with a considerably worse chance of running into a wyvern or a griffin nest, or a nice, friendly, straightforward road robbery?"

"You know what?" Lilia snapped, already shifting her weight forward onto the balls of her feet. "Bandits are BETTER. I'll take the bandits."

The men surrounding them, who had until this point been enjoying the private, self-congratulatory smugness of a well-executed ambush, seemed to take a moment to actually register what they were looking at — three girls, none of them older than ten by the look of it, one riding what appeared to be a fully grown Electro Crocodile, with two enormous birds circling lazily overhead.

The one who appeared to be in charge — a broad, scarred man with a rusted cleaver strapped across his back — barked out a short, ugly laugh.

"Check out these brats," he said, grinning around at his men. "Lost, are we? Wandered a little far from home?"

"Get them," another voice called from further down the line, considerably more enthusiastic about the prospect. "Kids like that'll fetch good coin down south. Especially the pretty ones."

A third man — younger, leaner, with an expression that made Rosaria's skin crawl even from thirty feet away — licked his lips slowly, eyes tracking over all three of them with an appraising, entirely unwelcome interest.

"They look fresh," he said.

Rosaria's eye twitched, visibly, the temperature around her fingertips dropping several degrees in an instant. "Disgusting," she said, flat and cold.

"That's life, sister," Elliana said cheerfully, already swinging down off Sparky's back with her sword in hand. "Some people are just garbage. Good thing we're about to take out the trash."

The fight, when it actually started, lasted considerably less time than the buildup to it.

Elliana moved first, closing the distance to the nearest bandit with the same fluid, wild-turned-structured speed that three months under Grayer's instruction had built into her, sword catching the man's clumsy overhead swing and turning it aside before he'd even fully committed to the motion. She didn't kill him — a sharp strike to the wrist sent his blade spinning off into the brush, followed immediately by a boot to the chest that put him flat on his back in the dirt, gasping.

Behind her, Sparky surged forward with a low, rolling growl, electricity crackling visibly along his hide, and two of the bandits who had been circling toward Lilia's flank abruptly reconsidered their life choices and threw themselves sideways out of his path.

Zeus and Hera dropped from the sky in a screaming dive, talons and beaks flashing, and the resulting chaos of shouting, scrambling men gave both remaining sisters all the opening they needed.

"They have a DRUID!" someone shrieked, scrambling backward as Sparky snapped his jaws shut inches from his leg. "They've got a beast-tamer with them, get the druid, TAKE OUT THE DRUID FIRST—"

It would have been, Elliana reflected later, a genuinely reasonable tactical call, if the "druid" in question had actually been some robed hedge-mage hanging back at a safe distance directing her monsters from cover.

Instead, the men charging toward her found themselves facing a ten-year-old girl who met their rush head-on, ducked under the first wild swing, and put her shoulder into the man's gut hard enough to fold him in half.

"Shit," one of them managed, backpedaling fast, "these brats are DANGEROUS—"

Lilia, meanwhile, had abandoned any pretense of defensive positioning entirely, throwing herself into the thick of it with a flurry of strikes that moved faster than half the men could track, her blade a blur of silver in the morning light. One bandit's sword shattered clean against hers on the first parry. Another found himself disarmed and sitting in the dirt before he'd even registered being hit.

Rosaria, for her part, hung back just far enough to work — small, precise bursts of frost catching men's boots and freezing them fast to the ground, a sharp, cracking gust of wind knocking two more clean off their feet before they could close the distance. She was breathing hard by the end of it, more from the concentration than any real exertion, but the results spoke for themselves: a dozen grown, armed men, and not one of them left standing after less than two minutes.

The whole engagement was, in the end, almost embarrassingly one-sided.

"Well," Rosaria said, surveying the scattered, groaning bandits with a critical eye, already dusting off her sleeves, "that was considerably faster than anticipated. I suggest we conserve our remaining strength and continue forward. We've lost enough time as it is."

Elliana, sheathing her sword with the satisfied air of a woman who had thoroughly enjoyed her morning, shook her head.

"Nah," she said. "Bad idea."

Both her sisters turned to look at her.

"What do you mean, bad idea?" Lilia asked, still catching her breath, sword lowered but not yet sheathed. "We won. They're down. Let's just go."

"If we leave them alive and conscious in a few hours," Elliana said, tapping her chin thoughtfully, "they regroup, they lick their wounds, and either they come after us again once we've moved further from any actual reinforcements, or — more likely — they go find some OTHER group of travelers who don't happen to have a crocodile, two thunderbirds, and a heroine's blessing on hand, and do to them exactly what they were about to do to us." She grinned, sharp and entirely unrepentant. "I'm not a fan of leaving loose ends. Especially loose ends that specialize in selling children."

Rosaria and Lilia exchanged a glance.

"...That's actually a fair point," Rosaria admitted, somewhat reluctantly.

"So what did you have in mind?" Lilia asked, with the particular, weary caution of a girl who had learned, over a decade of sisterhood, that this question rarely had a comfortable answer.

Elliana's grin widened into something considerably more dangerous.

"Oh," she said, "you'll see."

• • •

Fifteen minutes later, Elliana stood back from her work, wiping dirt from her hands with an expression of deep, professional satisfaction.

Twelve heads poked up out of the packed earth along the roadside, arranged in a neat, orderly row, each man buried up to the neck, entirely and thoroughly stripped of every stitch of clothing, weaponry, and coin they'd carried into the ambush.

"That's—" Lilia stood a short distance away, staring at the row of exposed, humiliated heads with an expression caught somewhere between horror and reluctant awe, "—that's so EVIL."

"They're bandits, sis," Elliana said, entirely unbothered, dusting off her palms. "It's better to give them what they actually deserve. And frankly? It's a lot more merciful than just killing them outright, which — let's be honest — most kingdoms would've been well within their rights to do, given what they were planning to do to the three of us."

"But looting them until they're stripped completely NAKED?!"

Rosaria, standing a short distance off with her arms wrapped tightly around herself, shuddered — a full, whole-body shudder, the kind reserved for genuinely traumatic experiences.

"Ugh. Don't even make me remember it," she said, voice tight. "I had to pull off their UNDERWEAR. Personally. With my own two hands. That was, without exaggeration, the single worst thing my eyes have had to witness in this entire ten-year-old life, and I have watched Elliana eat an entire boar leg with her bare hands."

"It's called karma," Elliana said serenely, stepping back to admire the finished lineup with visible pride. "We had to teach them a lesson. And honestly? This is a lesson that's going to stick a lot longer than a bruise. Some farmer's going to find them by tonight, word's going to spread, and every bandit crew within fifty miles is going to think twice before setting up shop on this particular stretch of road again."

"There were," Rosaria said, still visibly recovering, "considerably less traumatic ways to accomplish that exact same deterrent effect."

"Were there, though?"

"...I would like to believe so, yes."

"Well, we'll never know now, will we," Elliana said cheerfully, already climbing back up onto Sparky's back and reaching down to help haul the remaining supplies back into place. "Come on. Daylight's wasting, and we've still got a long way to go before we make camp tonight."

Lilia cast one final, deeply uncomfortable glance back at the row of buried, humiliated bandits — several of whom had, by this point, regained enough consciousness to begin a low, mortified moaning that carried faintly on the wind — and shook her head slowly.

"I'm not going to be able to unsee that," she muttered, hauling herself up behind Rosaria.

"You'll live," Elliana said. "Probably. Unlike them, if a wolf finds them before a farmer does."

"ELLIANA."

"I'm KIDDING. Mostly." She clicked her tongue, urging Sparky forward, and the three of them set off once more down the road, the muffled, indignant groaning of a dozen buried, thoroughly humiliated bandits fading slowly into the morning behind them. "Anyway. Safe route, huh, Rosaria? Really working out great so far."

"I am not having this conversation with you."

"Just saying. Maybe the forest wouldn't have been so bad after all."

"I will freeze your tongue to the roof of your mouth."

"Noted. Retracting the comment."

Rosaria huffed, adjusted her glasses, and returned her attention firmly to the road ahead, while behind her, Elliana's laughter rang out bright and unrepentant across the empty countryside, and somewhere overhead, Zeus and Hera wheeled back into formation, scanning the road ahead for whatever fresh trouble the day still had waiting for them.

It did not, as it turned out, take very long to find some.

• • •

The morning had actually started well enough, if Rosaria was being scrupulously fair about it in her travel log — cool, clear weather, an easy pace along a well-worn stretch of the trade road, and, for a rare, blessed hour or two, no immediate sign of anything trying to eat, rob, or otherwise inconvenience them. Lilia had spent most of that hour practicing sword forms from atop Sparky's back, much to Rosaria's visible dismay every time she nearly overbalanced.

"You are going to fall off," Rosaria had said, not for the first time.

"I am NOT going to fall off. I have excellent balance."

"You have excellent balance on solid ground. You are currently on a moving crocodile."

"Sparky is very stable."

"Sparky," Elliana had put in cheerfully, "once got startled by a particularly aggressive goose and threw me clean into a hedge. He is not, historically, 'very stable.'"

"That was YEARS ago."

"He held a grudge against that goose for MONTHS, Lilia. I don't think geese are the only unpredictable variable here."

It was, in retrospect, exactly the sort of lighthearted, meandering argument that made the ambush that followed feel so abruptly, jarringly out of place — one moment bickering about crocodile temperament, the next surrounded on both sides by armed men who had clearly been watching the road for some time, waiting for exactly the kind of unsuspecting, poorly defended travelers that three young girls appeared, at a glance, to be.

It was, Rosaria reflected afterward, a genuinely useful lesson in the gap between looking vulnerable and actually being vulnerable — one that the bandits themselves had learned rather more painfully than she had.

In the aftermath, once the last of the bandits had been thoroughly, humiliatingly dealt with and the three of them were once again moving down the road, Lilia found herself unable to entirely let the matter drop.

"I still think there was a middle ground," she said, glancing back over her shoulder at the distant clearing, now well behind them, where twelve buried, naked heads continued their muffled, indignant protests to no one in particular. "Between 'let them go and risk them hurting someone else' and 'strip them naked and bury them up to the neck on the side of a public road.'"

"Name it," Elliana said, entirely unbothered.

"I don't KNOW, that's rather the point—"

"See, that's the problem with 'middle ground' as a concept," Elliana said, warming to the subject with obvious relish. "It sounds reasonable in theory. In practice, it usually just means doing something halfway, which means it doesn't actually solve anything. You either let them go and hope they've learned their lesson — which, statistically, bandits do not — or you make sure the lesson actually sticks. I went with 'sticks.'"

"There is something faintly terrifying about how much thought you've clearly put into bandit rehabilitation philosophy."

"I contain multitudes."

"You keep SAYING that, and I still don't think it means what you seem to think it means."

Rosaria, riding a short distance ahead, allowed herself a small, private smile at the familiar rhythm of her sisters' bickering — a welcome, grounding sort of normalcy, even against the considerably less normal backdrop of buried bandits and open mountain road. She made a brief, careful note in her travel log regarding the encounter — location, approximate bandit numbers, general disposition of the road going forward — and, after a moment's further thought, added a small addendum regarding Elliana's efficiency in neutralizing hostile numbers roughly three times their own, if only for the sake of a complete and accurate record.

It was, she noted wryly to herself, becoming rather a long list.

By the time the sun began sinking low over the western hills, painting the sky in deep, bruised oranges, the day's earlier violence had faded into little more than another entry in that growing list — replaced by the far more immediate business of finding a defensible clearing to make camp, dividing up the evening's rations, and settling in for whatever the following day's travel might bring.

"You know," Lilia said, watching Elliana methodically build up their evening fire with the same practiced efficiency she brought to nearly everything else, "for someone who buried thirty-five men up to their necks a few months back and just did the exact same thing to twelve more today, you really don't seem bothered by any of it."

"Should I be?" Elliana asked, genuinely curious rather than defensive.

Lilia considered this for a long moment. "I don't know, actually," she admitted. "I think that's what bothers ME about it, if I'm honest. Not that you did it. That you don't seem to carry any of it afterward."

Elliana was quiet for a moment, poking thoughtfully at the growing fire.

"I carry plenty," she said finally, more serious than her usual tone. "Just not the stuff you'd expect. I don't lose sleep over bandits who were planning to sell three children into slavery an hour before I stopped them. That's not weight worth carrying. But I remember every single person I've ever actually failed to help in time. That list is a lot shorter, thank the gods, but it's considerably heavier."

Lilia blinked, caught off guard by the sudden, genuine weight behind the answer.

"...I didn't expect that answer," she admitted.

"Yeah, well." Elliana shrugged, the familiar grin sliding back into place. "Don't get used to it. Moments of genuine emotional depth are a limited-time offer around here."

"Duly noted," Rosaria called from where she was laying out her own bedroll nearby, not quite hiding her own small smile.

The fire crackled on into the gathering dark, and somewhere beyond the edge of the firelight, Sparky settled into a low, contented rumble, Zeus and Hera tucking their heads beneath their wings for the night, and the three sisters, bellies full and bandits well behind them, drifted, one by one, into an easy, well-earned sleep.
