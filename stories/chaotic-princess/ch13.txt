Chapter 13: TEA, TREASON, AND TOOTH-SHAPED SOUVENIRS

Saturday afternoons in the Valentine palace had, over the years, developed their own peculiar gravity — a small, sacred pocket of time carved out of an otherwise chaotic week, where three princesses who spent most of their days being pulled in three entirely different directions reconvened, without fail, at the same wrought-iron table beneath the same old willow tree, for tea.

It was, technically, Rosaria's idea originally — she'd proposed it at age six with the clinical justification that "sibling cohesion correlates positively with long-term political stability in multi-heir households," which had made absolutely no sense to anyone at the table except Rosaria herself, who had read it in a book and thought it sounded appropriately official. Lilia had agreed mostly because there were usually biscuits. Elliana had agreed because any excuse to sit somewhere and watch her sisters be ridiculous at each other was, frankly, some of the best entertainment the kingdom had to offer.

Four years later, the tradition had survived boar hunts, magical mishaps, an actual kidnapping, and one memorable afternoon when Sparky had tried to fit his entire head into the teapot. It was, against all odds, the one constant.

This particular Saturday, the weather had cooperated beautifully — warm without being oppressive, a light breeze rolling in off the eastern gardens, carrying the smell of roses and, faintly, the distant clang of the training yard, where several unlucky knights were presumably still recovering from their morning with Sir Baldric.

Lilia arrived last, as she often did, dragging her feet across the garden path with the specific, full-body exhaustion of a girl who had spent the entire morning being systematically demolished by a man twice her size.

She collapsed into her chair with a groan so dramatic it startled a nearby sparrow off its perch.

"I'm dying," she announced, forehead thunking down onto the table hard enough to rattle the teacups. "I have died. This is my ghost. Please inform Mother that I perished honorably in the training yard, cut down by Sir Baldric's eleventh consecutive footwork drill."

"You say this every week," Rosaria said, not looking up from the book balanced primly in her lap. "And yet, remarkably, you continue to survive it."

"That's because ghosts are resilient." Lilia lifted her head just enough to glare. "You'd know that if you ever left your tower long enough to develop actual muscle mass."

"I have muscle mass. It is simply allocated efficiently, toward my fingers, for spellcasting, rather than wasted flailing a sword at Sir Baldric until he takes pity on me."

"He does NOT take pity on me—"

"He absolutely does. I've watched from the window. He slows his swing by at least forty percent whenever you start making that noise."

"WHAT NOISE?"

"The one you're making right now," Rosaria said serenely, finally glancing up over the top of her glasses. "The dying-woodland-creature noise."

Lilia sat bolt upright, cheeks flushing with the particular, sputtering outrage that only a sister could produce in her. "I do NOT sound like a dying woodland creature—"

"You genuinely do," Elliana said cheerfully, not even looking up from where she was busy stacking three sugar cubes into a small, deeply unstable tower on the edge of her saucer. "It's very distinctive. I could pick it out blindfolded, from across the palace, over the sound of an actual battle."

"You're supposed to be on MY side."

"I'm on the side of accurate reporting."

Lilia made a strangled, wounded sound and slumped back down over the table, though this time it looked considerably less dramatic and considerably more like genuine sulking. Rosaria, satisfied that she'd landed her point, returned her attention to her book — though not before allowing herself the faintest, smug little smile, the kind she reserved specifically for moments of decisive victory over her older sister.

Elliana watched all of this with the quiet, contented delight of a woman settling in for her favorite show.

This — this, right here, was one of her simple pleasures in this life. Not the boar hunting, not the crocodile, not even the chaos she caused herself, though that was certainly up there. It was this: watching Lilia and Rosaria bicker with the effortless, casual cruelty that only genuine love could produce, two sisters who would throw themselves into a burning building for each other without a second's hesitation, currently locked in mortal combat over the definition of a wounded-animal noise.

She giggled — a small, delighted sound she didn't bother hiding — and both her sisters' heads swiveled toward her at once, expressions instantly, identically wary.

They had, over the years, developed a very specific radar for that particular giggle. It rarely meant anything good for whoever happened to be nearby.

"What," Rosaria said flatly.

"Nothing," Elliana said, entirely unconvincingly, still grinning.

"You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face you do right before something catches fire, or someone ends up buried, or a bandit camp mysteriously ends up compromised overnight," Lilia said, sitting up straighter, some of her earlier exhaustion visibly evaporating in favor of pure, defensive vigilance.

"I have no idea what either of you are talking about. I am simply enjoying tea, on this beautiful Saturday, with my two favorite sisters, watching you argue about your relative attractiveness like the mature, dignified young ladies you are."

Both of them stared at her for a long, suspicious beat.

Then, deciding — correctly, if belatedly — that engaging further would only make things worse, they turned pointedly back to each other and resumed their argument, apparently on the theory that if they simply ignored Elliana hard enough, whatever scheme she was currently incubating might lose momentum and quietly die on its own.

It would not. It never did. But the effort was, Elliana thought, genuinely touching.

"Anyway," Rosaria said, smoothing her skirts with the air of someone reasserting control over a conversation that had briefly gotten away from her, "as I was saying. You could stand to dress with slightly more decorum, Lilia. You look like a stable boy who wandered in off the road and decided to sit down for tea."

Lilia looked down at herself — plain shirt, simple trousers, boots still faintly caked with training-yard dirt she hadn't bothered changing out of — and then back up at Rosaria with an expression of pure betrayal.

"I came straight from training! What was I supposed to do, fight Sir Baldric in a ball gown?"

"I'm simply saying there's a version of this outfit that doesn't make you look like you're about to muck out the stables."

"At least I don't dress like I'm one bad afternoon away from being mistaken for a LIBRARY."

"Being mistaken for a library," Rosaria said, entirely unbothered, "is not the insult you seem to think it is."

Lilia scowled, cast about visibly for a better angle of attack, and — with the particular ruthlessness of a younger sibling who had run out of patience — found one.

"At least I don't look like a BOY," she said, with the triumphant air of someone playing a winning card. "You're flat as a board, Rosaria. Flatter than a board. A board at LEAST has some texture to it."

The reaction was immediate and, frankly, spectacular.

Rosaria's entire body went rigid. Her teacup, halfway to her lips, froze mid-air. For one long, suspended second, she looked like she'd been struck by a physical blow — which, Elliana supposed, in a manner of speaking, she had.

Then she choked.

Not delicately. Not the composed little cough she usually deployed when flustered. A genuine, undignified, tea-spraying choke, one hand flying up to clutch dramatically at her own chest as though checking to confirm the grievous wound Lilia had just inflicted upon it.

Elliana, watching this unfold, dissolved into silent, shaking, thoroughly unhinged laughter — the kind that produced no actual sound, just her whole small body vibrating with the effort of containing it, tears already gathering at the corners of her eyes.

"You—" Rosaria managed, once she'd recovered enough breath to speak, voice cracking with barely restrained fury, "—you take that back. Immediately."

"Why?" Lilia said, grinning now, sensing blood in the water and leaning fully into it. "Is it not TRUE?"

"It is a temporary developmental circumstance that will absolutely, unequivocally resolve itself with time—"

"That's what you've been saying for THREE YEARS—"

"Puberty operates on its OWN SCHEDULE, Lilia, I cannot personally accelerate my own—"

"Meanwhile," Lilia said, sitting back with the smug, expansive satisfaction of a girl who knew exactly what she was about to say and had been saving it, "SOME of us were just naturally gifted."

Rosaria's eyes narrowed to dangerous slits. "Meaning?"

"Meaning," Lilia said, and then, because subtlety had never once in her life been her strong suit, gestured with both hands, quite unnecessarily, at her own chest, "I'm already a C-cup. At TEN. My tailor has had to let out three dresses this year alone."

Rosaria's face went through several distinct stages of emotion in rapid succession — shock, outrage, something that might have been genuine despair — before settling, finally, into pure, incandescent fury.

"You are FAT," she snapped, jabbing one finger across the table, "like a PIG. That's not a blessing, that's just excess. You could feed a small village with the sheer mass you're currently carrying around."

"I am ATHLETIC," Lilia shot back, scandalized. "There is a DIFFERENCE between muscle and — and — whatever THAT was supposed to be—"

"Muscle doesn't concentrate itself specifically in—"

"IT'S GENETICS, ROSARIA, I CAN'T HELP BEING BLESSED—"

Elliana, at this point, had entirely abandoned any pretense of composure. She'd slid partway out of her chair, one hand pressed over her mouth, shoulders shaking with the effort of laughing as silently as physically possible, because the moment either of her sisters clocked how thoroughly she was enjoying this, they'd turn their combined fury on her instead, and honestly? This was too good a show to cut short.

Oh, this is beautiful, she thought, wiping an actual tear from the corner of her eye. Truly, deeply beautiful. I should be selling tickets.

"Both of you," she finally managed, once she'd wrangled her laughter down to something resembling manageable, "are correct, and also both being unbelievably petty, and I would like it on the record that I am simply an innocent bystander in this conversation."

"You started it by GIGGLING," Rosaria said, rounding on her.

"I did not START anything. I merely observed. Observation is a passive activity. I cannot be held responsible for the natural consequences of two sisters left unsupervised near a tea table."

"That's not how CAUSALITY works—"

"Actually," Elliana said, holding up one finger with the exaggerated gravity of a scholar about to make a very important point, "speaking of things happening — I have gossip. Real gossip. Concerning Lysander."

The effect was instantaneous. Both Rosaria and Lilia's argument evaporated on the spot, mid-sentence, as they turned to face her with the sharp, sudden focus of hunting dogs catching a scent.

"What kind of gossip," Rosaria said, all traces of her wounded dignity momentarily forgotten.

"What happened," Lilia demanded, already leaning halfway across the table, "is he okay, did something happen, did Celestine—"

"Nothing BAD happened," Elliana said, drawing the moment out with visible relish, setting her teacup down with deliberate, unhurried precision. "Quite the opposite, actually. Things have gotten... considerably better between the two of them."

"Define 'considerably better,'" Rosaria said, narrowing her eyes, though she'd already set her book aside entirely, which was, for Rosaria, the equivalent of anyone else dropping to their knees in rapt attention.

"Well," Elliana said, "for starters, they've started sharing a horse."

Both her sisters blinked.

"...That's it?" Lilia said, sounding faintly disappointed. "They shared a horse? That's not gossip, that's just — logistics."

"Ah," Elliana said, holding up a finger, "but WHO was riding WHERE is the important part."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning Celestine," Elliana said, drawing the words out with obvious, delicious relish, "was riding BEHIND him. Arms around his waist. The whole scene."

Lilia gasped — a real, genuine gasp, both hands flying up to cover her mouth. Rosaria's expression remained carefully, deliberately neutral, but Elliana caught the tiny, telltale flicker at the corner of her eye, the almost imperceptible way her fingers had curled slightly tighter around her teacup.

"That doesn't NECESSARILY mean anything," Rosaria said, in a tone that suggested she desperately wanted it to mean something. "Sharing a horse is a practical transportation decision. It could simply be—"

"They were holding hands, too," Elliana added, entirely too casually, examining her own fingernails.

The dam broke instantly.

"THEY WERE WHAT—" Lilia's voice climbed an entire octave.

"Under the table, during dinner, three nights ago," Elliana confirmed, grinning now, fully committing to the performance. "I happened to be sitting close enough to notice. Neither of them said a WORD about it, obviously, because they're both hopeless and would rather die than acknowledge their own feelings out loud, but the hands were absolutely, one hundred percent, doing the holding."

"I KNEW it," Lilia breathed, practically vibrating in her seat. "I knew something had changed, he's been SMILING more, I thought I was imagining it—"

"There's more," Elliana said.

Rosaria, despite her very best efforts to maintain a cool, scholarly detachment from the whole affair, had gone very still, teacup halfway raised, clearly no longer drinking from it so much as using it as a prop to hide behind.

"MORE," Lilia repeated, practically climbing out of her chair. "What MORE?"

"Celestine," Elliana said, lowering her voice slightly, leaning forward with the conspiratorial air of someone delivering genuinely classified information, "leaned her head on his shoulder. During the ride. I saw it myself, from the east tower window."

Lilia made a sound that wasn't entirely human — somewhere between a squeal and a sob, both hands clapped over her cheeks, which had gone noticeably pink.

Rosaria, meanwhile, said nothing at all, her expression schooled into perfect, icy composure — except for the small paper fan that had somehow materialized in her free hand, which she was now employing, with great and entirely unnecessary vigor, to cool a face that did not, she would insist, need cooling in the slightest.

Heh, Elliana thought, watching both of them with immense, private satisfaction. Even just talking about this makes them blush this hard. Amateurs. The two of you have SO much to learn about the art of chaos.

"Anyway," Elliana continued, warming further to her subject now that she had her audience fully hooked, "there was also the incident at the pond, two days ago."

"The POND," Lilia said, in the strangled voice of a girl bracing herself for something she both desperately wanted and was not sure she could survive.

"They went out there in the afternoon. Apparently just to enjoy the weather, splash around a bit, that sort of thing. Very innocent. Very wholesome. Right up until—" Elliana paused, drawing the silence out with genuine theatrical cruelty, watching both her sisters lean further and further across the table, "—right until Celestine slipped, and went fully under."

"AND?" Lilia demanded.

"And," Elliana said, "when she came back up, her dress was — let's just say considerably more transparent than it had been going in."

Rosaria's fan snapped shut so fast it nearly took off a finger. Her expression remained rigidly, determinedly composed — chin up, posture perfect, the very picture of scholarly indifference — utterly ruined by the deep, blooming red climbing up from her collar all the way to the tips of her ears.

Lilia had abandoned all pretense entirely, sitting frozen, both hands pressed over her mouth, eyes enormous, breathing in short, shallow little pants like she'd just run a full lap of the training yard.

"Well," Elliana said, folding her hands primly in her lap and offering both of them her most serene, entirely unbothered smile. "That's that."

There was a beat of stunned silence.

"THAT'S THAT?" Lilia practically shrieked, lunging halfway across the table. "You can't just — you can't just STOP there, what happened NEXT, did Lysander SEE, did he — Elliana, I need MORE—"

"I require additional details," Rosaria said, in a voice that had climbed at least an octave higher than her usual measured register, fan already snapping back open, "for — for scholarly purposes. Documentation. This is important sociological data regarding courtship rituals among the nobility."

"Mm," Elliana said, considering this request with exaggerated gravity, tapping one finger against her chin. "As the eldest of the three of us, I really think it's my duty to maintain a certain level of prim, proper, dignified restraint befitting a royal princess. I couldn't possibly indulge in something as unbecoming as gossip."

Both her sisters stared at her.

The silence stretched.

Somewhere in the garden, a bird chirped, entirely unaware of the sheer scale of the lie that had just been told.

"You," Rosaria said, very slowly, very flatly, "are the single least dignified person in this entire kingdom."

"You once buried thirty-five bandits up to their necks and wrote 'LOSER' on all of their foreheads," Lilia added.

"Allegedly."

"There is nothing 'alleged' about it, the entire capital is still talking about it—"

"I have no idea what you're referring to." Elliana rose smoothly from her chair, brushing invisible crumbs from her skirts with the unbothered elegance of a woman who had absolutely nothing to hide and several things to hide. "Anyway. Speaking of things I definitely did not do — I got something cool yesterday. I want to show you both."

The subject change was so abrupt, so shameless, that both her sisters simply stared at her for a long moment, visibly recalibrating.

"That's not — you can't just change the subject after THAT," Lilia said. "I demand pond details."

"You'll get no more pond details today. But you will get to see something considerably more interesting than pond details, so really, I'm doing you both a favor."

Rosaria, despite her every instinct screaming at her to demand the rest of the Lysander-and-Celestine saga first, found herself — against her own will — mildly curious. It was, she reflected sourly, a design flaw specific to conversations with Elliana. She always left you wanting to know more, about everything, all at once, whether you'd asked for it or not.

"Fine," she said, standing and smoothing her skirts with rather more force than strictly necessary. "But this had better be worth interrupting an important conversation."

"Oh," Elliana said, already turning to lead the way toward the palace, grin widening into something distinctly more dangerous than her earlier, performative one, "trust me. It is."

• • •

Elliana's bedchamber, when they arrived, looked more or less as it always did — considerably less like a princess's room and considerably more like the private quarters of a very small, very successful mercenary. A rack along one wall held an assortment of weapons in various states of repair, mostly wooden practice blades alongside the one real steel sword Grayer had approved for her use. A second corner had been given over almost entirely to a pile of furs and feathers — souvenirs, Elliana called them, though "trophies" would have been more accurate — collected from a decade of hunts. Two enormous, half-dozing Thunderbirds occupied a specially reinforced perch near the window, cracking one lazy eye open each as the three princesses entered.

And propped up against the far wall, wrapped loosely in cloth but clearly, unmistakably enormous even through the covering, was the fang.

"There it is," Elliana announced, crossing the room and yanking the cloth away with a flourish entirely too dramatic for the moment, though given the reaction it produced, entirely justified.

Both her sisters froze in the doorway.

"That," Rosaria said, very slowly, adjusting her glasses as though the lenses themselves might be responsible for the impossible scale of what she was looking at, "is not a normal tooth."

"It is EXTREMELY not a normal tooth," Lilia agreed, taking several cautious steps closer, eyes wide, mouth working through several attempts at a sentence before actually producing one. "That's — how is that — what CREATURE — Elliana, that's bigger than SPARKY—"

"Isn't it gorgeous," Elliana said, running one hand fondly along the fang's curved length the way another child might have stroked a beloved pet.

"How," Rosaria said, "did you acquire this."

"Funny story."

"I would very much like to hear it."

"Would you, though?" Elliana grinned — that same sharp, delighted, entirely unhelpful grin she'd worn her entire life — and pressed one finger to her lips. "You'll never know~"

"I want to strangle you so badly," Rosaria said, with real, heartfelt sincerity, "that I am actively fantasizing about the exact spell I would need to research to make it possible."

"Love you too, sis."

Rosaria made a strangled noise of pure frustration and pinched the bridge of her nose beneath her glasses, in a gesture so identical to their mother's that Elliana had to bite back another wave of laughter.

"Fine," Elliana relented, taking pity on her — mostly because the story really was too good not to tell someone. "I went out to the Forbidden Forest a few nights ago. Found a bandit camp — real one, actual bounty poster and everything, thirty-some hostiles. Cleared it out, no casualties, found this in their loot pile. Presumably stolen off a caravan somewhere, because I have no idea where a bunch of two-bit bandits would've gotten their hands on a dragon's tooth otherwise."

There was a long, ringing silence.

"You went ALONE," Lilia said, voice climbing, "into the FORBIDDEN FOREST, to fight THIRTY BANDITS, and you didn't tell ANYONE—"

"Isn't it dangerous?" Rosaria added, though notably, her tone carried more resignation than genuine alarm, the specific weariness of someone who had asked this exact question about this exact sister more times than she could count and had never once received a satisfying answer.

"Sister," Rosaria said, before Elliana could even respond, turning instead to Lilia with the flat, world-weary patience of an older soul trapped in a ten-year-old's body, "it's Elliana. She can handle herself."

Lilia opened her mouth to argue, considered the sheer, overwhelming weight of a decade's worth of evidence — the crocodile, the boars, the kidnappers, the six-seven incident, the golden crib, the fact that their sister had apparently strolled into an active bandit stronghold and walked out with a dragon's tooth as a SOUVENIR — and, finding herself entirely unable to construct a counterargument, simply sighed, long and defeated.

"That's — yeah. That's fair," she admitted. "Why do you even want it, though?"

"I'm going to have it forged into a weapon," Elliana said, patting the fang fondly. "Spear, probably. Something dramatic. Something suitably me."

Both her sisters considered this for a moment, and — much to their own visible reluctance — found that it actually made a fair amount of sense, in the specific, warped internal logic that governed most of Elliana's decisions.

"Okay," Rosaria said slowly, studying the fang with a more clinical eye now, tilting her head, adjusting her glasses. "Setting aside the reckless disregard for your own safety for a moment — because clearly that ship has long since sailed — I don't think this is an ordinary tooth. Even setting aside the scale of it. The way it's catching the light. The density." She reached out, not quite touching it, fingers hovering an inch from the surface. "This reads as something considerably rarer than a standard monster fang."

"Oh wow," Lilia said, entirely flat, "what a surprise, right? A gigantic mystery tooth that fell into Elliana's lap during an unauthorized solo raid on a bandit stronghold turns out to be special. Shocking. Groundbreaking."

Rosaria shot her a sharp look. "You know what I mean."

"I do. I'm simply pointing out that at this point I'd be more surprised if it turned out to be mundane."

"Regardless," Elliana said, cutting back in before the two of them could spiral into round two of their earlier bickering, "I'm going to have it appraised properly. I already know exactly who to go to."

"Who," Rosaria asked, immediately suspicious, "because I feel obligated to point out that discretion is not exactly a strong suit among most of the palace staff, and Mother has made VERY sure that nothing stays a secret for long around here."

"Since when was the survival guide updated again?" Elliana asked, blinking.

"Last week," both her sisters answered, in perfect, weary unison.

"Oh." Elliana considered this information for a moment, filed it away, and then waved it off with the easy, unbothered confidence of a woman who had already thought several steps ahead of the problem. "Nah. Not a problem." Her grin widened, sharp and knowing. "I know exactly who won't say a word."

Rosaria and Lilia exchanged a glance — the specific, resigned glance of two people who both knew, with total certainty, that whatever came next was going to be considerably more trouble than either of them had originally signed up for that afternoon, and that neither of them was going to be able to talk their way out of it.

"I don't like that face," Lilia said.

"You'll like it less in about fifteen minutes," Elliana said cheerfully, already wrapping the fang back up and hoisting it, with visible effort but considerable determination, over one shoulder. "Come on. Both of you. Let's go."

"Go WHERE—"

"You'll see!"

And with that, Princess Elliana Evangeline Aurora Valentine — Boar Slayer, chaos gremlin, self-appointed architect of her siblings' romantic destinies, and current owner of one deeply illegal dragon's tooth — marched out of her own bedchamber, dragon fang balanced over one shoulder, two deeply reluctant sisters trailing in her wake, already composing in her head exactly how she was going to explain all of this to Sera.

Not that she planned on explaining much.

She never really did.
