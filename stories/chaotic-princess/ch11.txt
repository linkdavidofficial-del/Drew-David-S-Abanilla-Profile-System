Chapter 11: A WIND SPELL, A WANTED POSTER, AND A VERY BAD IDEA

Three months is a long time, when you fill every single day of it with the same relentless, grinding repetition.

It started before dawn, every morning, in the empty training yard: Grayer's low, rumbling voice calling out corrections, Elliana's wooden practice sword—upgraded, eventually, to a blunted steel one—cutting the same forms over and over until her arms shook and her palms blistered and then, slowly, callused. Footwork drilled until it stopped feeling like footwork and started feeling like breathing. Strikes broken down into their smallest component parts and rebuilt, piece by piece, into something that still carried all of her old wild unpredictability but now had actual structure underneath it, like a house finally getting proper foundations poured under a building that had, until now, simply been willed into standing upright through sheer stubbornness.

"Again," Grayer would say, and she'd go again.

"Hips first, then shoulders, then the blade follows—not the other way around," he'd say, and she'd adjust, and get it wrong, and adjust again, until one morning, roughly six weeks in, she got it right on the first try and he actually stopped mid-sentence to stare at her.

"There it is," he'd muttered, more to himself than to her. "Knew it was in there somewhere."

By the two-month mark, she was fast enough, precise enough, that he started bringing in the rank-five knights again—not as easy warm-up fodder this time, but as genuine sparring partners, and even then, more often than not, she came out ahead.

"You're not ready for aura training yet," Grayer told her, toward the end of the second month, wiping sweat from his brow after a particularly grueling session. "Body's not mature enough. Rush that process and you'll burn out channels before they're ready to hold real power—seen it happen to eager young idiots more times than I care to count, and half of them never fully recovered use of their sword arm afterward. But the foundation you're building right now? This is exactly what lets you skip years of catch-up once your body actually is ready. You're doing the work most students don't bother with because it isn't flashy."

"I contain multitudes," Elliana said, breathing hard, grinning despite the ache in every muscle.

"You contain something, at least." Grayer clapped her on the shoulder, hard enough to stagger her slightly. "Same time tomorrow, Boar Slayer."

By the third month, he was, by his own admission, "highly pleased"—a phrase he used exactly once, gruffly, refusing to elaborate further, but which Elliana treasured considerably more than she let on.

* * *

After every morning session with Grayer, sore and sweaty and thoroughly pleased with herself, Elliana made the trek across the palace grounds to the far tower—Sera's tower, now, thick with the smell of old parchment and fresh herbs and, faintly, ozone, from whatever the resident mage had been experimenting with the day before.

The lessons here were a different kind of exhausting. Less physical, more precise—the careful, exacting work of shaping mana into structures that held their form under pressure instead of collapsing the moment concentration wavered.

"Rank one and two spells first," Sera had said, on the very first day, staff tapping thoughtfully against the stone floor while the Armored Anaconda draped across her shoulders watched with slow, half-lidded interest. "No skipping ahead, no matter how bored you get. Foundation matters more in magic than almost anywhere else. A swordsman with sloppy footwork just fights worse. A mage with sloppy mana control occasionally explodes."

"Noted," Elliana had said. "Foundation. Got it. Explosions bad."

"Explosions catastrophic, generally, yes."

The early lessons were simple enough—cantrips, minor illusions, the basic mana-shaping exercises that most children her age had already mastered years earlier if they'd shown any magical aptitude at all. Elliana burned through them faster than Sera had clearly expected, though the older mage had, by this point, stopped being surprised by much of anything her new student did.

It was the taming spell, though, that Sera seemed most eager to actually teach.

"You already know the theory, more or less," Sera said, one afternoon, gesturing for Elliana to take a seat across from her at the tower's cluttered central worktable, scrolls and half-finished potion ingredients pushed carelessly aside to make room. "A taming spell succeeds when a unique mark manifests on the beast—but the mark isn't something you consciously design. It's a reflection of the bond itself. Where it appears, what shape it takes, how it settles—all of that comes from the nature of the connection between tamer and beast, not from any specific incantation choice on your part."

"So it's less 'casting a spell' and more 'formalizing something that already happened,'" Elliana said.

"Precisely. Which is why—" Sera reached out, gently tilting Elliana's wrist to get a better look at nothing in particular, mostly out of habit, "—your crocodile already carries one. I mentioned this before, but I confirmed it properly, this time, with a full diagnostic. Small mark, forehead, mostly hidden beneath the scale ridges. A genuine, official taming bond. You did this at three weeks old, Elliana. Most trained tamers don't manage a bond that clean until their second or third attempt, on considerably smaller, considerably less electric creatures."

"I told you. Vibes."

"Vibes," Sera repeated, delighted, shaking her head slowly. "I'm going to write a paper about you someday. Anonymously, probably, because I don't think the Academy would believe the actual details."

The lesson that followed on mana rings went, if anything, even faster than the taming theory had.

"A mana ring," Sera began, staff tapping once against the floor to summon a small, glowing diagram in the air above the table, "forms as a physical structure around the—"

"—the heart, yes, I know, denser rings mean more power, multiple rings stack for more capacity, my sister's a third circle mage and can cast rank four and five spells because of it," Elliana finished, examining her own fingernails with practiced disinterest. "Rosaria explains this over dinner. Repeatedly. In detail. I could probably draw you a diagram from memory at this point."

Sera stared at her, then dissolved into laughter, the glowing diagram flickering and dispersing, forgotten. "You know, most tutors would find it insulting to have their entire lesson pre-empted by a bored ten-year-old. I find it delightful. Efficient, even. Saves us both time." She waved a hand, banishing the last traces of the diagram entirely. "Fine. Skip ahead, then. What DON'T you know?"

Elliana considered this seriously for a moment—a rare, genuine pause in her usual performance of effortless nonchalance.

"How to actually control a taming bond with something that isn't already emotionally attached to me through years of proximity and shared crimes," she admitted. "Sparky and I grew up together. The Thunderbirds decided I was worth following after I fought them to a draw and then fed them half my lunch for a month straight. I don't actually know how to walk up to something wild and cold and form a bond on purpose, on the first try, without a decade of buildup."

Sera's grin turned sharp, pleased, genuinely impressed. "Now THAT," she said, "is an excellent question, and also the actual meat of everything I'm here to teach you. Good. You've been paying attention to your own gaps. That's rarer than talent, in my experience."

"That's exactly what Grayer said."

"Because it's true, dear. Now—let's talk about intent-projection, and why it matters more than raw mana when you're trying to convince something wild that you're worth trusting."

* * *

It was after one such lesson, several weeks later, that Elliana finally succeeded in properly, deliberately taming the Thunderbirds—not the accidental, slow-building bond that had formed between them over months of shared meals and mutual tolerance, but a real, conscious casting, mana shaped with actual intent and structure behind it, the way Sera had spent weeks teaching her to do.

The marks appeared almost instantly—small, lightning-bolt-shaped sigils, one settling just beneath each bird's left wing, glowing faintly gold for a moment before fading into their feathers like they'd always been there.

"There," Elliana said, grinning, scratching Zeus under the beak while Hera preened with visible, smug satisfaction at having been made official. "Now it's proper. Sera's going to be thrilled."

She sat back on her bedchamber floor that evening, surrounded by two enormous, contentedly dozing Thunderbirds, and let herself actually reflect—something she didn't do often, mostly because reflection had a tendency to lead her back toward memories of overtime and fluorescent lighting and a half-eaten convenience store sandwich, and those thoughts were never especially fun to sit with.

But this—training with Grayer, learning real magic with Sera, actually building toward something instead of just careening from one chaotic incident to the next—this, she realized, was good. Genuinely good. Not because it made her more responsible, or more princess-like, or any closer to whatever Duke Remington had once wanted her to become.

It was good because it made her *more* chaotic, not less. More capable. More dangerous, in all the best ways. Every hour spent with Grayer was an hour spent becoming harder to stop. Every lesson with Sera was another tool in a rapidly growing arsenal of things she could theoretically use to cause spectacular, memorable disasters.

Take arson, for instance.

The thought arrived, fully formed, entirely unprompted, and she sat with it for a moment with the calm, considering expression of someone weighing a genuinely interesting hypothetical.

I could learn fire magic properly. Controlled, structured, difficult-to-trace fire magic. The kind of fire magic that starts very specific, very deniable, very inconvenient fires in very specific, very deserving locations.

She shook her head, grinning despite herself, and firmly filed the thought away in the same mental drawer where she kept most of her genuinely terrible ideas—not discarded, never discarded, just... patient. Waiting for the right moment.

Later,* she promised herself. *Definitely later.

With that pleasant thought settled, she pushed herself up off the floor, stepped past her dozing Thunderbirds, and wandered out onto her bedchamber's balcony to test something she'd learned only that afternoon: a simple wind spell, rank one, barely more than a parlor trick.

"Wind Whisper," Sera had called it, during the lesson. "About as gentle as spells come. Meant to nudge things—rustle a curtain, carry a scent, push a piece of paper a few feet across a table. Nothing more. If you're looking to impress anyone with raw power, this isn't it."

Elliana had privately thought, at the time, that "gentle parlor trick" sounded exactly like the kind of spell that could be stretched considerably further than intended, given enough mana behind it and a complete disregard for whether that was a good idea.

She leaned against the balcony railing, gazing out over the darkened rooftops of the capital, market district lights flickering faintly in the distance, and murmured the incantation under her breath, gathering considerably more mana into the shape than any reasonable rank-one casting called for.

"There's no way I'll catch anything interesting," she said aloud, mostly to herself, grinning at the absurdity of even trying.

The spell unspooled from her outstretched hand—a visible ripple in the night air, gathering, gathering, then surging outward across the rooftops toward the distant market square with considerably more force than a gentle breeze had any business carrying.

It caught something.

A moment later, riding the tail end of the returning gust, a single crumpled piece of paper spiraled up through the night air and landed, almost neatly, directly in her outstretched palm.

Elliana blinked down at it.

It was a notice—thick parchment, official Adventurers' Guild seal stamped in wax at the top corner, the kind of poster normally pinned to a board in the guild hall for passing adventurers to claim. She smoothed it out against the balcony railing and read.

BOUNTY: BANDIT ENCAMPMENT — "THE BLACKTHORN GANG"

Rank: A

Last confirmed location: Forbidden Forest, eastern ridge, near the old quarry road.

Approximately 30+ hostiles confirmed. Armed, organized, believed responsible for at least eleven caravan raids in the past two months. Reward: 2,000 gold, plus recovered goods.

Elliana read it twice, slowly, a grin spreading across her face with each passing second.

The Forbidden Forest. Of course it was the Forbidden Forest. Where else would something this convenient turn up.

"An A-rank bounty," she murmured, delighted, "just blew in through my window on a rank-one wind spell I definitely wasn't supposed to be able to catch anything with." She turned the poster over, checking for additional details, already half-formed plans blooming behind her eyes. "Thirty bandits. Loot. Probably some genuinely fun equipment lying around a camp that's been raiding caravans for two months straight."

A small, distant, sensible voice somewhere in the back of her mind—the same voice that had, at twenty-eight, once sat through four-hour meetings without complaint, the same voice that had known, on some level, that dying at a desk over a half-eaten sandwich was probably a sign she should have made different life choices—pointed out, quite reasonably, that an A-rank bounty against thirty armed bandits was not, generally, something a ten-year-old girl was meant to handle alone, regardless of how many boars she'd hunted or how many months of sword training she'd put in.

She ignored it. She'd been ignoring that voice fairly successfully for a decade now.

"Sparky!" she called, already moving, already halfway to the door, poster clutched triumphantly in one hand. "Zeus! Hera! Up, you two, we've got places to be!"

The Thunderbirds startled awake with matching indignant squawks, and Sparky—alerted, somehow, from all the way across the palace grounds, by whatever bond let him sense exactly when his princess was about to do something reckless—was already surfacing from his enclosure's pool by the time she reached the courtyard, electricity crackling with what Elliana chose to interpret as enthusiasm rather than concern.

She vaulted onto his back, Zeus and Hera settling onto her shoulders with matching sharp-eyed alertness, and together the three of them thundered across the courtyard, past startled stablehands, past a pair of very confused gate guards, and out through the palace's main gates before anyone had time to properly register what was happening or, more importantly, to try to stop it.

The two gate guards watched her go, dust settling slowly in her wake.

"Was that the princess?" the first one asked, squinting after the rapidly retreating shape of a girl on a crocodile disappearing down the eastern road.

The second guard shrugged, entirely unbothered, already turning back to his post. "She's probably out hunting boars again."

Oh, how wrong they were.
