Chapter 17: A GRAVE MISUNDERSTANDING

The next few days passed in a rhythm that had, much to Rosaria's private and considerable annoyance, already begun to feel almost normal — wake before dawn, break camp, travel until the light started to fail, make camp again, repeat. Rosaria kept meticulous notes in her travel log each evening, cataloguing distances covered, terrain encountered, and — with growing, resigned frequency — a running tally of exactly how many of Elliana's decisions had directly resulted in unplanned combat.

It was on the fourth day that the tally took its most dramatic jump yet.

"I still think we should have stuck to the marked trail," Rosaria said, for the third time in as many hours, glancing down at her map with a frown that had, by this point, become semi-permanent.

"The marked trail added an extra six hours," Elliana said, not looking back from where she rode at the front of their small procession. "This is faster. Trust me."

"Your track record with 'trust me' has been, thus far, deeply inconsistent."

"Statistically we're still ahead," Elliana said breezily. "Mostly."

It was around the time the trees began to thin unnaturally — the undergrowth giving way to bare, cracked earth, and the air taking on a faint, unpleasant sweetness that none of them could quite place — that Rosaria's frown deepened into something closer to genuine alarm.

"Do you smell that?" she asked, slowing her pace.

"Smell what?" Lilia asked, wrinkling her nose. And then, a half-second later, more urgently, "Oh. OH. I smell it now. What IS that?"

Rosaria's eyes swept over the terrain ahead — low, uneven mounds of disturbed earth scattered across the clearing in irregular rows, some crowned with crude wooden markers, others simply sunken and bare — and her expression shifted from alarm into something considerably grimmer.

"It appears to be a gravesite," she said, voice tight. "A large one. And judging by the smell, not a properly maintained one. I can detect the scent of the dead beneath the soil quite clearly from here."

"Oh no," Lilia said, going pale. "Oh no, we've been disturbing the dead, haven't we? Is that — is that a curse thing? Are we cursed now?"

She did not get an answer, mostly because the ground directly ahead of them chose that exact moment to erupt.

A rotted, grey-fleshed hand punched up through the packed earth first, followed almost immediately by a dozen more, clawing their way free of the soil in a slow, staggering, horribly synchronized wave. Milky, sightless eyes turned toward the three intruders. A low, guttural moan rolled out across the clearing, picked up and echoed by a second grave, and a third, until the entire clearing seemed to be groaning at once.

"There are SO MANY OF THEM!" Lilia shrieked, already scrambling backward, sword coming up on pure reflex.

"Define 'so many,'" Elliana called, swinging Sparky around to face the rising tide of shambling corpses with an expression of bright, entirely inappropriate interest.

"I am NOT counting them, Elliana, there's TOO MANY TO COUNT—"

"Fair enough."

What followed was, by any reasonable standard, a genuinely chaotic scramble — Sparky bulldozing a path directly through the thickest cluster of the dead with the low, satisfied rumble of a crocodile who had clearly decided he approved of this particular activity, electricity arcing off rotted limbs with each snapping bite. Zeus and Hera swept low over the clearing, wings crackling, sending bursts of static down into the shambling ranks that left entire clusters of zombies twitching and sparking on the ground.

Elliana, meanwhile, had abandoned her sword in favor of the axe strapped across her back, spinning it in a wide, gleeful arc that caught three reaching, grasping arms in a single pass, tossing the weapon casually from hand to hand as she carved a path forward.

"This is FUN," she called out, entirely too cheerfully for the circumstances, ducking under a lunging grab and burying her axe squarely between a zombie's shoulders. "I've never actually fought undead before! Ten out of ten, would recommend, genuinely underrated experience—"

"IT IS NOT FUN," Lilia shouted back, backpedaling through the clearing with her sword sweeping in wide, defensive arcs, cleaving through rotted limbs and skulls with a speed that left the surrounding air humming, though her voice cracked with something closer to genuine distress with every swing. "Ellie, they're PEOPLE, they USED to be people, this is — I'm so sorry! I'm so sorry, I really am, please just — please just rest now—"

She struck down another with a clean, precise cut, wincing visibly even as the blow landed true, murmuring a fresh, breathless apology to the crumpling corpse before spinning to meet the next.

Rosaria, positioned at the clearing's edge with both hands raised, had abandoned subtlety entirely in favor of raw, sweeping destruction, glowing sigils spinning rapidly beneath her palms as fireball after fireball arced out across the clearing, each one detonating amid the shambling ranks in a burst of flame and scattering ash.

"There is a REASON," she called out, voice tight with concentration, launching another burst of fire into a fresh knot of rising corpses, "that reputable travelers CONSULT LOCAL BURIAL RECORDS before selecting a shortcut through unfamiliar terrain—"

"Noted for next time!" Elliana called back, cheerfully burying her axe into another skull.

"THERE SHOULD NOT BE A NEXT TIME—"

The fight, though considerably more numerous than the morning's bandit ambush, proved in the end no more difficult to actually win — a full-body horde of shambling, mindless corpses was, it turned out, considerably less dangerous than a dozen armed, coordinated men, particularly when facing down a girl with an axe and a decade's worth of pent-up combat instinct, a heroine who could level three at a time with a single swing, and a mage capable of clearing entire clusters with a well-placed fireball.

Within twenty minutes, the clearing had gone still and silent once more, smoke curling lazily up from a dozen scattered, smoldering patches of earth, the disturbed graves finally, mercifully quiet.

Lilia stood in the center of it all, breathing hard, sword lowered, staring out at the ruined clearing with an expression of profound, lingering discomfort.

"I feel awful," she admitted quietly, once the last of the adrenaline had begun to fade. "They didn't ask to be — whatever that was. Someone did this to them. Some necromancer, or a curse, or something. They deserved to actually rest, not get dragged back up out of the ground to attack random travelers."

"For what it's worth," Rosaria said, joining her at the clearing's edge, voice gentler now that the immediate danger had passed, "you did give them that. A proper end, this time. Whatever brought them back clearly isn't something benevolent. You gave them peace they weren't going to get otherwise."

Lilia managed a small, tired smile. "I suppose that's one way to look at it."

"It's the only sensible way to look at it," Rosaria said firmly.

Elliana, meanwhile, had already moved on entirely from the emotional weight of the encounter and was instead crouched near the edge of the clearing, poking curiously at a half-collapsed grave marker with the tip of her axe.

"You know," she said thoughtfully, "somebody should probably report this. A gravesite this size, reanimating on its own — that's not a natural occurrence. Somebody's meddling with necromancy out here, and that's the kind of thing that tends to get considerably worse if it's left unchecked."

"Agreed," Rosaria said, already producing her travel log and jotting down the clearing's approximate location with brisk efficiency. "I'll make a note of the coordinates. We can report it properly once we're back — assuming, of course, we survive the rest of this trip long enough to make it back at all."

"Don't jinx it," Lilia said, with real feeling.

"I'm being REALISTIC."

"You're being OMINOUS."

Elliana straightened up, dusting ash and grave dirt from her knees, and glanced back over the clearing one final time — rows of disturbed earth finally settling back into stillness, the faint smell of smoke slowly giving way once more to ordinary forest air.

"Alright," she said, swinging back up onto Sparky's back with her usual easy confidence. "Let's get moving. I'd rather not make camp anywhere near a reanimating gravesite tonight, personally."

"For once," Rosaria said, climbing up behind her, "I agree with you completely."

"See? We can be reasonable when it counts."

"This is a shockingly low bar for reasonable."

"I contain multitudes."

The three of them pressed on through the clearing and back into the thinning trees beyond, Zeus and Hera resuming their watch overhead, and behind them, the disturbed gravesite settled slowly back into silence — undisturbed, for now, though none of the sisters were foolish enough to believe that whatever had caused it in the first place had been dealt with for good.

That, Rosaria noted grimly in her travel log that evening, was a problem for another day. Preferably one considerably further removed from unmapped mountain wilderness, with considerably more institutional support than three exhausted ten-year-old princesses and one increasingly threadbare protection totem apiece.

She underlined the coordinates twice, just to be safe, and closed the log with a tired, resigned sigh.

Three more weeks of this, at minimum, she thought.

Gods help them all.

• • •

They made camp that night considerably further from the gravesite than strictly necessary, on a low, exposed hilltop that Rosaria had insisted on for the clear sightlines it offered in every direction — a decision none of them argued with, given the day's events.

Lilia, still visibly unsettled, sat close to the fire with her knees drawn up to her chest, staring into the flames with an expression that suggested her mind was still very much back in that clearing.

"Can I ask you something?" she said, finally, breaking the long, comfortable silence that had settled over the camp.

"Always," Rosaria said, not looking up from where she was carefully cleaning a fresh coat of ash from her staff.

"Do you think—" Lilia hesitated, chewing her lip. "Do you think it's weird that this whole trip, the thing that's bothered me the most so far wasn't the bandits, or nearly getting robbed, or any of that. It was the zombies. Fighting people who used to just be normal people, who didn't ask for any of what happened to them."

Rosaria set her staff down gently, considering the question with the same careful seriousness she gave to any genuine problem worth solving.

"I don't think it's weird," she said. "I think it makes sense, actually. The bandits chose what they were doing. Whatever they became, they became through their own decisions. The people in that gravesite didn't choose anything. Someone else made that choice for them, long after they'd lost any ability to object." She paused. "That's a different kind of wrong. I think it's reasonable that it sits with you differently."

Lilia nodded slowly, some of the tension easing slightly from her shoulders. "That actually helps. Thank you."

"Anytime you need chaos rationalized into something manageable," Elliana said, from where she was sprawled comfortably on her own bedroll a short distance away, "Rosaria's your girl. It's practically her second calling, right after magic."

"It is NOT my 'second calling,' I simply enjoy applying logical structure to—"

"Second calling," Elliana said again, grinning.

Rosaria opened her mouth to argue further, considered the sheer, pointless futility of winning an argument with her sister about anything at this hour, and instead simply huffed and returned her attention to her staff.

Lilia, watching the familiar exchange, felt some of the day's heaviness finally begin to lift, replaced by the easy, grounding comfort of her sisters' company — bickering, chaotic, and entirely, reliably present, regardless of what horrors the road ahead might still have waiting.

She fell asleep not long after, considerably more at ease than she'd expected to be, with the low murmur of Rosaria and Elliana's continued bickering fading gently into the crackle of the campfire and the distant, comforting rumble of Sparky's breathing nearby.

Morning, when it came, brought with it a fresh, considerably more mundane set of problems — chief among them the fact that their food stores, four days into the journey, had begun to run distressingly low.

"We're going to need to resupply soon," Rosaria observed, surveying their dwindling provisions with a critical eye over breakfast. "According to the map, there should be a village within a day's travel from here. Small, but it should have a market, or at least a general store."

"A village," Lilia repeated, brightening considerably at the prospect. "With actual buildings. And maybe an actual bed."

"Don't get your hopes up too high. It's a small settlement, not a proper town."

"At this point I would take a barn with a roof and consider it a luxury suite."

Elliana, already breaking down her portion of the camp with brisk efficiency, grinned at the prospect. "A village means a tavern," she said, with obvious, growing anticipation. "And a tavern means people. And people mean information, gossip, and, if I'm lucky, a decent card game."

Rosaria's expression flattened instantly. "Absolutely not," she said. "We are resupplying, resting, and leaving. We are NOT repeating the poker incident."

"There was no 'poker incident.' There was a poker TRIUMPH."

"There will be no gambling of ANY kind."

"We'll see," Elliana said cheerfully, already swinging up onto Sparky's back, entirely unconvinced by her sister's protest, and — as events would very shortly prove — entirely, predictably correct not to be.

• • •

The walk toward the promised village took the better part of that day, and it was somewhere in the long, quiet stretch of mid-afternoon, with the gravesite well behind them and the trafficking ring still a full day ahead, that Rosaria found herself turning the morning's events over more carefully in her mind.

"Something's been bothering me," she said, breaking a comfortable silence that had settled over their small procession.

"Just the one thing?" Elliana asked. "Bold of you to limit yourself."

"About the gravesite," Rosaria clarified, ignoring the jab. "Reanimation on that scale doesn't happen naturally. It requires sustained necromantic influence — either an active caster maintaining the effect, or some kind of cursed object or location generating it continuously. Either possibility troubles me."

"Because if it's a caster," Lilia said slowly, catching up to the implication, "that means there's someone out here powerful enough to raise an entire gravesite's worth of corpses, and we just... left them out there. Unaddressed."

"Precisely."

"And if it's a cursed location," Elliana added, "that's arguably worse, because it means it'll just keep happening, indefinitely, until someone actually deals with the source."

Rosaria nodded grimly. "I've marked the coordinates as accurately as I'm able. Once we're home, this needs to be reported to the crown — probably to the Adventurers' Guild as well, given the scale involved. It's well beyond what the three of us could reasonably investigate further on our own, particularly mid-journey."

"Add it to the pile," Elliana said, though her usual breeziness had dimmed somewhat at the reminder of just how much trouble seemed to be quietly accumulating in their wake. "Bandits, zombies, and whatever's actually causing the zombies. We're basically single-handedly generating an entire season's worth of Adventurers' Guild bounty postings."

"I would prefer if we generated somewhat fewer of them going forward," Rosaria said. "Purely for my own peace of mind."

"No promises."

"I wasn't expecting any."

The rest of the day's travel passed without further incident, the mountains drawing steadily, encouragingly closer with every mile. Lilia spent a good stretch of the afternoon practicing her footwork alongside Sparky, using the crocodile's steady, plodding pace as an improvised balance drill, while Rosaria alternated between updating her travel log and quizzing herself on minor cantrips from memory, muttering incantations under her breath with her eyes half-closed in concentration.

"You're going to walk into a tree doing that," Elliana warned, watching her sister nearly clip a low branch without so much as noticing.

"I have excellent spatial awareness."

"You just almost got clotheslined by a tree branch."

"...Almost. The operative word being almost."

"I'm just saying, maybe save the cantrip practice for when we're not actively walking through unfamiliar terrain."

"I'll take that under advisement," Rosaria said, immediately going right back to muttering under her breath, entirely unrepentant.

Lilia, watching this exchange from atop Sparky's back, laughed quietly to herself, grateful, if nothing else, for the sheer, familiar predictability of her sisters bickering about something so wonderfully mundane after the morning they'd just had.

It was strange, she thought, how quickly the three of them had settled into this new rhythm — danger, argument, recovery, and back again, over and over, in a cycle that should by rights have felt exhausting but instead, more often than not, felt oddly comforting. Whatever waited for them at Ashwick Hollow, whatever waited beyond it on the long road to the mountains, she found herself trusting, more each day, that the three of them together could handle it.

She just hoped, privately, that "handling it" wouldn't involve quite so much mud, blood, or buried, humiliated men going forward. Though given the trip's track record so far, she wasn't holding her breath.

Ashwick Hollow's rooftops grew steadily larger against the horizon as the last light faded, warm, flickering lanterns beginning to wink on one by one along its narrow streets, entirely, blissfully unaware of the storm of chaos about to walk through its gates.

By the time the sun began sinking low enough to signal the approach of evening, the faint, distant shapes of chimney smoke and rooftops finally came into view along the horizon — Ashwick Hollow, closer now than any of them had quite realized, and entirely unaware of just how eventful its next twenty-four hours were about to become.
